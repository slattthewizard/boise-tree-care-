If you want a stump gone without renting or hiring a grinder, you have three real options: dig it out by hand (small stumps only), speed up decay with a chemical stump killer and wait several months, or let it rot on its own over a year or two. Epsom salt does not kill a stump, and burning one in place is rarely a workable option inside Boise or Ada County. None of the no-grinder methods are fast, and that trade-off is the whole story here.

This guide walks through what each method actually does, what it does not do, and which species around the Treasure Valley (cottonwood, aspen, locust, Siberian elm) will send up new shoots no matter which method you pick unless you also deal with the roots. It ends with a straight answer on when paying for grinding beats all of this.

## Does Epsom Salt Kill a Tree Stump? Short Answer: No

Epsom salt is magnesium sulfate. It is a fertilizer, not a desiccant like the rock salt (sodium chloride) people sometimes use to kill weeds in cracks. [University extension horticulturists who have looked at this specific question](https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=765302) report no evidence that Epsom salt speeds up decay or kills root tissue, and no clear mechanism for how it would even reach the roots once the leaves are gone and the tree has stopped moving water and nutrients through the trunk. If a stump seems to die faster after an Epsom salt treatment, something else is doing the work, usually just time.

Skip it. It will not hurt anything, but it will not do what the internet claims either.

## Chemical Stump Killers: What They Actually Do

Most bottled "stump killer" or "stump remover" products are [potassium nitrate, sometimes called saltpeter](https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=400351). Despite the marketing, potassium nitrate does not dissolve wood. It is a nitrogen source that feeds the fungi and bacteria already breaking the stump down, so it can speed up natural decay rather than replace it.

The usual process: drill several holes down into the stump, angled inward so they meet near the center, fill them with the granules per the product label, add water to help them dissolve, and cover the stump to keep the holes from drying out or filling with rain. From there it is a waiting game, typically weeks to a few months before the wood softens enough to break apart with an axe or mattock, not overnight and not without follow-up work.

Two things to know before you buy a bag. First, [a university extension breakdown of stump removal methods](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2025-07-18-what-do-tree-stump-guide-tree-stump-removal) notes chemical treatments speed up decomposition only modestly, and a separate university trial comparing potassium nitrate and nitrogen fertilizers against untreated stumps found no measurable difference in decay after eight weeks, so results vary by wood type, stump size and moisture. Second, once the stump is punky and broken apart, you are usually left digging out the softened chunks and backfilling the hole yourself, which is real labor even after the chemical has done its part.

## Digging Out a Stump by Hand

Hand digging works, but only within limits. A stump under roughly 10 to 12 inches across, especially from a shallow-rooted species, is realistic to remove with a mattock, root saw, and a lot of sweat over a weekend. Anything bigger, or anything with a deep taproot, turns into a much longer project than it looks like from the surface.

A workable approach:

1. Dig a trench around the stump, wide enough to expose the major roots, roughly 18 to 24 inches out from the trunk.
2. Cut through the exposed roots with a root saw, loppers, or a reciprocating saw as you go.
3. Rock the stump back and forth as roots are cut to find the ones still holding it in place.
4. Once the stump is loose, pry it free with a digging bar or pull it with a chain and vehicle, done slowly and from well clear of the stump.
5. Backfill the hole with soil, tamping it in layers so it does not settle into a dip later.

An air spade or a pressure washer can speed up exposing roots by blasting soil away without cutting through the roots themselves, but that is rented equipment, not a shovel-only job anymore. If you hit a taproot straight down that will not budge, that is usually the point where hand digging stops being worth the time. ## Should You Burn a Stump in Boise or Ada County?

We would not plan on it. Open burning inside Boise city limits and in unincorporated Ada County is tightly restricted, tied to air quality index thresholds that shut burning down on bad-air days, and in several areas requires a permit before you strike a match. Recreational fire rules that let you burn firewood in a small contained pit do not cover burning a stump in place, since that is yard debris and wood waste, not a contained recreational fire. Rules differ by exactly where you live in the valley and change with air quality conditions, so confirm current rules with the City of Boise or Ada County directly before you consider it, rather than assuming a stump fire is allowed because a campfire is.

Even where burning is technically permitted, a stump does not burn well on its own. Green or moist wood smolders more than it burns, the surrounding soil can get scorched enough to kill grass and slow future planting, and you are usually looking at hours of tending a fire for a result that a chemical treatment or grinding gets you with less risk and less smoke drifting onto a neighbor's laundry.

## Suckers and Resprouts: The Trees That Fight Back

Cutting a tree down does not always end the story. Cottonwood, aspen, black locust, and Siberian elm, all common around the Treasure Valley, are [prone to sending up new shoots](https://www.butler.k-state.edu/horticulture/agent-articles/trees-and-shrubs/removing-suckers/) either from the stump itself or from suckers rising out of the root system some distance from the original trunk. None of the methods above stop that on their own. Epsom salt does nothing to the roots, a slow chemical stump killer treatment leaves live root tissue intact for months while it works, and hand digging that misses smaller lateral roots leaves plenty behind to resprout.

The one step that reliably stops resprouting is treating the freshly cut stump surface with an herbicide labeled for stump treatment, applied within minutes of the cut while the wood is still taking up moisture. Waiting even a day or two lets the cut surface seal over and cuts the treatment's effectiveness sharply. If suckers have already appeared, pulling them while small works better than mowing or cutting them, which tends to trigger even more growth. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Epsom salt really kill a tree stump?

No. Epsom salt is a fertilizer, not a wood-killing agent, and university extension sources have found no evidence it speeds decay or reaches root tissue once a tree is cut down. Skip it and use a potassium nitrate stump killer or just let the stump rot naturally instead.

### How long does a chemical stump killer take to work?

Plan on several weeks to a few months before the wood is soft enough to break apart, and results vary by stump size, wood type, and moisture. It also leaves you with punky wood to dig out and dispose of afterward, so it saves effort but not necessarily time compared to grinding.

### Can I just burn a stump in my yard in Boise?

Usually not in any straightforward way. Open burning is restricted inside Boise and unincorporated Ada County, tied to air quality conditions, and recreational fire rules for a contained pit do not cover burning yard debris or a stump in place. Confirm current rules with the City of Boise or Ada County before considering it.

### Will my stump send up new shoots if I don't remove the roots?

Possibly, especially with cottonwood, aspen, black locust, or Siberian elm, all of which are known for suckering from the root system. Treating the freshly cut surface with a labeled stump herbicide within minutes of cutting is the most reliable way to stop it; waiting even a day lets the wood seal over and reduces effectiveness.

### Do old tree stumps attract termites?

A decaying stump can draw wood-eating insects looking for moist, softening wood, which is one more reason to deal with a stump near your house rather than leaving it to rot for years. See our guide on [whether dead stumps attract termites](/blog/tree-stumps-attract-termites/) for what to actually watch for.

### Is it worth digging out a stump myself instead of hiring someone?

For a small stump, under about 12 inches across, with shallow roots, hand digging is a realistic weekend project. Anything larger, or with a deep taproot, usually costs more in time and effort than paying for [stump grinding](/stump-grinding-boise/) would have in the first place.
