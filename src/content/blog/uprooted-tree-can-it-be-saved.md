A small, young tree that tips over with its root ball mostly intact can sometimes be pulled upright, staked, and saved. A mature tree with a lifted root plate almost never can, and standing it back up usually just delays a removal that was already coming. The difference comes down to trunk size, how much of the root system tore loose, and how long the tree has been down.

If you are looking at a tree in your yard right now and trying to decide whether to call someone or grab a shovel, the short version is this: small and recently planted, there is a real chance. Large and established, treat it as a safety problem first.

This guide walks through the size threshold that actually matters, how to tell a full uprooting from a partial lift or a simple lean, and the warning signs that mean a tree is close to going all the way over.

## Fully Uprooted vs. Partially Lifted vs. Leaning

These three situations look similar from the street but call for different responses, so it helps to know which one you're actually looking at.

A tree is fully uprooted when the trunk is down or nearly flat and the root plate has torn out of the ground, often leaving a crater and exposed roots behind it. A partially uprooted tree is still upright, or leaning at an angle, but the base has heaved, and you'll usually spot a gap or ridge of raised soil on one side of the trunk. Then there's the tree that's simply leaning after saturated soil: it's tilted, but the ground around the base looks undisturbed, because wet soil let the whole root mass shift slightly without anything actually tearing.

That last case is a different problem than the first two. If yours is a straightforward lean with the ground intact, [our guide to leaning trees](/blog/leaning-tree-dangerous-boise/) covers when that's genuinely dangerous and when it isn't.

## The Size Threshold: When Re-Standing Is Realistic

Whether a tree can be pulled upright and survive comes down mostly to how big it was when it went over and how much root it kept.

[Iowa State University Extension's guidance on partially uprooted evergreens](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/large-evergreen-was-partially-uprooted-storm-can-i-save-it) puts real numbers on this: a small tree, roughly 10 feet or less in height, with 50 percent or more of its root system still intact, can sometimes be pulled back upright, re-set, and staked with a real chance of survival. Beyond that size, the same source is blunt about the odds: a larger tree is unlikely to make it, and even one that can physically be stood back up often dies from the root damage or blows over again in a future storm.

Young or recently planted trees have small, shallow root systems that haven't anchored deeply into the surrounding soil yet. If a storm or wind event knocks one over, the whole root ball often lifts out in one piece with most of its fine roots still attached, which is exactly the kind of case that guidance is describing.

Established or mature trees are a different story. They've spent years or decades building an anchoring root system that spreads well beyond the trunk, so when one of these uproots, the roots that tear are the same large structural roots the tree depends on to take up water and stay upright. A large share of that root mass gets damaged or severed in the fall, not just repositioned. Standing the tree back up doesn't restore what broke. It just leaves the tree trying to support a full canopy on a fraction of a working root system.

## Why a Lifted Root Plate on a Big Tree Usually Means Removal

A root plate is the mass of soil and roots that moves as one unit when a tree tips. On a mature tree, that plate can weigh thousands of pounds and still only represents a small share of the tree's original root spread, since the roots that anchored it furthest out are the ones that snapped first. Most of a tree's roots spread out sideways rather than straight down, which is part of why a heaved plate does so much damage; see [how deep tree roots actually go](/blog/how-deep-do-tree-roots-go/) for more on that structure.

Once that plate has visibly lifted, several things have already happened that re-standing cannot fix:

1. The structural roots on the side opposite the lean have torn or snapped, not just stretched.
2. Soil-to-root contact on the remaining roots has been disturbed across the whole base, which cuts off water uptake even where roots are technically still attached.
3. The trunk has likely flexed or cracked at or below the soil line, which will not show up until the tree is under load again.

Pulling a large tree with a lifted plate back upright puts the full weight of the canopy back onto a root system that can no longer support it reliably in wind. [Penn State Extension's guidance on leaning trees](https://extension.psu.edu/what-to-do-about-leaning-trees) puts it plainly: once root or soil failure has happened, re-anchoring a tree successfully is difficult, even though some trees may go on standing at an angle for a while after the event. For a tree in that condition, [fallen tree removal](/blog/fallen-tree-removal-boise/) is usually the safer and more practical outcome, even when part of the tree still looks green.

Young trees are the exception because the whole exercise of staking a newly planted tree is designed around exactly this problem. ## Warning Signs the Ground Is Telling You

Before a tree fully uproots, the soil around its base usually gives some warning. Walk the base of any tree that worries you and look for:

- A crescent-shaped crack or ridge of heaved soil on the side of the trunk opposite the direction of the lean. This is the ground being pulled up as the root plate rotates.
- A gap or hollow forming between the trunk flare and the surrounding soil, sometimes wide enough to see daylight or slip a hand into.
- Soil that looks freshly disturbed, darker, or looser than the surrounding lawn, especially right after wind or a heavy, saturated rain event.
- The lean itself increasing day to day, rather than staying at a fixed angle.

Any one of these on a mature tree is a sign the root plate is already partially failed, even if the tree has not gone all the way over. That is an active hazard, not a wait-and-see situation, particularly if the tree could reach a house, driveway, fence, or a spot where people or vehicles pass underneath.

## Saturated Soil: Why Storms Are the Common Trigger

Most uprootings in the Treasure Valley happen after a stretch of wet weather, not from wind alone. As [Alabama Cooperative Extension explains](https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/forestry/saturated-soils-and-wind-a-recipe-for-toppling-trees/), dry soil normally grips roots with enough friction to keep a tree anchored against wind, but soil that becomes fully saturated turns closer to a gravy-like consistency, and roots start slipping through it instead of holding fast. Idaho's clay and compacted soils are quick to hold water once they are saturated, and a tree that has stood through years of dry summer wind can go over in a single storm once the ground underneath it turns soft, because the roots slide rather than break.

This is also why a tree can look perfectly fine the morning after a storm and then lean further, or go all the way over, a day or two later as the soil continues to drain and shift. If a storm has passed through, it is worth checking every tree on your property, not just the one that fell; [our windstorm damage checklist](/blog/windstorm-tree-damage-checklist/) walks through what else to look for.

## What to Do If You Find an Uprooted or Leaning Tree Today

If the tree is fully or partially uprooted and could reach a structure, a power line, a walkway, or a spot where people or cars pass, treat it as an active hazard rather than a landscaping decision.

- Keep people, pets, and vehicles clear of the lean direction and the root plate area, both of which can shift further without warning.
- Never approach a downed tree or branch anywhere near a power line. Call Idaho Power for the line and 911 if a line is down, and stay well back until it is confirmed safe.
- Do not attempt to cut, pull, or re-stand a large uprooted tree yourself. The stored tension in a partially uprooted trunk and root plate can release suddenly.
- For a small, recently planted tree with an intact root ball and no structures at risk, it is reasonable to prop it upright temporarily and get it assessed before deciding on staking.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can an uprooted tree be replanted or stood back up?

Sometimes, if the tree is young and small with most of its root ball still intact and undamaged. It needs to be re-set quickly, watered heavily, and staked while new roots establish. Mature trees with a torn or heaved root plate are much less likely to survive being stood back up, even when part of the canopy still looks healthy.

### How do I know if my tree is fully uprooted or just leaning?

Check the ground at the base of the trunk. If you see cracked or heaved soil, a gap opening between the trunk and the soil, or exposed roots, some or all of the root plate has lifted. If the soil surface around the base looks undisturbed and level, the tree is likely just leaning rather than uprooted.

### Is a leaning tree after a storm always dangerous?

Not always. A tree that was already growing at an angle before the storm, with no disturbed soil at the base, may simply be its normal growth habit. A tree that has newly started leaning, especially with cracked or raised soil nearby, should be treated as a possible hazard until checked.

### How long can an uprooted tree survive before it has to be removed?

There is no fixed timeline. A partially uprooted tree can sometimes hold on for days or weeks while it slowly declines, but it can also fail completely in the next wind event with no further warning. Because the risk does not go down on its own, it is worth having it assessed rather than waiting to see what happens.

### Will my homeowners insurance cover an uprooted tree?

That depends on your policy and what the tree damaged or is threatening to damage, which is a question for your insurance provider rather than a tree company. We can help with removal or stabilization regardless of how the cost is being covered.

### What is a root plate?

The root plate is the mass of soil and roots that stays attached to a tree and moves together when it tips over. On a small tree it might be a foot or two across; on a mature tree it can be several feet across and weigh thousands of pounds, though it still represents only part of the tree's full root spread.
