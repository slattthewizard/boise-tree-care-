Trees and solar panels can share a property, but a tree that shades the roof during the middle of the day will cost you production, and the trees that matter most are the ones to the south, southeast and southwest of the panels. Most of the time the answer is not to cut the tree down. It's to prune the branches that actually cast shade, and to remove a tree only when it's big, close and impossible to prune around.

This guide covers how shade affects panels, how to tell which trees are the problem, and how to sort out tree work before, during and after an install.

## How shade affects solar panels

The U.S. Department of Energy's [Homeowner's Guide to Solar](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/systems/homeowners-guide-solar) says that trees creating excessive shade on your roof can make rooftop panels a less ideal choice, and that panels generally perform best on south-facing roofs with a slope between 15 and 40 degrees. It doesn't give a shade cutoff, and we won't invent one. Any single cutoff number you hear is a rule of thumb, not a standard.

What matters is how much of the day, and how much of the year, a shadow falls on the panels. A shadow across a few panels during the first hour of the morning costs little. The same shadow at noon in June costs a lot more. And because panels are wired together, a shaded panel can affect more than itself. Ask your installer how the system you're being quoted handles partial shade, since equipment choices vary.

## Which trees actually matter

Not every tree near a house is a solar problem. In the northern hemisphere, the sun sits in the southern part of the sky, so the trees that shade a roof are the ones to the south of it. The American Solar Energy Society's [Solar Today](https://ases.org/trees-sunlight-and-aolar-panels-finding-the-right-balance-optimizing-generation-from-a-solar-array-by-minimizing-shade-loss/) analysis says trees to the southwest and southeast have the greatest impact on generation. Deciduous trees to the east or west can be fine, because they block harsh summer sun while costing less in winter.

Height and distance matter together. A tall tree far away can throw a shadow that clears the roof. A short one right beside the house may not. In the ASES worked example (a 25-foot-wide array at 38 degrees north latitude, which is a model, not a Treasure Valley measurement), a 50-foot tree caused about 170 hours of shade a year and roughly 2.6% annual loss. That's a useful reminder: one big tree often costs a few percent, not half your output. Your own roof, with your own trees, can come out differently, which is why a proper shade analysis beats guessing.

Winter is its own story. The sun is low in December, so a tree that clears your roof in summer can shade it for hours in winter. ASES points out that low-sun shade has less effect on annual totals, because little energy is produced then anyway. But if your goal is winter production, or you have a south-side tree that's tall enough, it still counts.

## Deciduous vs. evergreen

This is where tree species changes the answer. The Department of Energy's [Guide to Home Landscaping](https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/guide_to_home_landscaping.pdf) notes that deciduous trees block the sun in summer but let sunlight through in winter once they lose their leaves, while dense evergreens give shade all year.

For panels, that means:

- A bare deciduous tree in winter lets a fair amount of light through, but its trunk and branch structure still cast some shadow. ASES says its model assumes a solid shadow, so its estimates are conservative for leafless trees.
- A spruce, pine or other evergreen shades the roof all year, and pruning rarely fixes that, because the needles are always there. If an evergreen is the problem, it's the likeliest candidate for removal or heavy reshaping.
- A deciduous tree that shades the roof only from May to October is shading during the months when panels produce the most. That's the trade: the shade you want on your house in July is the shade you don't want on your panels.

## Pruning vs. removal

We'd start with pruning almost every time. ASES says the goal is generally minimal tree removal, and that trimming branches (or sometimes moving the array) can cut shade loss without losing the tree. Removal is worth considering when:

- The tree is an evergreen or a dense, wide crown directly south of the array, and it's too big to prune without ruining its shape.
- The tree is already declining, hazardous or in the wrong spot for other reasons. That's a case for [removal](/tree-removal-boise/) on its own merits.
- Repeated pruning would be needed every few years just to keep the shadow off, and you'd rather be done with it.

Pruning is the better call when only a few limbs reach into the sun path. [Professional trimming](/tree-trimming-boise/) of those limbs, done by an ISA-certified arborist, usually costs far less than losing a mature tree that was cooling your yard and your house. As a ballpark, small trimming jobs run $150-$400 and medium ones $350-$900, and our [cost calculator](/cost-calculator/) gives a better range for your tree.

## The right way to prune for solar

There are two good tools and one bad one. Our [guide to crown thinning, raising and reduction](/blog/crown-thinning-raising-reduction/) covers the details. In short:

- **Crown thinning** removes selected branches inside the canopy to let light through, which helps with dappled shade but doesn't change the tree's height.
- **Crown reduction** shortens the tree by cutting back to appropriate side branches. This is the one that lowers the shadow's reach.
- **Crown raising** lifts the lower branches. It helps if the shade comes from low limbs, but not if the problem is the top of a tall tree.

What we'd never do is top the tree. Cutting the main leaders flat to get rid of the shade is the tempting shortcut, and it causes weak, fast regrowth that fills the shadow back in within a few years, plus large wounds that decay. See [why topping is bad for Boise trees](/blog/tree-topping-bad-boise/). A tree that has to be kept at a fixed height by constant hacking is a tree in the wrong place.

Plan for regrowth either way. ASES recommends planning for growth by trimming branches or leaving extra space, since trees change over time. A tree that fits the sun path today will not in ten years if you do nothing.

## Branches over the roof and debris on panels

Shade isn't the only issue. Branches that hang over the roof drop leaves, seeds, twigs and sap onto the panels, and a limb that fails in a windstorm can hit them. The Department of Energy's landscaping guide advises planting trees far enough from the house that, at maturity, their branches do not damage the roof. That's good advice for panels too.

Keep overhanging limbs cut back from the roof surface. Panels do get cleaned by rain, but debris that sits or sticks, like sap, tends to stay. Don't climb onto a roof with panels to clear it yourself. Wet leaves and glass-covered panels are a bad combination.

## Planning tree work before you install

The cheapest time to deal with a tree is before the panels go up. Removing or pruning afterward means working around equipment, and falling limbs can damage new panels. So:

1. Get the shade analysis first. Your installer should measure shade through the day and through the seasons. Ask which specific trees are the problem and how much they cost you.
2. Be wary of "take them all down." A quote that assumes you'll remove every tree is worth a second opinion from an arborist.
3. Schedule pruning or removal before the install date, and let the cuts finish before the crew arrives.
4. Check what's allowed. Boise regulates street and right-of-way trees (see [tree removal permits in Boise](/blog/tree-removal-permits-boise/)), and removal rules vary by property, so confirm before you cut anything that isn't clearly yours.

## What to plant near a solar home

If you want shade, think about where it lands over the next 20 years. The DOE's landscaping guide puts tall deciduous trees with high, spreading branches on the south side of a house for summer roof shade, but notes that a slow-growing tree may need up to 10 years to shade a roof. For a solar roof, we'd reverse the thinking: keep tall trees off the south side of the panels, and put them on the west or east, at a distance, or use smaller-growing species. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much do trees reduce solar panel output?

It depends on the tree, the distance and the season. In one American Solar Energy Society worked example, a single 50-foot tree caused about 2.6% annual loss. A shade analysis of your own roof is the only way to know your number.

### Should I remove trees before installing solar panels?

Often not all of them. Start with a shade analysis, then prune the branches that cast shadows. Remove a tree when it's an evergreen or large crown right on the south side and pruning won't solve it. Do the tree work before panels go up so nothing falls on them.

### Which side of the house matters most for trees and solar?

The south side, plus the southeast and southwest. The sun crosses the southern sky, so trees in that direction shade a roof the most. Deciduous trees to the east or west matter less, and trees to the north rarely matter at all.

### Do deciduous trees shade solar panels in winter?

Less than evergreens, since they drop their leaves and let sunlight through. Bare branches still cast some shadow, and the winter sun is low, so a tall deciduous tree to the south can still shade panels for part of the day.

### Is it okay to top a tree to stop shading my panels?

No. Topping leaves large wounds and triggers fast, weak regrowth that restores the shade quickly. Crown reduction by an ISA-certified arborist, which cuts back to side branches, is the proper way to lower a tree.

### Can tree branches or sap damage solar panels?

Branches that fall in storms can hit panels, and overhanging limbs drop leaves, seeds and sap onto them. Keep limbs cut back from the roof, and have a professional do any pruning near the array rather than working on the roof yourself.
