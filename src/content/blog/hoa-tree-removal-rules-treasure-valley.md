HOA tree removal rules across the Treasure Valley almost always require your architectural review committee's approval before a tree comes down, even one that sits entirely inside your own lot line, from Eagle subdivisions to south Meridian. Skip that approval and you risk a fine, a forced replanting order, or a stop-work notice delivered mid-job.

That approval has nothing to do with any city permit. Boise runs its own street-tree permit process for trees in the public right-of-way, which we cover in our guide to [tree removal permits in Boise](/blog/tree-removal-permits-boise/). An HOA's rules sit on top of that and apply to trees the city never touches, including the tree in your own backyard. The rules live in a subdivision's CC&Rs, short for covenants, conditions, and restrictions, a document recorded with the county ([Ada County](https://adacounty.id.gov) or its Canyon County counterpart) when the neighborhood was platted.

## How Treasure Valley HOA Tree Removal Rules Actually Work

Every covenanted subdivision has an architectural review committee, sometimes called an ARC or a design review board, whose job is to approve exterior changes before they happen. Tree removal almost always falls under that authority, right alongside fence height, paint color, and shed placement. The logic is that a mature tree affects how the whole street looks, not just your own yard.

Some HOAs fold tree rules directly into the CC&Rs. Others keep a separate design guidelines document that gets updated more often than the covenants themselves. Either way, the rule usually applies to any tree above a certain size, and it typically does not matter whether the tree was planted by the builder, a prior owner, or you.

**A homeowner in an Eagle subdivision off Floating Feather Road** wanted to take down a large silver maple that had developed significant dieback on one side of the canopy. The HOA's architectural committee asked for photos and a written condition assessment before it would approve removal, since the tree still had green leaves on part of the crown. Once the paperwork was in, the request cleared in just under three weeks, and removal of the large maple came to about $2,750.

## The Approval Process, Step by Step

The exact form changes from one HOA to the next, but the shape of the process is close to universal across the Treasure Valley.

First, pull your CC&Rs and any separate design guidelines and check what they say specifically about trees. Second, submit a written request to the architectural committee, usually through a form, an email address, or a management company's online portal. Third, attach documentation: photos of the tree from a few angles, and ideally a written assessment from an ISA-certified arborist if the tree's condition is anything but obvious.

A tree health assessment typically runs $75 to $250, and many tree services waive that fee if you schedule the removal work with them afterward. Our [tree health assessment](/blog/tree-health-assessment-boise/) service explains exactly what a certified arborist checks during that visit. That written assessment does double duty: it tells you honestly whether the tree needs to come down, and it gives the committee something concrete to approve instead of your word alone.

After you submit, expect a wait of roughly 2 to 4 weeks, depending on how often the committee meets. Some meet monthly, others review requests as they come in. If your request is denied and you disagree, most HOAs have an appeal path to the full board, which is a separate and usually slower step.

## When You Can Skip the Committee: Hazard and Dead Tree Exceptions

Nearly every set of CC&Rs we have seen in the Treasure Valley carves out an exception for genuine hazards. If a tree is dead, split, or actively threatening a structure, most HOAs let you remove it without waiting for a hearing, especially right after a storm.

That exception is not a blank check, though. You still want to document the condition before the crew arrives: photos of the split trunk, the dead canopy, or the limb resting on the roofline. Submit that documentation to the HOA after the fact, even if the rules did not require it before you acted. It protects you if anyone on the committee questions the decision later.

This is also where your homeowner's insurance can come into the picture. Idaho homeowner policies typically cover $500 to $1,000 per tree for storm-related removal, up to $5,000 per storm event. Photos taken before the crew starts work support both the insurance claim and the after-the-fact HOA notification, so it is worth taking five minutes to shoot them from your phone before anyone touches a chainsaw.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does HOA approval replace the need for a city permit?
No. An HOA's architectural review process and a city street-tree permit are two separate systems that can both apply to the same job. A tree on your private lot inside an HOA needs the HOA's sign-off, while a tree in the public right-of-way in front of your house may need a city permit regardless of what your HOA decides.

### What if my HOA takes too long to respond to my request?
Check your CC&Rs for a stated response window, since many include one even if it is rarely enforced. If there is no deadline or the committee has gone quiet, a polite written follow-up referencing your original submission date is usually the fastest way to get movement, and keeping every request in writing protects you either way.

### Can an HOA force me to remove a tree I want to keep?
Generally only if the tree violates a specific, recorded rule, such as a height restriction or a documented safety hazard confirmed by an inspection. Most CC&Rs are written to control what you remove and how you replace it, not to mandate removal of a healthy tree you want to keep.

### Do I need a written arborist assessment for every request?
Not always. Straightforward cases, like an obviously dead tree, often move through committee with photos alone, but anything borderline, such as partial dieback or a lean that is hard to describe in writing, moves faster with a professional assessment attached.

### What if the tree causing the problem is on my neighbor's side of the line?
Your HOA's authority typically only reaches trees within its jurisdiction, and a tree fully on a neighbor's lot is a matter between you, your neighbor, and possibly the HOA if both properties are covered.
