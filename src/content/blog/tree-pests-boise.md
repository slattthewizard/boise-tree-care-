## The Four Categories Behind Most Tree Pests in Boise

Almost every pest call we get in the Treasure Valley falls into one of four buckets: aphids, scale insects, borers, or mites. They look and behave differently enough that a quick visual check usually narrows it down fast.

**Aphids** are soft-bodied, pear-shaped insects, usually green, black, or gray, clustered on new growth and the undersides of leaves. They are the most common cause of sticky residue on cars, patio furniture, and anything parked under a tree.

**Scale insects** look less like bugs and more like small bumps stuck to bark or leaf undersides, brown, gray, or waxy white depending on species. They barely move once settled, which is why people mistake them for a bark texture or a disease rather than an insect.

**Borers** are the larvae of beetles or moths that tunnel under bark and through wood, feeding on the tree's vascular tissue from the inside. You rarely see the insect itself. You see the damage: dying branches, D-shaped or round exit holes, and sometimes sawdust-like frass at the base of the trunk.

**Spider mites** are barely visible without a hand lens, but their damage is not. Stippled, dusty-looking, bronze-tinged leaves in the heat of summer are the classic sign, along with fine webbing on heavily infested branches.

## Aphids: Common, Ugly, Rarely Dangerous

Aphids are the pest we get the most panicked calls about and the one that usually needs the least intervention. A tree covered in aphids looks alarming, sticky leaves, ants running up and down the trunk to farm the honeydew, sometimes a black sooty mold growing on the residue below. But established, healthy trees generally tolerate aphid populations without lasting harm.

**When it's worth treating:** young trees under heavy aphid pressure, or when honeydew is dripping onto a patio, deck, or parked cars enough to be a real nuisance. Insecticidal soap or a strong hose spray targeting the colonies works for light to moderate infestations. Ladybugs and lacewings, which show up naturally once aphid populations build, often bring numbers down on their own within a few weeks.

**When spraying is pointless:** on a large, mature, otherwise healthy shade tree. You are not going to meaningfully reduce aphid numbers across a 40-foot canopy with a backyard sprayer, and the tree was not in danger to begin with. Save the money.

## Scale Insects: The Ones People Miss for Years

Scale is the pest most likely to go undiagnosed because it does not look like an insect at all. Homeowners describe it as "weird bumpy bark" or "some kind of fungus." By the time it is causing visible branch dieback, populations have often been building for a couple of seasons.

Two types matter here. **Soft scale** produces honeydew like aphids do and tends to be less immediately damaging. **Armored scale** does not produce honeydew but can be more destructive to branches and bark over time, since it feeds more directly on plant tissue under a hard protective covering.

**Treatment window matters a lot with scale.** Once mature scale insects have formed their waxy or armored covering, most contact sprays cannot penetrate it. The window that actually works is the "crawler stage," when newly hatched scale are mobile and unprotected, typically for a couple of weeks in late spring depending on species and that year's temperatures. Spraying outside that window is largely wasted effort. If scale is a recurring problem, dormant oil applied in late winter, before bud break, smothers overwintering scale and is often more effective than trying to time the crawler stage exactly right.

## Borers: The Category That Actually Worries Us

Borers are the pest group where we take a call seriously right away, because the damage happens invisibly under the bark and by the time it is obvious, a significant amount of vascular tissue is already compromised.

Common borers in the Treasure Valley include flatheaded borers (which favor stressed or sun-scalded trees), roundheaded borers, and clearwing moth borers that target fruit trees and ornamentals like birch and ash. We are not covering emerald ash borer in detail here since it is its own major topic. If you have an ash tree, [our dedicated guide to emerald ash borer in Idaho](/blog/emerald-ash-borer-idaho/) covers detection and treatment specifics that matter enough to need their own space.

**The pattern with most borers:** they target trees that are already stressed, from drought, recent transplant, sun damage, or mechanical injury (mower and trimmer wounds are a bigger factor than people realize). A vigorous, well-watered, unstressed tree is much less attractive to egg-laying adult beetles and moths in the first place.

**Signs to check for:**

- Small, round or D-shaped holes in the bark where adults have emerged
- Sawdust-like frass caught in bark crevices or piled at the trunk base
- Branch dieback that starts in one section and spreads
- Bark that sounds hollow or looks sunken over an area of trunk
- Increased woodpecker activity, since woodpeckers hunt borer larvae under bark

**What actually helps:** for confirmed borer activity, systemic insecticide treatments applied by a professional, timed to adult emergence, can protect a valuable tree. But treatment only makes sense if the tree still has enough healthy tissue left to save. A tree with borer damage circling more than half the trunk circumference or extensive dieback through the main scaffold branches has usually passed the point where treatment pays off.

## Spider Mites: The Heat-Loving Nuisance

Spider mites thrive in exactly the conditions Boise summers deliver: hot, dry, dusty. They are technically arachnids, not insects, which is part of why standard insecticides do not touch them and sometimes make things worse by killing off the predatory insects that would otherwise keep mite populations in check.

Damage shows up as a stippled, faded, dusty bronze look to foliage, usually starting on the lower, interior parts of the canopy where dust settles and airflow is poor. Fine webbing on the undersides of leaves or between branches confirms it under a hand lens.

**Treatment:** a strong, direct spray of water on the undersides of leaves, repeated every few days, physically knocks mite populations down and is often as effective as a miticide for moderate infestations. Keeping trees adequately watered during heat matters too, since drought-stressed trees are more susceptible to mite damage and slower to recover from it. True miticides exist for severe cases but are rarely necessary on established landscape trees.

## When Spraying Anything Is a Waste of Money

This is worth saying plainly because it saves people from buying products they do not need. Spraying is pointless when:

- The tree is large and mature and the pest pressure is light to moderate, since the tree's own reserves can absorb the damage
- The pest has already moved past the treatable life stage (mature scale, adult borers that have already emerged)
- The real problem is a stressed tree, in which case treating the pest without addressing the underlying stress (usually water, sometimes soil compaction or a recent construction wound) just means the pest problem comes back next year
- General-purpose "tree and shrub" sprays are applied without actually identifying which pest is present, since the wrong product at the wrong time does essentially nothing except cost money and kill beneficial insects

## A Real Example from Garden City

A homeowner in Garden City called us convinced her honeylocust had a fungal disease because of a black coating covering the lower branches and everything parked underneath. It was sooty mold growing on aphid honeydew, not a fungal infection of the tree itself. The tree itself was healthy and mature, well past the size where aphids posed any real threat. We recommended against spraying the tree at all and suggested she wash down the patio furniture and give it a season, since aphid populations on established trees like that one tend to cycle with natural predator activity. By late summer the ladybug population had caught up and the honeydew dripping had mostly stopped. Total cost to her: the price of the assessment, about $95, instead of an unnecessary spray program that could have run several hundred dollars a season.

## FAQs: Tree Pests in Boise

**What is the sticky stuff falling from my tree?** That is honeydew, a sugary waste product from aphids or soft scale insects feeding on the tree's sap. It is not sap itself and it is not usually a sign of serious tree damage on its own.

**Do I need to spray my tree every year for pests?** No. Most healthy, established trees in Boise do not need routine spraying. Reactive treatment based on an actual identified problem is more effective and cheaper than a preventive spray schedule.

**How do I know if it's borers and not just a dead branch?** Look for small round or D-shaped exit holes in the bark, sawdust-like frass in bark crevices, and increased woodpecker activity on that section of the tree. A single dead branch with none of those signs is more likely storm damage, disease, or normal shedding.

**Are spider mites dangerous to my tree?** Rarely fatal on their own for an established tree, but they add stress during Boise's hottest months, which can compound with drought stress and make a tree more vulnerable to other problems.

**Can I treat scale insects myself?** For light infestations, yes, dormant oil in late winter or targeted treatment during the crawler stage in late spring both work well for a homeowner to apply. Heavy infestations on large trees usually call for professional equipment to get coverage into the upper canopy.

**Why did my neighbor's identical tree get infested and mine didn't?** Pest pressure often tracks tree stress and microclimate more than species. A tree that got less water last summer, sits in a warmer spot, or suffered mower damage to the trunk is simply a more attractive target.

**Should I remove a tree that has a pest problem?** Almost never for aphids, scale, or mites alone. Removal becomes a real conversation with severe, advanced borer damage circling much of the trunk, especially combined with significant canopy dieback.
