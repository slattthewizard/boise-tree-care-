Scratch a twig with your thumbnail. If the layer underneath is green and moist, that part of the tree is alive. If it is dry, brown, and papery all the way around, that part is dead. Do this on a few branches in different spots, because a tree can be alive in one section and dead in another.

That single check will not always give you a full answer, especially in April or May when a handful of Boise's common trees are simply slow to leaf out. Six checks together, the scratch test, a bend test, a bud check, the bark, fungus at the base, and the calendar, will tell you whether you are looking at a dead tree, a dormant tree, or a tree in decline.

None of this is a substitute for someone standing under the tree with you. But it will tell you whether to relax, watch it for a season, or start getting removal estimates.

## The Scratch Test: What the Cambium Tells You

Just under the bark is a thin green layer called the cambium. It is the tree's growing tissue, the part that carries water and sugar up and down the trunk. A live cambium is pale green and slightly moist when you nick it. A dead one is brown, tan, or dry and crumbly.

Use a pocketknife or your thumbnail on a few young twigs, roughly pencil-thickness, in different parts of the crown: high, low, one side, the other side. One brown scratch does not mean the tree is dead. Several brown scratches spread across the whole crown, with no green anywhere, usually does. [Colorado State University Extension confirms](https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=659654) the same reading: bright green under the bark means the tissue is alive, tan or brown means that part is dead.

## The Bend Test for Small Branches

Pick a pencil-thick twig and bend it. A living branch flexes and springs back, or if it snaps, the wood inside is pale and slightly moist. A dead branch snaps cleanly and dry, almost like a breadstick, and the wood inside looks gray or brown all the way through.

Test several twigs from different parts of the tree. On a partly dead tree, you will get a mix: some bend, some snap dry. That mix is itself useful information, and we come back to it below.

## Check the Buds Before You Panic

If it is late winter or early spring and the tree has no leaves yet, look at the buds instead of the branch tips. Living buds are firm and plump, sometimes slightly sticky or waxy depending on the species. Dead buds are shriveled, dry, and often fall off if you rub them between your fingers.

Deciduous trees (the kind that drop their leaves every fall) hold next year's buds through the whole winter. If those buds are plump in March, the tree is dormant, not dead, even if it looks bare and gray.

## Bark Falling Off or Peeling in Sheets

Some bark shedding is completely normal for certain species and is not a sign of anything wrong. But bark that is falling off in large sheets, especially if it exposes dry, gray wood underneath with no cambium moisture, is a stronger sign of a dead section. Peeling bark paired with a failed scratch test underneath is a much more reliable combination than either check alone. Our guide to [tree bark peeling off](/blog/tree-bark-peeling-off/) walks through which peeling patterns are normal for which species and which ones are not.

## Fungus or Mushrooms at the Base

Shelf-like fungus (sometimes called conks or bracket fungus) growing out of the trunk or from roots at the base is a sign of internal wood decay, and it usually means the decay has been going on for a while. It does not always mean the whole tree is dead. A tree can carry a fungal infection in its heartwood for years while the outer, living wood keeps growing. [Clemson Extension notes](https://blogs.clemson.edu/fnr/2024/01/08/tree-issues-from-conks-to-cavities-to-decay/) that conks at the base can point to root rot, and that conks anywhere on a tree indicate advanced decay and a real chance of failure. Combined with a hollow-sounding trunk when you knock on it, that is a structural red flag on top of a health one. ## Wait, Is Your Tree Just a Late Leafer?

Boise gets a wave of "is my tree dead" calls every spring, and a fair number of those trees are just running behind. A few species common in the Treasure Valley are known for leafing out later than almost everything else on the block, sometimes not until late May:

- Honeylocust
- Catalpa
- Kentucky coffeetree

[Iowa State University Extension lists these same three species](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/late-leaf-emergence-fruit-and-ornamental-trees-and-shrubs) among landscape trees that regularly leaf out later than others nearby. If your tree is one of these and every other tree on the street already has leaves, that alone is not a red flag. Run the scratch and bend tests before you assume the worst. A late leafer with green cambium and flexible twigs is fine. It is just running on its own clock.

## What "Half of My Tree Is Dead" Actually Means

A tree with one dead side and one healthy side, or a dead top with a leafy lower crown, is not an unusual thing to find. It is called dieback, and it points to a specific stress rather than the tree simply dying of age. Common causes include root damage on one side (construction, trenching, a car backing into the trunk), a split or girdling root feeding one section, storm or ice damage to one part of the crown, or a disease or pest that started in one limb and spread.

A tree with significant dieback is not automatically a removal candidate. Some trees can be pruned back to the live wood and kept for years. Others have lost enough of the crown, or have decay working through the trunk, that removal is the safer and cheaper long-term choice. This is a judgment call worth getting an arborist's eyes on rather than guessing at.

| What you find | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Green, moist cambium on most branches | Alive, possibly dormant or stressed |
| Brown, dry cambium everywhere you check | Dead |
| Mix of green and brown cambium by section | Partial dieback, not a simple yes or no |
| Plump buds, no leaves yet, early spring | Dormant, not dead |
| Honeylocust, catalpa, or Kentucky coffeetree, bare in April | Probably just a late leafer |
| Bark peeling in sheets over dry wood | Likely dead in that section |
| Conks or mushrooms at the trunk base | Internal decay, get it checked |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it take a tree to die completely once it starts declining?

It varies enormously by species, the cause, and the tree's overall health, from a single bad season to several years of slow decline. A tree with a sudden total die-off (all branches brown at once) usually points to a root problem or trunk injury, while a tree that loses one section at a time over multiple years is more often dealing with disease, pests, or long-term stress. There is no single timeline that applies to every case.

### Can a tree be half dead and still be saved?

Sometimes. If the dead section is limited and caused by something that has stopped (old storm damage, a resolved root injury), pruning out the dead wood and supporting the living side can work. If the cause is ongoing, such as active decay in the trunk or a disease still spreading, saving the tree is much less likely. A closer look at the cambium and the cause of the dieback usually points to which situation you're in.

### Does a tree with no leaves in April always mean it's dead?

No. Boise has a normal range of leaf-out timing, and a few common species, including honeylocust, catalpa, and Kentucky coffeetree, are known for leafing out later than most other trees in the same yard. Run the scratch test and check the buds before assuming a bare tree in early spring is dead.

### Is it dangerous to leave a dead tree standing?

A standing dead tree becomes more unpredictable over time as roots and wood decay, branches dry out and become brittle, and the tree loses the flexibility that let it bend in wind rather than break. How much risk it poses depends on its size, condition, and what is nearby: a small dead tree in an open yard is a different situation than a large one near a house, driveway, or fence line.
