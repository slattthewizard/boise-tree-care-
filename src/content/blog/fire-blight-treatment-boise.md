## What Fire Blight Actually Looks Like

Fire blight is a bacterial disease (*Erwinia amylovora*) that infects blossoms first, then spreads down through the twig and into larger wood. The name comes from the appearance: infected branches look scorched, as if a fire swept through just that section of the tree.

The signature symptom is the "shepherd's crook." New shoots wilt suddenly at the tip and curl over into a hook shape, turning black or dark brown while the leaves stay attached instead of dropping. That combination, a hooked, blackened shoot with dead leaves still clinging on, is about as diagnostic as a tree disease gets. If you see it in May or June on a pear or apple tree, it is almost certainly fire blight.

Other signs to look for:

- Blossoms and young fruit turning black and shriveling instead of developing normally
- A milky or amber-colored ooze seeping from infected bark, especially in warm, humid weather
- Cankers on branches and trunk with dark, sunken, cracked bark at the edges
- A general "burnt" look to entire sections of canopy by midsummer

## Which Trees in the Treasure Valley Are at Risk

Fire blight targets plants in the rose family, which covers a lot of what grows in Boise yards.

**High-risk hosts we see constantly:**

- Pear trees, both fruiting varieties and ornamental pears
- Apple and crabapple trees
- Mountain ash
- Hawthorn
- Cotoneaster and pyracantha, both popular hedge and foundation plants here

Ornamental pears deserve a special mention. They are everywhere in newer Meridian and Eagle subdivisions because they were sold as low-maintenance flowering trees, and fire blight moves through a row of them like wildfire once one tree gets infected. If you planted a line of them along a fence and one has blackened tips, check all of them.

## Why Boise Springs Set This Disease Up to Win

Fire blight bacteria need two things to spread fast: warm temperatures and moisture during bloom. The Treasure Valley gets both in most years. A stretch of 65 to 80 degree days combined with spring rain or even heavy dew during the two or three weeks your tree is flowering is exactly the window the bacteria exploit. Bees and other pollinators pick up the bacterial ooze from an infected blossom and carry it straight to the next flower they visit, so the disease can jump from tree to tree across a whole block in a single warm week.

Wind-driven rain splashes bacteria from cankers onto nearby blossoms too, which is why fire blight tends to show up worse on the side of a tree facing the prevailing spring wind.

## Fire Blight vs. Frost Damage vs. Normal Dieback

This is where most homeowners get stuck, and honestly it is a reasonable place to get stuck. All three can leave a tree looking rough in late spring.

| Symptom | Fire Blight | Frost Damage | Normal Dieback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shoot shape | Curls into a hook (shepherd's crook) | Wilts straight down, no curl | Gradual thinning, no curl |
| Leaf attachment | Dead leaves stay attached | Leaves often drop | Leaves drop normally |
| Ooze | Milky or amber ooze present | No ooze | No ooze |
| Pattern | Scattered, spreads from blossoms outward | Uniform, tied to a specific cold night | Isolated, usually one weak branch |
| Timing | Appears during or right after bloom | Appears after a late freeze | Can appear any time |
| Bark | Dark, cracked cankers at wound margins | No cankers | Cankers rare unless disease is present |

If you are still not sure after checking this table, that is a normal place to land. This is one of the cases where a five-minute look from someone who has seen a thousand of these beats guessing from photos online.

## The Pruning-Out Protocol: How We Actually Stop It

Once fire blight is confirmed, pruning is the primary treatment. There is no spray that cures an active infection. The goal is to physically remove the bacteria before they travel into wood you cannot afford to lose.

**The protocol we follow:**

1. **Cut at least 12 inches below the last visible symptom**, into clean wood. Fire blight travels inside the branch faster than the visible blackening suggests, so cutting right at the edge of the damage almost always leaves bacteria behind.
2. **Sterilize the pruning tool between every single cut.** A 10 percent bleach solution or isopropyl alcohol works. Skipping this step is the number one reason DIY pruning spreads the disease instead of stopping it. One infected cut, one clean cut with the same unsterilized blade, and you have just inoculated healthy wood.
3. **Remove cut material from the property.** Do not compost it, do not leave it in a brush pile near the tree. Bag it or burn it where local rules allow.
4. **Prune during dry weather.** Cutting into an infected tree during a wet spell can push bacteria into fresh wounds. Dry, cool days (or dormant season, when possible) are safer.

This works well for shoots you can reach from the ground or a step stool. It stops being a DIY job once infected wood is up in the main scaffold branches, near power lines, or high enough to need a ladder or chainsaw. That combination, height plus a tool that can weaken already-compromised wood, is where amateur tree work turns into an ER visit. Call a professional instead of pushing it.

## Timing: When to Cut and When to Wait

The safest time for major fire blight pruning is during winter dormancy, when the bacteria are not actively spreading and the tree is not pushing new growth for them to infect. Unfortunately, fire blight usually gets discovered in late spring when it is already visibly active, and waiting until winter means letting an active infection keep moving toward the trunk all summer.

Our general approach: if you spot shepherd's crooks in May or June, prune those specific shoots immediately using the 12-inch rule, focusing only on what is clearly infected. Save the bigger structural cleanup, thinning out cankered scaffold branches, for the following dormant season once the active spread has stopped. Cutting into a heavily infected tree during peak summer heat and growth can do more harm than good if it is not done carefully.

## What About Sprays?

Streptomycin and copper-based bactericides exist and can reduce new infections when applied during bloom, before symptoms appear. They are preventive, not curative. Once you see a shepherd's crook, spraying that branch does nothing for it. These products also need precise timing tied to bloom stage and weather, which is part of why we generally recommend them only as a supplement to good pruning and sanitation, not a replacement for it. Talk to us or check with [University of Idaho Extension](https://www.uidaho.edu/extension) before buying anything off the shelf, since products and availability change.

## When the Tree Has Already Lost

Sometimes fire blight wins. Signs a tree is past the point of saving:

- Cankers have reached the main trunk or major scaffold limbs, not just outer branches
- More than half the canopy shows active shepherd's crooks in a single season
- The tree has been reinfected two or three years running despite aggressive pruning
- Rootstock blight has set in, visible as oozing cankers near the graft union or soil line, which usually means the tree is done regardless of what you do above ground

A young ornamental pear in a Nampa front yard we looked at last spring had blight in the crown two years running. Compare that to the ongoing cost of yearly emergency pruning calls plus the risk to nearby fruit trees, and removal was the more sensible answer once the trunk was affected.

## Preventing Fire Blight Next Season

- **Avoid heavy nitrogen fertilizer** on susceptible trees. Fast, soft new growth is exactly what the bacteria prefer to infect.
- **Plant resistant varieties** when you have the choice. Ask about blight-resistant pear and apple cultivars before you plant, not after.
- **Prune for airflow** during the dormant season as part of routine [fruit tree pruning](/blog/fruit-tree-pruning-boise/). Dense, crowded canopies hold humidity longer after rain, which favors the bacteria.
- **Watch neighboring host plants.** Cotoneaster and hawthorn hedges near your fruit trees can carry fire blight without looking obviously sick, then pass it along during bloom.
- **Sanitize tools between every tree you prune**, not just between cuts on the same tree, if you are working on multiple susceptible plants in one visit.

## FAQs: Fire Blight in Boise

**Is fire blight contagious to other trees in my yard?** Yes. It spreads through pollinators, wind-driven rain, and contaminated pruning tools. A single infected tree can pass fire blight to every susceptible tree on the block in one wet spring.

**Can I save a pear tree with fire blight?** Often, yes, if you catch it early and prune aggressively using the 12-inch rule. Trees with infection limited to outer branches usually recover with proper pruning. Trees with trunk or rootstock cankers usually do not.

**Does fire blight kill apple trees?** It can, particularly young trees or trees where the infection reaches the trunk. Mature apple trees with infection limited to branch tips often survive with pruning and come back the following year.

**What does fire blight ooze look like?** A milky white to amber, sticky liquid seeping from cankers or cut wood, usually visible in warm, humid conditions. It dries to a dark, varnish-like residue on the bark.

**Should I remove my ornamental pear tree if it has fire blight?** Not automatically. If the infection is limited to a manageable portion of the canopy, pruning can save it. If cankers have reached the main trunk or the tree has been reinfected multiple years running, removal is usually the more cost-effective choice, especially if it is putting other fruit trees nearby at risk.

**Will spraying my tree cure fire blight?** No. Sprays like copper or streptomycin are preventive and only work when applied before or during bloom, before symptoms appear. Once you see blackened, hooked shoots, pruning is the only real treatment.

**How do I know if my pruning tools spread the disease?** If new infections appear in a straight-line pattern matching the order you made your cuts, unsterilized tools are the likely cause. This is the most common way we see DIY fire blight pruning backfire.
