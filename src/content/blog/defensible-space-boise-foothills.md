Defensible space means clearing flammable vegetation around your house in three rings, measured out from the structure itself: 0 to 5 feet, 5 to 30 feet, and 30 to 100 feet. The rules get stricter the closer you get to the house, and Idaho Department of Lands guidance uses this same three-zone layout for every property sitting against open hillside.

If you live along Hidden Springs, the Bogus Basin corridor, the Boise Highlands, or the foothill edges of Eagle, you are in what fire agencies call the wildland-urban interface. That is a fancy way of saying your backyard grass and the open hillside behind it are the same fuel bed, and a fire moving through cheatgrass and sagebrush behaves nothing like a fire in a subdivision three miles into the valley floor.

## The Quick Answer

| Zone | Distance from the House | What It Requires |
|---|---|---|
| Zone 0 (Immediate) | 0 – 5 ft | Nothing flammable touching the structure: no bark mulch against siding, no shrubs under windows, no tree limbs overhanging the roof |
| Zone 1 (Intermediate) | 5 – 30 ft | Tree crowns spaced at least 10 ft apart, lower limbs raised 6 – 10 ft off the ground, lawn mowed and irrigated, dead plants removed |
| Zone 2 (Extended) | 30 – 100 ft | Dead and downed wood cleared, ladder fuels removed, tree canopies thinned so fire can't carry from crown to crown |

These distances come from the same framework Idaho Department of Lands uses in its wildfire preparedness guidance, and they scale up on sloped ground. A hillside lot pushes fire uphill faster than flat ground does, so IDL recommends extending Zone 2 well past 100 feet on steep sites, not shrinking it.

## Why Boise's Foothills Are a Different Fire Environment

Most of Boise sits on flat valley floor, irrigated and landscaped, where a grass fire has a hard time finding anything to burn. The foothills are the opposite. Cheatgrass cures to a fire-ready tan by early summer, and it sits right up against fences, decks, and often the house itself.

Wind is the other multiplier. Foothill canyons funnel gusts downhill in exactly the conditions that make a small fire spread fast, and embers thrown ahead of an active fire front can travel well over a mile and start a new spot fire with no direct flame contact at all. That is why the three zones exist: each ring is designed to deny embers and flame a place to catch before they ever reach your walls.

## Zone 0: The First 5 Feet, Your Ember-Proof Ring

This is the smallest zone and the one worth getting right first, because it is the last line of defense. Nothing combustible should sit within 5 feet of siding, a deck, or a fence attached to the house: no bark mulch, no juniper or arborvitae planted against the foundation, and no tree branch low enough to brush the roofline.

If a tree on your lot has a limb reaching into this zone, that limb needs to come off, and if the whole tree leans into it, the tree itself may need to go. Deadwood removal alone typically runs **$150 to $800**, and a full crown raise to lift limbs clear of the house falls under standard trimming work. Our guide on [pine and conifer problems in Boise](/blog/pine-tree-problems-boise/) covers why resinous evergreens, a common foothills landscaping choice, need extra attention in this zone specifically.

## Zone 1: 5 to 30 Feet, Breaking Up the Fuel

Zone 1 is about spacing, not removal. The goal is 10 feet of clear air between tree crowns so fire can't leap from one canopy to the next, plus limbs raised 6 to 10 feet off the ground so a ground fire can't climb into the canopy in the first place.

Drought-stressed trees are a bigger liability here than healthy ones. A stressed tree drops needles and small dead branches faster and holds more dry fuel in its crown, which is exactly what this zone is meant to eliminate. ## Zone 2: 30 to 100 Feet, Slowing the Fire Down

This outer ring does not need to look manicured, it needs to not carry fire. That means clearing dead and downed wood, removing dying trees, mowing dry grass down, and thinning tree density so crowns aren't touching across the zone.

A homeowner off Bogus Basin Road with a half-acre of native ponderosa and dry brush behind the house typically faces a bigger job here than the standard trim: removing several dead or dying trees and thinning the rest can land anywhere in the **$1,500 to $8,000+** lot-clearing range depending on density and access. It's a real cost, but it is the zone that buys firefighters room to actually defend a structure if a fire does reach the property.

If your Zone 2 clearing brings a crew close to an overhead service line, that line belongs to Idaho Power, not to any tree company. Never let anyone climb or cut near an energized line themselves; Idaho Power's number for downed or hazardous lines is **(208) 388-2323**.

Removing the junipers, raising the limbs on two mature pines nearby, and hauling off the debris ran just under **$2,100**, most of it Zone 0 and Zone 1 work rather than anything in the outer ring.

Some of this work is safe to do yourself: raking needles, mowing dry grass, moving firewood stacks away from the house. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the three defensible space zones?
Zone 0 runs from the structure out to 5 feet and should have nothing flammable touching the house. Zone 1 extends from 5 to 30 feet and focuses on spacing trees apart and raising their lower limbs. Zone 2 runs from 30 to 100 feet and focuses on thinning density and clearing dead fuel so fire loses momentum before it reaches the closer zones.

### How much defensible space does a foothills property actually need?
The standard is 100 feet total on flat ground, but Idaho Department of Lands guidance recommends extending that distance on sloped sites, since fire moves faster uphill. A property backed directly against open hillside should treat 100 feet as a minimum, not a target.

### Do I need a permit to remove trees for defensible space in Boise?
It depends on the tree and its location. Some larger or protected trees fall under city or county rules even on private property, so it's worth confirming before cutting rather than assuming defensible space work is automatically exempt.

### Does defensible space guarantee my house won't burn in a wildfire?
No, nothing does. What it does is remove the fuel that lets embers and ground fire reach the structure directly, which meaningfully improves the odds and gives firefighters a safer space to work if a fire does reach the property.

### How often do I need to redo defensible space clearing?
At least once a year, typically in spring before grass cures and cheatgrass dries out. Trees keep growing and dropping deadwood, so a property that was compliant last summer can drift out of compliance by the next one without a repeat check.
