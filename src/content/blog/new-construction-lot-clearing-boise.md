Permits aren't automatic either way: your building permit covers grading the pad, but any tree sitting in the public right-of-way or a utility easement still needs its own sign-off before a saw touches it.

If you're about to build in Boise, Meridian, Nampa, Caldwell, Eagle, or Garden City, the lot rarely arrives as a blank slate. Most raw parcels carry a mix of volunteer growth that has to go and a few established trees worth keeping if the site plan allows it. Getting the order of operations right, permit check first, then clearing, then protection for whatever stays, saves real money over calling a crew back mid-build.

## The Permit Question Most Builders Skip

Two separate permit questions collide on a build lot, and it's easy to assume one covers the other. Your city or county building and grading permit authorizes disturbing the site for the new structure. It does not automatically authorize removing a tree that sits in the public right-of-way, the strip between the sidewalk and the curb, or an unopened frontage easement.

Boise, like the other cities in the service area, runs its own street-tree permit process, and a raw lot in an established neighborhood is exactly where an old street tree tends to sit. Outside city limits, road-adjacent trees typically fall under the county rather than a city forestry office. Either way, a five-minute call to the local planning department before grading starts is worth more than guessing.

Utility easements are the second trap. A tree growing over a buried gas, water, or sewer line, or anywhere near an overhead distribution line, may need coordination with the utility before it comes down. Idaho Power handles all vegetation work on its own distribution lines and can be reached directly at (208) 388-2323 for anything touching their equipment. Never assume a tree crew can cut near a power line without that call happening first.

Before you assume a specific tree is free and clear, check current requirements with [the City of Boise](https://www.cityofboise.org) if you're building inside city limits, or [Ada County](https://adacounty.id.gov) for an unincorporated parcel. Canyon County builders working around Nampa or Caldwell should confirm with their own county planning office the same way.

## What Actually Drives the Cost of Clearing a Build Lot

Lot clearing pricing follows the same size tiers as our standard [tree removal service](/tree-removal-boise/), just stacked across however many trees are coming down. A small tree under 30 ft runs $300 to $800, a medium tree from 30 to 60 ft runs $800 to $2,500, and anything over 60 ft lands in the $2,500 to $5,000+ range. A lot with several large trees, or one requiring crane access for a tight or extreme situation, can push the extreme tier of $5,000 to $8,000 or more.

Stump grinding is priced separately, generally $75 to $400 per stump depending on diameter, with volume discounts common when several stumps get ground in one visit. Access matters just as much on a raw lot as it does on a finished property. A parcel a truck and chipper can drive straight onto prices closer to the size-tier baseline, while a landlocked back portion that has to be hand-carried out adds real labor cost.

**A scenario from Eagle:** A homeowner building off a half-acre parcel near Floating Feather Road needed six volunteer cottonwoods and a thick stand of overgrown Russian olive cleared before the foundation survey. Truck access was good on three sides of the lot. The full clearing, stump grinding, and debris haul-off came to roughly $5,600, within the expected range for that many trees at that size.

**A scenario from Nampa:** A smaller in-town lot only had three trees in the way of the new footprint, a dead honey locust and two overgrown ornamentals near the property line. With easy access and no crane needed, the job priced at about $2,100 including stump removal, close to the low end of the range for a lot that size.

## Deciding Which Trees Are Worth Keeping

Not every tree on a raw lot is a problem, and clearing everything down to dirt usually isn't the cheapest or the smartest move. A healthy, well-placed tree outside the building footprint, driveway, and utility trenches can stay and still add shade and curb appeal to the finished property.

The decision comes down to three things: is the tree healthy, does its location conflict with the actual construction plan rather than just looking like it might, and can its root zone survive the grading and trenching that's about to happen nearby. A quick health assessment before the survey stakes go in answers the first question and often changes the site plan for the better. **If a tree is staying, plan its protection before equipment ever shows up on site**, not after the first pass with a skid steer. ## Getting the Order of Operations Right

The cheapest lot clearing happens in a specific sequence: survey and mark the trees first, confirm which ones need a permit check, clear and grind stumps for everything coming out, then fence off protection zones for anything staying before grading equipment arrives. Doing clearing and grading as one undifferentiated pass is how a tree you meant to keep ends up damaged by accident.

**Ready to get your lot surveyed and priced?** [Schedule a free on-site estimate](/#contact) before your grading permit comes through. A crew can flag any right-of-way or easement trees during that same visit, so you're not finding out about a permit requirement after equipment is already booked.

Timing also matters for cost and for the trees you keep. Clearing during the dormant season, roughly late fall through late winter, puts less stress on any nearby root zones you're trying to protect, and crews are often more available before spring building season ramps up.

## When to Stop and Call a Professional

Lot clearing on a raw parcel is not a weekend DIY project once trees get past a modest size. Stop and call a licensed and insured crew the moment any of these apply: the work needs a chainsaw above shoulder height, a tree is anywhere near a power line, felling requires climbing, or a tree could reach a structure, a fence, or a neighboring property if it falls wrong.

New building sites also tend to have less clearance than a finished yard, with survey stakes, silt fencing, and future foundation lines all in play. A crew that clears lots regularly knows how to drop and section trees without damaging stakes, utility markers, or the grading plan underneath.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a permit to clear trees on a lot before building?

It depends on where the trees sit rather than whether you're building. A tree in the public right-of-way or a utility easement typically needs its own approval separate from your building or grading permit, while trees entirely inside your private lot line usually don't trigger a city tree permit on their own. Check with your city's planning department or, outside city limits, your county, before grading starts.

### How much does it cost to clear a build lot in the Boise area?

Most lot clearing jobs run $1,500 to $8,000 or more, with the total driven by how many trees come down, their size, and how much stump grinding and debris hauling follows. A small lot with two or three trees typically lands near the low end, while a larger or densely wooded parcel with crane-access trees can push toward the top of the range.

### Can I keep some trees while the rest of the lot gets cleared?

Yes, and it's common on lots with at least one well-placed, healthy tree outside the building footprint. The key is fencing off a protection zone around any keeper before grading equipment arrives, since root damage from equipment traffic often doesn't show up as decline until a year or two later.

### What happens to the wood and stumps after clearing?

Stump grinding is priced separately from removal, generally $75 to $400 per stump depending on diameter, with volume pricing common when several stumps are ground in the same visit. Wood and debris hauling is typically handled by the clearing crew as part of the job, though options and costs vary depending on how much material comes off the lot.

### Should clearing happen before or after the building permit is approved?

Survey and mark the trees, and confirm any right-of-way or easement permit questions, before you finalize grading plans. Actual clearing usually happens once the building permit process is underway, but flagging protected trees early lets the site plan work around them instead of discovering a conflict after equipment is already scheduled.
