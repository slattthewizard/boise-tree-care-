You need a written arborist report when someone besides you, a city permit reviewer, an insurance adjuster, an HOA board, or the other side of a dispute, needs documented proof of a tree's condition instead of just your word for it. In Boise, that comes up most in four situations: a street-tree removal permit application, a storm damage insurance claim, a neighbor or HOA disagreement, and a construction project near a tree you're required to protect.

A verbal opinion is fine for most everyday decisions, whether to prune, whether to remove, whether to stop worrying about a tree. A written report is a different product entirely: a signed, dated document describing the tree's condition, the specific defects found, and a professional recommendation, built to be read by someone who never stood under the tree with you.

## Permits: Boise's Street-Tree Rules and When Proof Matters

Boise runs a street-tree permit program, and removing a tree in the parking strip without one, even though it sits on land next to your property, is not treated as an automatic right. If you're applying to remove a declining or damaged tree, [Boise's street-tree permit process](/blog/tree-removal-permits-boise/) moves faster when the reviewer has something concrete to look at instead of just your description.

If a tree is obviously dead, split, or already down, most reviewers won't push back much and a written report is overkill. The gray area is a tree that still looks alive but has a defect photos don't fully explain. That's exactly when paying for documentation upfront saves a second trip and a second wait for approval. Permit requirements can shift, so check the current rules directly at [cityofboise.org](https://www.cityofboise.org) before you apply.

## Insurance Claims: Proving What the Storm Actually Did

Insurance adjusters don't take a phone description as proof. Idaho homeowner policies typically cover $500 to $1,000 per tree for storm-related removal, up to $5,000 per storm event, but that coverage usually depends on proving the damage was sudden and weather-caused rather than years of untreated decline.

A homeowner in [Eagle](/tree-service-eagle/) had a large silver maple partially uproot in a spring windstorm, leaning hard onto a fence without fully going down. The insurer's first offer covered cleanup but denied the $3,400 removal cost, calling the tree "pre-existing decline" in the letter. A written report documenting fresh root-plate failure from wind, not slow decay, was enough for the adjuster to reopen the claim and approve the full amount.

## Neighbor and HOA Disputes: When Paper Beats a Shouting Match

Tree disputes between neighbors, or between a homeowner and an HOA board, usually come down to one disagreement: whose tree is it, and how bad is the problem really. [Idaho's rules on trimming a neighbor's tree](/blog/neighbor-tree-trimming-rights-idaho/) get complicated fast once roots or branches cross a property line, and a verbal opinion from one side rarely settles anything for the other.

In Nampa, two neighbors disagreed for over a year about a shared-line cottonwood whose roots had lifted one side's patio slab. Neither wanted to cover the $2,200 removal alone, and the argument over how much blame belonged to each yard wasn't going anywhere. A written report documenting the trunk's actual location and the extent of root damage on each property gave both sides a number to split instead of a fight to keep having.

HOA boards asking for removal or protection usually want the same thing: paperwork showing the decision wasn't arbitrary. If your HOA requires proof before approving anything, ask specifically what the board wants documented, a diameter measurement, a hazard rating, a disease diagnosis, since that shapes what the report needs to cover.

## Construction Near Trees You're Required to Protect

If you're pulling a permit for an addition, a new driveway, or a lot split near a tree you want to keep, [protecting trees during construction](/blog/protect-trees-during-construction/) sometimes isn't optional. Root zone and canopy protection can be a condition of the permit itself, especially on foothills lots or properties with mature trees the city has flagged.

A written report done before ground gets broken establishes the tree's baseline health, which matters if a dispute comes up later about whether construction activity caused decline that wasn't there before. It also tells you upfront whether a tree near the build area is worth protecting or already compromised enough that removal is the more honest plan.

None of this is a DIY judgment call. If a tree near a build site or a property line is leaning toward a structure, or the work would mean a chainsaw above shoulder height, climbing, or anything near a power line, that's the point to call a professional instead of guessing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who can write an arborist report that a city or insurer will actually accept?
An ISA-certified arborist, meaning someone credentialed through the International Society of Arboriculture, is the standard most cities and insurers expect on a signed report. A report from someone without that credential can get questioned or rejected outright, especially for a permit review or a contested claim. Ask upfront whether the person inspecting your tree is ISA-certified before you pay for anything.

### How long does it take to get a written report after the inspection?
Most straightforward reports, one tree with one clear issue, can turn around within a few business days of the site visit. More complex situations, like documenting root damage across two properties or building a construction protection plan, take longer since they involve more measurements and often photos from multiple angles. Ask about turnaround when you schedule, especially if you're working against a permit deadline or a claim filing window.

### Does a verbal opinion ever count instead of a written report?
For your own decision about whether to prune or remove a tree on your own property, yes, a verbal on-site assessment is usually all you need. The moment a third party, a permit reviewer, an adjuster, or the other side of a dispute, has to evaluate the tree's condition without having been there, a verbal opinion stops being enough. That's the line that decides whether you need paperwork or just a decision.

### Can one report cover both a permit application and an insurance claim?
Sometimes, if the report is thorough enough to answer both the city's permit questions and the insurer's damage-cause questions, but it depends on what each one specifically asks for. Tell us upfront if you need a report to serve double duty so nothing gets left out. Ordering one broad report instead of two narrow ones is sometimes the cheaper route, and sometimes it isn't.

### What if the report finds the tree isn't actually a hazard?
That's a genuinely useful outcome, not a wasted cost. A report clearing a tree can support a permit denial appeal, back up a decision to keep a tree an HOA wanted gone, or give you peace of mind about a leaning or hollow tree everyone's been worried about. Documentation that says a tree is fine carries just as much weight as documentation that says it isn't.
