You plant a young tree in spring, watch it settle in all summer, and by the following March the trunk is shredded, or worse, gone entirely at the base. Wildlife damage on new trees happens fast, usually overnight, and it hits properties in Eagle, Star, and the foothills-adjacent parts of Boise hardest, anywhere deer traffic is regular and snow sits on the ground long enough for voles to work undisturbed underneath it.

The good news is that both problems, deer rub and vole girdling, are well understood and genuinely preventable with the right barrier at the right time of year. The bad news is that most homeowners find out about the risk only after it's already happened once.

## Deer Rub: Why Bucks Damage Trees in Fall

Buck rub isn't deer browsing on foliage, though that happens too. It's a specific fall behavior tied to the breeding season. From roughly September through November, bucks rub their antlers against young tree trunks to remove the velvet covering, mark territory with scent from glands near their forehead, and, some research suggests, work off aggression heading into rut. The result is stripped, shredded bark on trunks typically between 1 and 4 inches in diameter, right in the sweet spot of a newly planted tree.

A single rubbing session can strip bark most of the way around a young trunk in one night. Unlike the gradual, repeated damage from something like [mower and trimmer contact](/blog/lawn-equipment-trunk-damage/), deer rub damage is often severe and complete the first time it happens, because it's a deliberate, sustained rubbing motion rather than an accidental nick.

Trees most attractive to bucks for rubbing tend to have smooth, thin bark and some flexibility, similar species profile to what's vulnerable to mower damage: young maples, fruit trees, and ornamentals with a trunk diameter in that 1 to 4 inch range. Established trees with thick, rough bark are far less appealing targets.

## Vole Girdling: The Damage You Don't See Coming

Voles cause a different kind of damage, and it's sneakier because it happens under cover, literally. These small rodents are active year-round but do the most damage in winter, when snow cover or thick mulch gives them a protected tunnel system right at ground level, hidden from predators and from you. Inside that cover, voles gnaw on bark and the cambium layer at the base of young trunks, sometimes girdling a tree completely around its circumference without a single visible sign until the snow melts.

This is the cruel part: by the time you notice the damage in spring, it's already done, and if the girdling went all the way around the trunk, the tree is often already dead or dying even though the canopy might still look fine for a few more weeks on stored energy.

Voles are drawn to the same conditions that are otherwise great tree care: mulch rings, tall grass or ground cover right up against the trunk, and snow that piles up around the base. The mulch that protects a tree from mower damage can, ironically, become vole habitat if it's piled too thick or too close to the trunk without any kind of guard underneath. This is part of why [winter tree care](/blog/winter-tree-care-boise/) in the Treasure Valley involves more than just wrapping a trunk and walking away; timing and placement matter as much as the material itself.

## Trunk Guards: How to Protect Young Trees from Both

The most effective, lowest-effort solution for both threats is a physical trunk guard installed on young trees, and it's worth doing proactively rather than waiting to see if you have a problem.

**Types of guards that work well in the Treasure Valley:**

- **Plastic spiral wraps:** inexpensive, easy to install, wind around the trunk from the base up to 18-24 inches. Good general protection against both deer rub and vole gnawing at ground level.
- **Rigid mesh or hardware cloth cylinders:** a cylinder of half-inch hardware cloth buried an inch or two into the soil and extending up 18-24 inches above the anticipated snow line blocks voles from tunneling underneath and deters rubbing.
- **Corrugated plastic tree guards:** rigid tubes that clip or wrap around the trunk, widely available and effective against both threats, though bulkier looking than wraps.

**Installation timing and care matters:**

- Install guards before the fall rutting season starts (by early September) and before ground freezes hard enough that soil work becomes difficult
- Extend the guard high enough to cover the trunk above expected snow depth, since voles will work at whatever level the snow lets them reach
- Leave enough room inside the guard for trunk growth, and check annually that it isn't constricting the trunk as the tree thickens, the same girdling risk that comes up with tight [staking materials left on too long](/blog/tree-staking-new-trees/)
- Remove or loosen wraps in late spring once rutting season and snow cover risk have passed, so the trunk isn't sitting inside plastic all summer trapping moisture

## Fencing vs. Repellent Sprays

For broader deer browsing protection (not just rub damage, but deer eating foliage and young branches), homeowners often ask whether fencing or spray repellents work better. Based on what we see across Treasure Valley properties, fencing wins decisively for anything you're serious about protecting.

**Fencing that actually works:** Deer can clear fences up to 8 feet in a standing jump, so effective deer fencing around a vulnerable planting area needs real height, typically 7 to 8 feet for full exclusion. For individual young trees rather than a whole garden bed, a simple cylinder of welded wire fencing 4 to 5 feet tall around each trunk, wide enough that deer can't reach foliage through the gaps, is usually sufficient since you're protecting the tree itself rather than trying to keep deer off an entire property.

**Repellent sprays:** these can reduce browsing somewhat, especially products with egg solids or predator scent compounds, but they need reapplication every few weeks and after rain, they don't address rub damage at all since rubbing isn't about taste or smell deterrence, and effectiveness varies a lot by how hungry local deer populations are that season. We consider sprays a supplement at best, not a primary defense for a tree you actually care about.

## A Local Example

**A property near the Boise foothills** planted six young ornamental trees, a mix of flowering crabapple and Japanese maple, in spring 2024 with no trunk protection. By the following February, four of the six had significant deer rub damage from the previous fall's rutting season, and one of those four also showed vole girdling discovered when the snow melted in March, essentially finishing off a tree the deer had already half-damaged. Two trees survived with partial trunk scarring; two others, including the one with combined deer and vole damage, died and needed replacement. Total replacement cost for two mature-ish ornamentals ran close to $700, on top of the original planting cost. Spiral wraps at roughly $8 each for all six trees, installed that first September, would have cost under $50 total.

**A different outcome in Star**, where deer traffic is heavy along a greenbelt-adjacent property: the homeowner installed rigid mesh cylinders around eight new fruit trees the same fall they were planted, sized to allow for several years of trunk growth. Four winters later, zero rub or vole damage on any of the eight, and the guards are only now getting snug enough to need upsizing.

## When Damage Has Already Happened

If you're finding rub or gnaw damage now rather than preventing it in advance, the same rule from mower damage applies: check how much of the trunk's circumference is affected. Damage covering less than about a quarter of the circumference gives the tree a reasonable chance to compartmentalize and continue growing. Damage that's gone most of the way or all the way around is usually not survivable, and no wound sealant or wrap applied after the fact reverses that.

For partial damage, install a guard immediately to prevent repeat rubbing on the same tree (bucks and voles will return to the same trunks in following seasons if nothing changes), and give the tree a full growing season before deciding whether it's recovering or declining.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When exactly is deer rubbing season in the Treasure Valley?** Primarily September through November, tied to the buck breeding cycle, though timing can shift a few weeks earlier or later depending on the year.

**Do voles cause damage in summer too, or only under snow?** Voles are active year-round, but snow cover gives them the safest, most sustained access to gnaw at trunk bases without being seen by predators, which is why winter damage tends to be worse and more complete.

**Will a repellent spray stop deer from rubbing their antlers on my tree?** No. Rubbing isn't a feeding or taste behavior, so taste and scent repellents that reduce browsing don't reliably stop rub damage. A physical barrier is the only dependable protection against rubbing.

**How tall does a trunk guard need to be?** Extend it to at least 24 inches above ground, and account for expected snow depth on top of that in higher-elevation or foothills properties, since voles will work right up to the snow line.

**Can I use chicken wire instead of hardware cloth for a vole barrier?** Chicken wire's gaps are usually too large to reliably stop vole access at ground level. Half-inch hardware cloth or purpose-made mesh guards are more effective.

**Is it too late to protect a tree that's already a few years old?** Not at all. Guards help at any age, though the risk period narrows somewhat as trunk bark thickens with maturity. Fruit trees and thin-barked ornamentals stay somewhat vulnerable for longer than you might expect.

**Do these same risks apply to trees I'm planting this spring versus in fall?** The tree itself doesn't care what season it's planted, but if you plant in spring, make sure a guard is in place well before the following fall's rutting season and before winter snow arrives, not after.
