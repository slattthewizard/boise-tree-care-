## Whose Insurance Actually Pays?

This surprises a lot of people: in most cases, your homeowners insurance pays for damage to your house, even if the tree that fell belonged to your neighbor.

Here's the general rule in Idaho and most states. Damage caused by a falling tree is typically treated as an "act of God" if the tree was healthy and the fall was due to weather, wind, or an unpredictable event. In that scenario, your own policy covers the repair to your structure, and your neighbor's policy is not involved, regardless of which yard the tree grew in.

**The exception is negligence.** If your neighbor knew their tree was dead, diseased, or visibly hazardous and did nothing about it, and it then fell on your house, their insurance may be liable instead of (or in addition to) yours. Proving negligence usually requires evidence that the danger was visible and reported beforehand, like a certified arborist's prior assessment, a code complaint, or documented warnings to the tree's owner. Without that kind of paper trail, insurers default to the standard rule: your policy, your roof.

This is general guidance, not legal advice specific to your policy. Every homeowners policy is written a little differently, so the details of your coverage, deductibles, and exclusions should come from your own insurance agent or a read of your declarations page. If you want a ballpark sense of what removal or cleanup might run before you're even deep into a claim, our [cost calculator](/cost-calculator/) is a reasonable starting point.

## What Your Policy Typically Covers

Most standard homeowners policies in Idaho include coverage for:

- Structural repair to your home (roof, siding, windows, framing)
- Damage to a detached structure, like a shed or fence, sometimes under a separate coverage limit
- Emergency tree removal, specifically the portion of the tree that's on or blocking access to your home
- Debris removal, often up to a set dollar limit separate from the structural repair limit

**What's usually not automatically covered:** removing the rest of the tree that didn't damage anything, or removing a tree that's simply leaning or at risk but hasn't fallen yet. If a storm knocked over a tree in your yard and it landed nowhere near your house, that's typically a homeowner expense, not a claim. Check your specific policy for how it handles that distinction.

## Step by Step: What to Do in the First 24 Hours

1. **Get everyone out of harm's way.** If there's a hole in the roof, a compromised ceiling, or any sign the tree is still shifting, do not stay in that part of the house.
2. **Call for emergency mitigation, not full removal, first.** A tarp over an open roof and stabilization of the tree itself prevents a bad situation from becoming a worse one (rain damage, additional structural failure) while you sort out the claim.
3. **Photograph everything before anything moves.** Wide shots of the whole scene, close-ups of the point of impact, and photos of the tree's base and root ball if it uprooted. This is the evidence your claim will lean on.
4. **Call your insurance company the same day, or as close to it as possible.** Ask specifically about emergency mitigation coverage; most policies will pay for temporary tarping and stabilization even before an adjuster visits.
5. **Do not sign anything or authorize permanent repairs until an adjuster has seen the damage,** unless your insurer explicitly tells you to proceed. Emergency tarping is the exception; that should happen immediately regardless.
6. **Keep every receipt.** Emergency services, temporary lodging if the home is uninhabitable, tarping, and removal costs are often reimbursable.

## Emergency Tarping and Removal: What Actually Happens

When we get an emergency call for a tree on a house, the first priority is stabilizing the situation, not aesthetics. That usually means:

- Assessing whether the tree or remaining limbs are still under tension and could shift further
- Removing or securing the portion of the tree causing active damage or blocking safe entry
- Coordinating with a roofer for emergency tarping if the structure is exposed
- Documenting the damage with photos before and during removal, which we can provide to you or directly to your adjuster

This work typically needs to happen within hours, not days, especially if weather is in the forecast. Most insurers understand this and will reimburse reasonable emergency mitigation costs even if you haven't gotten a claim number yet. Keep the invoice.

## Additional Living Expenses: If You Can't Stay in Your Home

If the damage is severe enough that part of your house is unsafe to live in, most homeowners policies include additional living expense (ALE) coverage, sometimes called loss of use coverage. This can reimburse reasonable costs for a hotel, temporary rental, and even extra costs for meals if your kitchen is unusable, while repairs are underway.

A few things worth knowing about this coverage before you assume it applies:

- It usually only kicks in when the home is genuinely uninhabitable, not simply inconvenient. A single damaged bedroom with the rest of the house functional may not qualify.
- There's typically a coverage limit and sometimes a time limit as well, so ask your adjuster directly what your specific policy allows.
- Keep every receipt related to temporary housing and meals. Insurers generally want documentation, not estimates, when it comes time to reimburse this expense.

If you're not sure whether your situation qualifies, ask your adjuster directly and early. It's a common enough scenario that they'll have a clear answer, and it's better to ask on day one than to assume you're covered and find out otherwise weeks later.

## What an Adjuster Is Actually Looking For

Understanding what the insurance adjuster needs to see can speed up the whole process considerably. When an adjuster inspects tree damage to a home, they're generally trying to establish three things: the cause of the failure (weather event versus neglect), the extent of the damage (structural versus cosmetic), and whether any pre-existing conditions affected the claim (a previously known hazard, prior damage, deferred maintenance).

This is exactly why photo documentation from before any cleanup matters so much, and why an arborist's assessment can carry real weight in a disputed claim. If the adjuster's initial read is that the tree was already unhealthy or that the homeowner should have addressed a known hazard sooner, having a professional opinion on record, ideally one obtained before the storm rather than after, changes the conversation considerably. This is also part of why an annual or biannual [tree health assessment](/blog/tree-health-assessment-boise/) is worth doing proactively, not just after something has already gone wrong.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does homeowners insurance cover a tree falling on my house in Idaho?**
In most cases, yes. Standard homeowners policies cover structural damage from a falling tree, along with the cost to remove the portion of the tree causing the damage, regardless of whose property the tree originated from, as long as the fall wasn't due to owner negligence.

**What if my neighbor's tree fell on my house?**
Your own homeowners policy typically pays first. Your insurer may later pursue your neighbor's insurance for reimbursement (subrogation) if negligence can be shown, but that process happens between insurers, not something you need to manage yourself.

**Will my insurance cover removing the whole tree, or just the part on my house?**
Usually just the portion causing damage or blocking access. Removing the remainder of the tree, if it's not touching the structure, is often a separate cost. Check your policy's specific debris removal terms.

**How fast do I need to act after a tree falls on my house?**
Get emergency mitigation (tarping, stabilization) done the same day if at all possible, and call your insurer as soon as it's safe to do so. Most policies expect prompt reporting and reasonable steps to prevent further damage.

**Do I need a professional assessment for the insurance claim?**
It helps significantly. An arborist's documentation of the tree's condition, the point of failure, and photos of the damage gives your adjuster (and your case, if negligence is disputed) solid evidence rather than a verbal description.

**What if the tree was already dead and I never reported it?**
This can complicate a claim if you're trying to establish neighbor liability, since proving prior notice is central to a negligence claim. If it's your own dead tree that fell on your own house, this usually doesn't change your coverage, since most homeowners policies still cover the resulting damage regardless of the tree's prior condition.

**Should I get a second opinion if my insurance company disputes the cause of the tree failure?**
Yes. An independent arborist assessment can document root health, decay, or structural defects that support or contradict the insurer's determination. This is common enough that we do it regularly for homeowners navigating a disputed claim.
