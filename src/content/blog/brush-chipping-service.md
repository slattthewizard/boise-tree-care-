Brush chipping is exactly what it sounds like: a crew feeds your branches, limbs and brush through a wood chipper on your property and turns the pile into wood chips. You end up with a chip pile (or none, if you ask them to take it) instead of a stack of branches. It is a good fit after pruning, a storm, or a cleanup, when you have a lot of woody material and no easy place to put it.

Below: what a chipper can and can't take, how chipping differs from hauling, what to do with the chips, and how to set up your pile so a crew can work fast.

## What a Brush Chipping Service Includes

A chipping service usually means a crew arrives with a chipper, either towed behind a truck or on a trailer, and processes the brush you have piled up. Some jobs are chipping only. Others are part of a bigger visit, like a [tree trimming](/tree-trimming-boise/) or removal, where the crew chips the limbs they cut as they go.

The pieces that matter:

- The crew feeds branches into the chipper, usually butt end first.
- Chips land in a pile, in the truck, or in a chip bed, depending on what you want.
- Anything the chipper cannot take, such as big trunk sections, gets cut up and either left as firewood or hauled.

If you just have a few small branches, you may not need a service at all. Chipping makes sense when the volume is more than you can bag, burn or drop at a yard waste site in a reasonable amount of time.

## What a Chipper Can and Cannot Take

Chippers are rated by the largest branch diameter they can feed, and that number varies a lot by machine. Small homeowner units handle thin branches. Larger trailer-mounted and truck-mounted chippers used by tree crews take much thicker limbs. We are not going to give you a single number because it depends on the machine the crew brings, so if you have big limbs, ask what they can chip when you call for an estimate.

Some general rules hold for almost any chipper:

- **Green, freshly cut limbs chip better than old dry wood.** Dry, hard, twisted wood is tougher on the blades.
- **Branches with lots of side shoots and leaves feed fine,** and they make a mix of wood and leaf that is good for mulch.
- **Stumps, root balls and big trunk sections do not go through a chipper.** A [stump grinder](/stump-grinding-boise/) handles those, and the grindings are a separate product from chips.
- **Dirt, rocks, wire and metal are the enemy.** They dull or damage the blades and can throw debris.

Anything above the machine's limit is bucked into shorter rounds and handled another way. That could mean firewood, hauling, or leaving them on site for you to deal with. Our post on [what to do with wood after tree removal](/blog/what-to-do-with-wood-after-removal/) covers those options.

## Chipping vs. Hauling Brush Away

These are two different ways to get rid of the same pile, and they are often confused.

**Chipping** reduces the volume. Branches that fill a truck bed take up a fraction of the space as chips. You can leave the chips on your property as mulch, or ask the crew to take them.

**Hauling** moves the material off your property whole or chipped, to a landfill, transfer site or recycling facility. It solves the "I don't want this here" problem but does nothing to reduce the pile or make use of it. Our [tree debris disposal guide](/blog/tree-debris-disposal-boise/) covers where hauled tree material can go.

Often you get both: the crew chips what is chippable and hauls the rest. If your goal is a tidy yard and nothing left behind, say so. If you want the chips, say that instead, because it changes how the crew sets up. If you are clearing an overgrown lot rather than dealing with a single pile, see [brush clearing for overgrown yards](/blog/brush-clearing-overgrown-yard/).

## What to Do With the Chips

Fresh wood chips are worth keeping. Most yards can use more of them than people expect.

[University of Maryland Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/mulching-trees-and-shrubs) says fresh arborist wood chips are fine around trees and shrubs as long as they are not mixed into the soil, and it does not recommend them for annuals or vegetables. It also says mulch should never touch the trunk, and suggests pulling it back about 3 inches. [Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2016-06-14-mulching-and-wood-chips) points to local tree care companies as a source of arborist chips and recommends a layer of chips 4 to 6 inches deep, reapplied as it breaks down. The two sources give different depths, so use the range as a guide, and in heavy Boise clay, we would lean to the thinner end so the soil can still breathe.

[Oregon State University Extension](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/ask-extension/featured/wood-chips-mulch) adds that fine chips, like sawdust, tie up more soil nitrogen than coarse chips, and that tilling chips into garden soil makes the nitrogen problem worse. Spread them on top. Paths, tree rings and shrub beds are good places.

Our guide to [mulching around trees](/blog/how-to-mulch-around-trees/) covers depth and the donut-not-volcano rule in detail, so we will not repeat it here.

A few practical notes:

- Piles of fresh chips can heat up as they break down, so do not leave a big mound against a house, fence or tree trunk.
- For fire safety, [Oregon State University Extension](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9184-keeping-your-home-property-safe-wildfire) says to keep piles of woody material, chips included, at least 30 feet from your home and other structures, and to use chips sparingly within 30 feet of the house. That matters in the foothills and anywhere with dry grass nearby.
- If you do not want them, tell the crew before the job. Chips can be loaded out and taken away.

## How to Prep a Pile for Chipping

A little prep makes chipping faster and safer, and it keeps the crew's time (and your bill) lower.

1. **Stack with the cut ends facing the same way.** The butt end of each branch is what feeds into the machine first, so lining them up lets the crew pull from the pile without turning every piece around.
2. **Keep it clean.** No dirt, rocks, wire, rope, nails, screws, fencing or treated lumber in the pile. Metal can wreck chipper knives.
3. **Cut or separate out trunk-size pieces.** If a limb is too thick to chip, set it aside.
4. **Sort out anything that is not wood.** Leaves and small twigs are fine. Trash, plastic and sod are not.
5. **Keep the pile where a truck can reach it.** A pile at the curb or driveway is easier than one behind a locked gate or down a narrow side yard. Tell the crew about tight access when you call.
6. **Do not mix in invasive or diseased wood without saying so.** Material from a tree with a disease or pest may need different handling, and a crew should know what is in the pile.

## When Chipping Makes Sense

Chipping is worth it when:

- You just pruned several trees or shrubs and have a big pile of limbs.
- A storm or windstorm dropped branches around the yard.
- You want mulch for beds or trees and do not want to buy it.
- You are clearing brush, hedges or fruit-tree prunings and want a fast, clean result.

It does not make sense for a handful of twigs you can bag, or for material full of dirt or metal. If a storm left a big limb across a roof, fence or driveway, that is a [tree emergency](/emergency-tree-service-boise/) first and a chipping job second.

If you are already booking a trimming or removal crew, ask for the chips to be left, hauled or split.
