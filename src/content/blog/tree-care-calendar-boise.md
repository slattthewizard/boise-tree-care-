Most tree care in the Treasure Valley is not urgent, which is exactly why it doesn't get done. Nothing forces the issue until a limb comes down or a tree turns yellow, and by then the cheap window has closed.

This is the year laid out in order: what to do when, what genuinely has to happen in a specific month, and what you can do any time you get around to it.

## The Quick Answer

| Season | Priority work |
|---|---|
| **Dec – Feb** | Structural pruning, removals, inspect from the ground while bare |
| **Feb – early Mar** | Fruit tree pruning, dormant oil, last chance before bud break |
| **Mar – Apr** | Planting window, mulch refresh, start irrigation, watch for frost damage |
| **May – Jun** | Pest watch, fruit thinning, deep watering begins in earnest |
| **Jul – Aug** | Watering is the whole job. No major pruning |
| **Sep – Oct** | Best planting window, deep watering continues, storm prep |
| **Nov** | Final deep watering before ground freeze, trunk protection |

Two windows genuinely matter and can't be moved: dormant pruning before bud break, and the final deep watering before the ground freezes. Everything else has some flexibility.

## Winter: December, January, February

Winter is when the most valuable tree work happens, and almost nobody thinks about trees in January.

**Structural pruning.** With leaves off, the architecture is visible: crossing limbs, codominant stems, cracks, decay pockets, deadwood. Cuts made in the dormant season also seal quickly once growth resumes in spring. [Crown thinning, raising, and reduction](/blog/crown-thinning-raising-reduction/) work is best done now, and [how often trees should be trimmed](/blog/how-often-should-trees-be-trimmed/) covers what interval your species needs.

**Removals.** Winter is often easier for removals: frozen ground carries equipment with less lawn damage, and crews are less booked than in summer.

**Ground-level inspection.** Walk your trees. Look for fungal conks at the base, bark that's missing or sounding hollow, cracks, and any lean that looks new. [Winter tree care in Boise](/blog/winter-tree-care-boise/) covers cold-season specifics.

**Snow and ice load.** Brush heavy wet snow off small ornamentals and young trees with an upward motion. Do not knock ice off branches, which snaps them. If a large limb fails onto a structure, our [emergency line](/emergency-tree-service-boise/) runs 24/7.

**Watch for sunscald.** South and west-facing trunks on young thin-barked trees crack from winter sun heating the bark in daytime and refreezing at night. [Frost cracks and sunscald](/blog/frost-cracks-sunscald-trees/) covers protection.

**Vole damage.** Check trunk bases beneath snow for chewed bark. [Deer and vole protection for young trees](/blog/deer-vole-protection-young-trees/).

## Late Winter: February into Early March

The single tightest window in the year.

**Fruit tree pruning.** Apples and pears get pruned now, before buds swell. Peaches wait a little later. [Fruit tree pruning in Boise](/blog/fruit-tree-pruning-boise/) covers timing by species and how much to take, at **$100 to $400** per tree.

**Finish dormant pruning on everything else.** Once buds break, the window closes for the year on most structural work.

**Plan the year's projects.** Removals, plantings, and any cabling work identified over winter. [Cabling and bracing](/blog/tree-cabling-bracing-boise/) at **$200 to $900** per system is best installed before the spring wind season, not after it.

## Spring: March, April, May

**Planting.** Spring is a good planting window, though fall is generally better here. [The best time to plant trees in Boise](/blog/best-time-plant-trees-boise/) covers the tradeoff, and [the best trees to plant in Boise](/blog/best-trees-to-plant-boise/) covers what actually thrives in our alkaline soil.

**Mulch.** Refresh to two to four inches in a wide ring, pulled back from the trunk. Never piled against the bark. [How to mulch around trees](/blog/how-to-mulch-around-trees/).

**Start irrigation deliberately.** Turning on the lawn system is not the same as watering trees. [How to water trees in Boise](/blog/how-to-water-trees-boise/) covers the difference, and it's the highest-impact thing most homeowners get wrong.

**Watch for late frost damage.** Late spring frosts hit new growth and fruit blossoms in this valley most years. Damage looks alarming and trees usually push new growth.

**Pest and disease watch begins.** [Fire blight](/blog/fire-blight-treatment-boise/) shows on apples and pears as blackened shepherd's-crook tips shortly after bloom. [Codling moth](/blog/codling-moth-fruit-trees-boise/) timing starts now for anyone growing apples.

**Bird nesting season.** Active nests constrain when work can happen. [Bird nesting season and tree removal in Idaho](/blog/bird-nesting-season-tree-removal-idaho/) covers what that means for scheduling non-urgent work.

**Check staking on last year's plantings.** Most stakes should come off after a year. [Tree staking](/blog/tree-staking-new-trees/).

## Early Summer: June

**Thin the fruit.** A few weeks after bloom, thin fruitlets to roughly a hand's width apart. This is what produces full-sized fruit and prevents limbs breaking under load later.

**Pest monitoring.** [Japanese beetle](/blog/japanese-beetle-boise-trees/), [emerald ash borer](/blog/emerald-ash-borer-idaho/), and the general run of [Boise tree pests](/blog/tree-pests-boise/) become visible now.

**Look for chlorosis.** Yellow leaves with green veins on new growth means iron is unavailable in our alkaline soil. [Leaves turning yellow in summer](/blog/tree-leaves-turning-yellow-summer/) covers diagnosis.

**Light pruning only.** Deadwood and hazards yes; major structural work no.

## High Summer: July, August

Watering is essentially the entire job.

**Deep, infrequent watering.** Established trees want water delivered slowly, well out toward the drip line, deep enough to reach the root zone. Frequent shallow lawn irrigation trains roots to stay near the surface, which is what makes trees fail in the next drought. [Drought stress in Boise trees](/blog/drought-stress-trees-boise/) covers what stress looks like before it's terminal.

**Don't fertilize a stressed tree.** [Fertilizing trees in Boise](/blog/fertilize-trees-boise/) is for a diagnosed deficiency, not a general tonic.

**Avoid major pruning.** Removing significant canopy in peak heat exposes bark to intense sun and stresses a tree already working hard.

**Watch for summer branch drop.** Large horizontal limbs occasionally fail on hot, still afternoons with no wind. If you have long lateral limbs over a driveway or patio, this is the season they'd choose.

**Storm response.** Summer thunderstorms bring the valley's sharpest wind gusts. Keep the [windstorm tree damage checklist](/blog/windstorm-tree-damage-checklist/) handy, and be alert for [storm chaser scams](/blog/tree-service-storm-chaser-scams-boise/) afterward.

## Fall: September, October

The best season for most tree work in the Treasure Valley.

**Planting.** Fall planting lets roots establish in warm soil through autumn without the tree having to support a full canopy in summer heat. It's the better window here.

**Keep watering.** Trees need water well into fall. Shutting irrigation off the day the weather cools is a common and costly mistake.

**Storm preparation.** Before winter, deal with the deadwood, the overextended limbs, and the defects identified over summer. Preventive work at **$150 to $800** for deadwood removal is dramatically cheaper than emergency response at **$1,500 to $4,000+** when a limb comes through a roof.

**Assess.** If a tree struggled through summer, now is the time to find out why, while symptoms are still visible. A [tree health assessment](/blog/tree-health-assessment-boise/) runs **$75 to $250**.

**Leaf cleanup with judgment.** Clearing leaves off lawns is fine. Leaving some leaf litter in mulch rings is good for the tree.

## Late Fall: November

**The final deep watering.** Before the ground freezes, water everything deeply, especially evergreens and anything planted in the last three years. Winter desiccation, not cold, is what kills a lot of young trees here, and they can't take up water once the soil is frozen. This is the second window that genuinely can't be moved.

**Trunk protection.** Wrap or shade young thin-barked trunks on south and west exposures against sunscald.

**Rodent guards.** Before snow arrives.

**Drain and blow out irrigation**, and check that tree bubblers and drip lines will still be where you left them in spring.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When is the best time to prune trees in Boise?

The dormant season, roughly December through early March, for structural pruning on most species. The tree's architecture is visible without leaves and cuts seal quickly once spring growth begins. Dead, damaged, and diseased wood can be removed any time of year.

### When should I stop watering trees in the fall in Boise?

Not when the weather cools. Keep watering into fall, and give everything a deep final watering in November before the ground freezes. Winter desiccation is a major cause of loss in young trees and evergreens here, because they continue losing moisture through winter but can't take up more from frozen soil.

### Is it better to plant trees in spring or fall in Boise?

Fall is generally better in the Treasure Valley, because roots establish in still-warm soil without the tree having to support a full canopy through summer heat. Spring planting works too, but requires more attentive watering through the first summer.

### Can trees be pruned in summer in Idaho?

Light pruning yes, major structural pruning no. Removing significant canopy during peak heat exposes previously shaded bark to intense sun and adds stress when the tree is already working hardest. Hazardous and dead wood is the exception and should come out whenever it's found.

### What is the most important tree care task in Boise?

Watering correctly, by a wide margin. Deep, infrequent watering out at the drip line rather than frequent shallow lawn irrigation. More Treasure Valley tree problems trace back to watering pattern than to pests, disease, and pruning combined.
