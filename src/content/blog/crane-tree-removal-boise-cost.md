Crane tree removal in Boise runs **$5,000 to $8,000 or more**, the top tier on the pricing table for trees 80 feet and taller or in genuinely complex spots. That sounds steep next to a standard large-tree removal at $2,500 to $5,000. But for a specific kind of tree, tall, wedged against a house, with no room to drop a section, the crane bill can land lower than what a crew would have to charge for taking the same tree down by hand and rope alone.

This is the part most homeowners never see quoted side by side: crane cost against rigging cost, for the same tree. Once you see both numbers, the crane starts looking less like an upcharge and more like the efficient option.

## The Quick Answer

| Removal Method | Typical Cost | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Standard removal (ground crew, rigging) | $2,500 – $5,000+ | Large trees (60 ft+) with a clear drop zone |
| Crane-assisted removal | $5,000 – $8,000+ | Trees 80 ft+, tight access, or hanging over a structure |
| Stump grinding add-on | $150 – $500 | Either method, once the tree is on the ground |

A crane isn't automatically the pricier call. It's the right call when hand-rigging would stretch a job from hours into days, and days of labor add up fast on a tree this size.

## Why Crane Removal Costs $5,000 to $8,000+

The extreme tier applies to trees 80 feet or taller, or any removal complex enough to need mechanical lifting instead of ground-based rigging. That's above the $2,500 to $5,000+ range for a large tree (60 feet and up) removed the standard way, and well above the $300 to $5,000+ headline range that covers most Boise removals overall.

The crane fee reflects real, fixed costs: mobilizing the crane truck to the property, the crane operator's time, and a ground crew still needed to cut, sling, and haul every section the crane sets down. None of that is optional. A crane doesn't replace the crew, it changes how the crew works.

What it buys is speed. A crane lifts a 2,000-pound section of trunk clear of a roofline in one controlled motion. Doing the same thing by hand means notching the cut, running a rope through a rigging point, and lowering that section inches at a time while a second person controls the line from the ground. Multiply that by every section of an 80-foot tree and the hours start to tell a different story.

## The Hidden Cost of a Long Rigging Job

Rigging-only removal isn't cheap labor just because it skips the crane fee. On a tall tree with no drop zone, a climber has to make dozens of individual cuts, and each one gets lowered on rope rather than dropped. That's a slow, physically demanding process, and it's billed by the hour with a full crew standing by the whole time.

For a tree in an open yard, that's manageable in a single day. For a tree pinned between a house, a fence, and a neighbor's shed, with every piece needing a controlled lower instead of a free drop, the same removal can stretch to two or three days. Add the disposal hauling for wood that has to be carried out piece by piece instead of dropped and chipped on site, and a job quoted at $2,500 to $5,000 for a standard large tree can quietly climb past that range before it's done, without ever touching a crane.

## When the Crane Becomes the Cheaper Option

Here's the comparison that matters: crane cost is mostly fixed once the crane is on site, while rigging cost scales with every extra hour and every extra day. On the right tree, a one-day crane job can undercut a two- or three-day rigging job on total labor alone, even after the crane fee is added in.

**A scenario from the North End of Boise:** A homeowner had a roughly 90-foot ponderosa pine standing a few feet off the corner of the house, with a garage on one side and a neighbor's fence on the other. A hand-rigging plan meant lowering every section on rope through a gap barely wider than the trunk, an estimated three full days of crew time running close to $7,000 in labor before disposal. The crew brought in a crane instead, lifted the tree out over the roofline in sections in a single day, and the total came in at roughly $6,400, less than the rigging-only estimate and with less time spent working directly above the house.

**A scenario near Eagle:** A large cottonwood stood over a pool and a narrow side yard with essentially no room to drop branches without hitting the pool deck or fence. A pure rigging approach was quoted at close to $6,800 spread across two days of careful, piece-by-piece lowering. A crane lift finished the same tree in one day for about $5,900, and kept every cut section off the pool deck entirely instead of lowering it inches away on rope.

Neither of those trees was near an Idaho Power distribution line, which matters, because we never work directly on the utility's lines. [Idaho Power](https://www.idahopower.com) handles all vegetation touching its distribution lines, and any tree with branches actually in contact with one needs to go through them first, crane or not.

## What Drives the Price Inside the Crane Tier

Not every crane job lands at the same number within the $5,000 to $8,000+ range. A few things move it:

Crane size and reach matter first. A tree close to the street might only need a smaller crane parked curbside, while a tree deep in a backyard can require a larger crane with a longer boom to reach over a roofline, which costs more to bring in and operate.

Site access for the crane truck itself is next. A driveway wide enough for the truck keeps the job simple. A crane that has to set up in the street, potentially closing a lane, can add a permit step through the [City of Boise](https://www.cityofboise.org) if the tree sits in the public right-of-way. That's on top of the street-tree permit rules that already apply to many boulevard and parkway trees in Boise, worth checking before any large removal near the curb.

Ground crew size and disposal also factor in. A crane moves the wood, but someone still has to buck it, chip the brush, and haul everything off site. Larger trees simply generate more material to deal with, which shows up in the final number.

## Signs Your Tree Needs a Crane, Not Just Rope

A few situations point toward crane-tier pricing rather than a standard large-tree removal:

The tree is 80 feet or taller, or it's a hazard tree with no safe climbing points because the wood is dead or hollow. If a trunk sounds hollow when tapped, that changes the entire removal plan, our guide on [hollow trees and whether they're a safe or a hazard](/blog/hollow-tree-safe-or-hazard/) covers how that gets assessed.

There's no clear drop zone: the tree overhangs a house, a garage, a pool, or a neighbor's property, and every section has to be lowered rather than dropped.

Access is tight enough that a rigging plan would take multiple days of crew time rather than one. If you're weighing whether a job like this is something to tackle yourself, it isn't. Our piece on [DIY tree removal versus hiring a professional](/blog/diy-tree-removal-vs-professional/) lays out exactly why. As a rule, any chainsaw work above shoulder height, any cut near a power line, any climbing, and any tree that could reach a structure on the way down is a call to make to a professional, not a weekend project.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does crane tree removal cost in Boise?
Crane-assisted removal typically runs $5,000 to $8,000 or more, depending on the tree's height, the crane size needed, and site access. That's the top tier of the pricing table, reserved for trees 80 feet and taller or genuinely complex removals where ground-based rigging isn't practical.

### Why would a crane ever be cheaper than a regular tree removal?
Rigging-only removal bills by the hour, and a tall tree with no drop zone can take a crew two or three full days of careful, piece-by-piece lowering. A crane lifts whole sections in one motion, often finishing the same tree in a single day, so the labor savings can offset the crane fee itself.

### Does a crane removal need a permit in Boise?
It can, particularly if the crane truck needs to set up in the street or the tree sits in the public right-of-way, since Boise has a street-tree permit regime for boulevard and parkway trees. Our guide on [tree removal permits in Boise](/blog/tree-removal-permits-boise/) covers when that applies.

### Can a crane be used near power lines?
A crane can work near power lines, but the crew never touches vegetation actually in contact with an active distribution line, that work belongs to Idaho Power. Our article on [trees near power lines in Boise](/blog/tree-near-power-lines-boise/) explains the boundary between what a tree crew handles and what the utility handles.

### How long does a crane tree removal take?
Most single-tree crane jobs finish in a single day once the crane and crew are on site, compared to two or three days for the equivalent tree removed by hand-rigging alone. Larger, more complex trees or difficult access can extend that, but it's still usually faster than the rigging-only alternative.
