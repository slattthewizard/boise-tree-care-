Whether you need a tree removal permit in Boise depends almost entirely on one thing: where the tree is standing. A tree in your back yard and a tree twelve feet away in the strip between your sidewalk and the curb are governed by completely different rules, even though you mow around both of them and probably think of both as yours.

Get that distinction wrong and you can end up cutting down a city tree, which is the version of this that comes with a bill.

## The Quick Answer

| Where the tree stands | Permit needed? |
|---|---|
| Public right-of-way / street tree | **Yes.** Approval from Boise Community Forestry |
| Private yard, City of Boise | Generally **no city permit**, but check the exceptions below |
| Private yard inside an HOA | HOA approval is a separate requirement |
| Within a recorded easement | Utility or agency approval may be required |
| Part of a subdivision landscape requirement | Often restricted by the development approval |
| Meridian, Nampa, Caldwell, Eagle, Garden City | Each city runs its own rules |
| Touching a power distribution line | Idaho Power's work, not yours and not ours |

**Do not assume removal is permit-free because it's your property.** The right-of-way boundary, easements, and covenants all sit on top of ownership, and none of them are visible from your lawn.

## The Street Tree Problem

The most common and most expensive mistake in Boise is assuming that the tree in front of your house is your tree because you have always watered it.

Trees in the public right-of-way, most often in the planting strip between the sidewalk and the curb, are regulated by the city even though the adjacent homeowner typically handles routine care. Removing or substantially pruning one requires going through **Boise Community Forestry** first. Our guide to [street trees in Boise and who owns them](/blog/street-trees-boise-who-owns/) covers how to work out where the right-of-way line actually falls on your lot, which is rarely where people assume.

If the tree is dead or hazardous, that's still a call to the city rather than a reason to skip the process. Community Forestry runs free inspections, and [Boise's dead tree nuisance code](/blog/dead-tree-nuisance-code-boise/) covers the 7-2-12 nuisance definition and what happens after an inspection. You can reach Community Forestry at **(208) 608-7700** or use the city's [tree inspection request form](https://www.cityofboise.org/departments/parks-and-recreation/community-forestry/public-trees/request-a-tree-inspection/).

A legitimate tree service will ask where the tree sits before quoting it. A crew that offers to take down a street tree without any mention of the city is a crew you don't want on your property, which is one of the patterns covered in [storm chaser scams](/blog/tree-service-storm-chaser-scams-boise/).

## Private Property: Usually Simpler, With Four Exceptions

For a tree standing well inside your own lot in the City of Boise, removal generally does not require a city tree permit. That's the ordinary case and it covers most of the work we do.

The exceptions are what catch people.

**1. HOA and subdivision covenants.** These are private agreements, entirely separate from city rules, and they are frequently stricter. Many Treasure Valley subdivisions require written approval before removing anything above a certain size, and some require replacement planting. [HOA tree removal rules in the Treasure Valley](/blog/hoa-tree-removal-rules-treasure-valley/) covers how those approvals usually work and what happens if you skip one. The city not caring does not mean your HOA doesn't.

**2. Easements.** Utility, drainage, and access easements can run through a yard invisibly. Removing a tree inside one is usually fine, but the equipment access, stump grinding, and any digging are where the constraints bite. [Utility easement tree removal in Idaho](/blog/utility-easement-tree-removal-idaho/) covers what to check on your plat first.

**3. Development and landscape conditions.** Newer subdivisions often had landscape requirements attached to their approval, including specific trees that were required to be planted and maintained. Those obligations can follow the lot.

**4. Rentals and shared property.** If you don't own the property outright, authority matters more than opinion. [Landlord and tenant tree responsibility in Idaho](/blog/landlord-tenant-tree-responsibility-idaho/) covers who can authorize what.

## Boundary Trees and Neighbors

A tree whose trunk straddles a property line is a different legal animal from a tree that merely leans over one, and Idaho treats them differently. Overhanging branches and encroaching roots come with well-established rights and limits, covered in [Idaho law on trimming a neighbor's tree](/blog/neighbor-tree-trimming-rights-idaho/), and the short version is that your rights stop at the property line and do not extend to killing the tree.

Removing a shared tree without the co-owner's agreement is where this gets expensive. If it comes to a dispute, [what happens when a tree falls on a fence](/blog/tree-fell-on-fence-responsibility-idaho/) covers the liability side of neighbor tree conflicts.

## Power Lines Are Not a Permit Question

If a tree is touching or growing into a power distribution line, this is not something a permit resolves and not something a homeowner or a private crew should be working around. Idaho Power handles vegetation on distribution lines. Call them, don't climb it, and keep everyone away from the area. [Trees near power lines in Boise](/blog/tree-near-power-lines-boise/) covers the specifics, including the emergency number for a downed line.

## Before the Grinder: Call 811

Permits govern what comes down. Utility locates govern what happens in the ground afterward.

Stump grinding, root excavation, and planting a replacement all involve digging, and Idaho's 811 service arranges free utility locates before you break ground. This is a separate obligation from any tree permit, it's free, and it applies to homeowners doing their own work as much as to contractors. Skipping it is how people hit a gas line with a rented grinder.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a permit to cut down a tree on my own property in Boise?

Generally not a city tree permit, if the tree stands well inside your lot. But HOA covenants, recorded easements, and subdivision landscape conditions can each require approval independently of the city, and a tree in the public right-of-way is regulated regardless of who has been watering it.

### How do I know if my tree is a street tree?

Check your plat or property survey for the right-of-way line rather than relying on the sidewalk or the fence, since the right-of-way often extends several feet beyond the sidewalk onto what looks like your lawn. Boise Community Forestry at **(208) 608-7700** can confirm it, and our guide to [street trees in Boise](/blog/street-trees-boise-who-owns/) walks through how to read the boundary.

### What happens if I remove a street tree without approval?

It's treated as damage to public property, and the city can pursue the value of the tree, which is assessed rather than estimated and is often far higher than homeowners expect. Given that a legitimate removal quote is a known number and the penalty isn't, it's not a good gamble.

### Does a dead tree still need a permit to remove in Boise?

If it's in the right-of-way, yes, though the process is straightforward and Community Forestry inspections are free. Dead and hazardous trees are handled under the city's nuisance provisions rather than waived, so the answer is to start the process rather than skip it.

### Do Meridian and Nampa have the same tree rules as Boise?

No. Each city in the Treasure Valley runs its own street tree program and code compliance office, so the rules that apply are the ones in the city where the tree stands. Confirm with that city before scheduling work.
