# Siberian Elm: Why the Treasure Valley Is Full of Them, and Whether to Keep Yours

If you have a fast-growing tree with small, rough, double-toothed leaves and a yard full of little elm seedlings every spring, you probably have a Siberian elm. It is one of the most common trees in older Boise, Meridian, and Nampa neighborhoods, and one of the most argued about, because it grows almost anywhere with almost no help and then causes almost every kind of maintenance headache a tree can cause.

Here is why the species took over so much of the valley, the specific damage it does, what it actually has to do with Dutch elm disease, and how to decide whether yours is a keeper.

## What makes a Siberian elm a Siberian elm

Siberian elm (Ulmus pumila) has small, oval leaves, usually 1 to 2 inches long, with a saw-toothed edge and a rough, sandpapery feel on top. Bark is gray and furrowed, and mature trees often split into several leaning trunks instead of one straight one. The giveaway in spring is the seed drop: thousands of small, round, papery seeds called samaras cover the ground, driveways, gutters, and pool covers before the leaves are even fully out. People sometimes call it "Chinese elm," but the true Chinese elm (Ulmus parvifolia) flowers and seeds in fall, has flaky, mottled bark, and is a much better-behaved tree. If your elm is dumping seed in April or May, it is Siberian.

## Why so many Treasure Valley yards have one

Siberian elm was planted across the West for decades because it does something few trees can do here: survive our alkaline, often clay or compacted soils, shrug off drought, and grow several feet a year with no irrigation once established. In a semi-arid climate with hot summers and cold winters, that made it an easy, cheap choice for fast shade on new lots. Very few of the Siberian elms in the valley today were actually planted on purpose, though. The tree seeds so heavily that most of what you see now grew from a seed that blew or was dropped by a bird from someone else's tree decades ago, not from a nursery purchase. It is no longer recommended for planting, and it shows up on our own list of [worst trees to plant in Boise](/blog/worst-trees-to-plant-boise/) for exactly the reasons below.

## The trade-off: fast growth means weak wood

The same growth rate that made Siberian elm popular is also its biggest liability. Wood that goes up fast does not have time to build the dense grain that resists wind, and Siberian elm's wood is genuinely brittle, prone to breaking at the crotch where a large limb meets the trunk. In a summer thunderstorm or a windstorm rolling off the foothills, a mature Siberian elm is far more likely to drop a limb, or split down the middle, than most other shade trees in the same yard. That risk goes up with age and with any prior storm damage the tree has already absorbed.

## Elm seed bugs and elm leaf beetles: the insects that come with it

Two insects follow Siberian elm into Treasure Valley yards.

**Elm seed bugs** are the one homeowners notice first. According to University of Idaho Extension, elm seed bugs invade homes in July and August, moving in from nearby elm trees where they feed on the seeds. They are about a third of an inch long, dark brown with a reddish, backward-pointing marking, and they show up by the hundreds around doors, windows, and attic vents looking for a place to overwinter. They do not bite, sting, or damage the house, and they do not hurt the tree either. The fix is sealing entry points and cleaning up seed litter near the foundation, not treating the tree.

**Elm leaf beetles** are more of a tree problem. Per the Pacific Northwest Pest Management Handbooks, adult beetles chew round holes in leaves while the larvae skeletonize them, eating everything but the veins, which can turn large portions of the canopy brown by midsummer. Left unchecked over several seasons, repeated defoliation weakens the tree and makes it more attractive to elm bark beetles, the insect that actually spreads Dutch elm disease between trees. ## Volunteer seedlings everywhere: why they keep coming back

A single mature Siberian elm can drop an enormous number of seeds in a few weeks each spring, and a large share of them sprout. That is why you find thin, whippy elm seedlings coming up in gravel beds, along fence lines, in cracks next to the driveway, and in flower beds that were weeded a month ago. Cut one down without treating the stump and it often sends up a thicket of root suckers instead of dying, the same strategy elms use after storm damage. ## Siberian elm and Dutch elm disease: a different story than American elm

This is where Siberian elm actually gets some credit. Dutch elm disease is a fungal disease spread by elm bark beetles that has killed enormous numbers of elms across North America, and it has a documented history right here: Pacific Northwest Pest Management Handbooks record diseased elms first turning up in the region in Boise in 1968. American elm, the tall, vase-shaped shade tree common along older streets and in older Boise neighborhoods, is highly susceptible to Dutch elm disease and can be killed by it. Siberian elm, by contrast, has real resistance to the disease. It is not why the tree was planted here originally, but it is one of the only genuine advantages it has over an American elm growing in the same yard.

That resistance does not mean a Siberian elm is disease-free. It still gets other fungal and bacterial problems, and a weakened, beetle-infested elm of any species is more vulnerable across the board. If your tree is dropping leaves out of season, has dying limbs scattered through an otherwise healthy canopy, or has bark that is splitting and peeling in patches, treat that as a signal to get it looked at rather than assuming Dutch elm disease resistance covers everything. ## Keep it or remove it: how to decide

There is no single right answer here. Run through these questions:

- **Where is it?** A Siberian elm over a driveway, roof, or play area carries more risk than one out in a side yard, simply because of what is under it if a limb comes down.
- **How big and how old is it?** Older, larger specimens have had more time to accumulate weak crotches and storm damage, and they are more expensive to safely take down later if you wait.
- **Is it your only shade?** On a lot with little other canopy, a mature elm's shade may be worth managing around rather than losing outright, at least until a replacement tree is established.
- **How bad is the seedling problem?** If you are pulling dozens of volunteers every spring out of beds, turf, and gravel, that is a real, recurring cost even if the tree itself looks fine.
- **Has it already dropped wood?** A tree that has already lost a large limb in a storm is telling you something about the rest of its structure.

## Controlling seedlings if you decide to keep it

Keeping the tree does not mean living with an endless carpet of seedlings. Pull volunteers while they are young and the roots are shallow, ideally within a few weeks of the spring seed drop, before they develop a taproot that makes hand-pulling harder. A layer of mulch over bare soil and beds cuts down on how many seeds actually make contact with soil and germinate. Raking up seed litter before it has a chance to sprout, rather than letting it sit through a wet spring week, also helps. If you removed a different elm previously and it is now suckering from the stump or roots, grinding the stump is usually what finally stops it; see [stump grinding versus stump removal](/blog/stump-grinding-vs-stump-removal-boise/) for how that decision works.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a Siberian elm the same tree as an American elm?

No. They are different species. American elm is the tall, classic vase-shaped shade tree that lines many older streets and is highly susceptible to Dutch elm disease. Siberian elm is smaller-leaved, faster growing, has weaker wood, and has real resistance to Dutch elm disease, though it brings its own set of problems with seeding and storm damage.

### Do Siberian elms get Dutch elm disease?

Siberian elm has documented resistance to Dutch elm disease, unlike American elm, which is highly susceptible. That resistance does not make it immune to every tree disease or pest, and a stressed or beetle-damaged elm of any species is more vulnerable overall.

### Are elm seed bugs harmful to my house or my health?

No. University of Idaho Extension describes elm seed bugs as a nuisance pest that invades homes in July and August looking for a place to overwinter. They do not bite, sting, damage wood, or spread disease. Sealing gaps around windows, doors, and vents, plus cleaning up seed litter near the foundation, is the usual fix.

### Why does my yard keep growing new baby elm trees every spring?

A mature Siberian elm produces an extremely large number of seeds each spring, and a large share of them sprout wherever they land, including gravel, mulch beds, and cracks in pavement. Pulling seedlings early, before the roots establish, and mulching bare soil are the main ways to keep the number down.

It depends mainly on the tree's height and how much access the crew has. ### My Siberian elm has branches near the power line. Can someone trim just that part?
