# How to Prune a Tree Properly, and When to Stop and Call Someone

A homeowner on the Boise Bench spent one Saturday with a rented pole saw and took roughly a third of the canopy off a 30-year-old maple. Two summers later the tree was covered in weak, whippy shoots, the bark on the south side of the trunk had split from sudden sun exposure, and the corrective work quoted at around $850. The original goal was simply more light on the lawn.

Knowing how to prune a tree is less about technique than most people expect. It comes down to three questions: why you are cutting, exactly where the cut goes, and whether you can reach the branch with both feet on the ground. Get those right and pruning is one of the best things you can do for a tree. Get them wrong and the damage is permanent, because a tree does not heal a bad cut, it just seals around it and lives with it.

This is a plain guide for Treasure Valley homeowners: the cuts that work, the timing that suits our climate, and the honest line where a job stops being a weekend project.

## Start With the Reason, Not the Saw

There are only four good reasons to prune an established tree.

**Safety.** Dead, broken, cracked or hanging limbs, especially anything over a roof, driveway, patio or play area. This is the one reason that justifies pruning at any time of year.

**Clearance.** Branches touching siding or shingles, blocking a walkway, hanging into a driveway, or crowding a fence line.

**Health.** Branches rubbing each other raw, crossing through the middle of the canopy, or carrying visible disease.

**Structure.** On young trees, choosing which limbs become the permanent framework. Ten minutes of pruning on a five-year-old tree prevents a much bigger problem at twenty-five years.

"It looks too big" is not on that list. Size is usually a planting decision made decades ago, and cutting a tree back to a size it does not want to be starts a fight the tree wins.

One number matters more than any other. Never remove more than about 25 percent of the living canopy in a single season, and on a mature tree stay closer to 10 or 15 percent. Older trees have less stored energy to rebuild with, and stripping them triggers a flush of weak sprouts that are worse than what you removed.

## How to Prune a Tree: Where the Cut Goes

Almost every pruning mistake is a cut in the wrong place, not on the wrong branch.

Look at where a branch joins the trunk. There is a slightly swollen ring of bark and wood there, called the branch collar. That collar holds the tissue the tree uses to seal the wound. Your cut goes just outside it, leaving the collar completely intact.

Two failures come out of ignoring that ring. A flush cut, sliced tight against the trunk, removes the collar and leaves a wound the tree cannot close, which is an open door for decay. A stub cut, left several inches long, gives the tree nothing to seal against, so the stub dies back and rot travels inward.

For any branch thicker than about an inch and a half, use three cuts instead of one:

1. An undercut roughly 12 to 18 inches out from the trunk, about a quarter of the way through from below.
2. A top cut an inch or two further out, all the way through, so the limb falls away.
3. A final clean cut just outside the branch collar, on the short stub that is left.

Skipping the undercut is how homeowners tear a two-foot strip of bark down the trunk when the weight of the limb peels it away mid-cut. That strip does not grow back.

When you are shortening a branch rather than removing it, cut back to a side branch that is at least a third the diameter of the one you are cutting. That side branch takes over as the new leader. Cut back to nothing and you have topped the tree, which is the single most damaging thing done to trees in this valley. There is more on that in our guide to [why topping ruins a tree](/blog/tree-topping-bad-boise/).

Two small things finish the job. Do not paint or seal the wound, because wound dressings trap moisture and slow the tree's own sealing process, and the International Society of Arboriculture has advised against them for years in its [homeowner pruning guidance](https://www.treesaregood.org/treeowner/pruningyourtrees). And keep the blade sharp, since a torn cut seals far slower than a clean one.

## When to Prune Trees in the Treasure Valley

For most shade trees here, late winter is the window. Roughly January through early March, while the tree is dormant and before buds break, is ideal. The structure is easy to read with the leaves off, wounds sit open for the shortest time, and the spring growth flush closes them quickly.

Dead wood is the exception. Dead, broken or hanging limbs come out whenever you find them, in any month, because they are a hazard and the tree has already written them off.

Avoid heavy pruning in late summer and early fall. Cuts made then push new growth that has no time to harden before the first hard freeze, and our valley can drop below freezing in October.

A few species need extra care. Elms and oaks should not take large cuts during the growing season, because fresh wounds attract the insects that spread disease between trees. Apple, pear, crabapple and hawthorn are fire blight hosts, so prune them dormant, and if you are cutting out an active infection, cut 8 to 12 inches below the visible damage and disinfect the blade between every cut. Fruit trees have their own rhythm, covered in [fruit tree pruning for Boise yards](/blog/fruit-tree-pruning-boise/). The University of Idaho Extension [forestry program](https://www.uidaho.edu/extension/forestry) publishes region-specific guidance if you want to go deeper on species.

Two local rules catch people out. Between roughly April and July, check large limbs for active bird nests before you cut, since nesting birds are federally protected. And if the tree stands in the strip between the sidewalk and the street, the City of Boise treats it as a street tree with its own permit regime, explained in our post on [tree permits in Boise](/blog/tree-removal-permits-boise/). Do not assume a tree in front of your house is yours to cut.

## When to Stop and Call Someone

This is the part that matters most, so it is blunt.

**Stop if you would need a ladder and a chainsaw at the same time.** A chainsaw kicks back, and a ladder gives you nowhere to go when it does. This combination causes more serious homeowner tree injuries than anything else on this list. Professional crews work from a rope and harness or a lift for exactly that reason.

**Stop if the branch is over something that costs money.** A roof, a fence, a vehicle, a shed, a neighbor's yard. A limb does not fall straight down, it swings on the hinge of wood you have not cut through yet. Putting it where you want it takes rigging, not luck.

**Stop if the tree is anywhere near a power line.** Do not prune it, do not let a general handyman prune it, and do not judge the clearance from the ground. Idaho Power handles vegetation on its distribution lines, so call them and let their crews deal with it.

**Stop if a limb is already hanging, split or under tension after a storm.** Loaded wood releases suddenly and in a direction you cannot predict. That is a same-day call, not a Saturday project.

**Stop if you cannot see the cut clearly from where you are standing.** Guessing where the branch collar is from twelve feet below is exactly how flush cuts happen.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much of a tree can I prune at once?

Keep it under 25 percent of the living canopy in one season, and on a mature tree stay closer to 10 or 15 percent. Older trees have less stored energy to recover with, and heavy cutting triggers a flush of weak, fast-growing shoots. If a tree needs more work than that, spread it over two or three seasons instead of forcing it in one visit.

### When is the best time to prune trees in Boise?

Late winter, roughly January to early March, suits most shade trees here. The tree is dormant, the structure is visible without leaves, and wounds close quickly once spring growth starts. Dead or hanging limbs are the exception and should come out whenever you spot them, in any month.

### Should I paint or seal a pruning cut?

No. Wound dressings and pruning paints trap moisture against the wound and can slow the tree's natural sealing process. A clean cut made just outside the branch collar seals itself. Ask an arborist first if you are dealing with a specific disease, since a few situations are handled differently.

Small ornamentals under 25 feet typically run $150 to $400, trees between 25 and 50 feet run $350 to $900, and large trees over 50 feet run $800 to $2,000 or more.
