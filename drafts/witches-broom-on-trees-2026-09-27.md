# Witches' Broom on Trees: Those Twiggy Clusters in Your Hackberry

If you have a hackberry in your yard or along your street, there's a good chance you've noticed tight, tangled knots of small twigs bunched up in the branches, especially in winter when the leaves are gone. That's witches' broom, and on hackberry it's almost always a mite and a fungus living together, not a sign your tree is dying.

Witches' broom is a general term for any dense cluster of shoots that grows from a single point on a branch, named for its resemblance to an old straw broom. Several different causes produce the same look, which is why the right response depends on what kind of tree you have and where the clusters are.

This guide covers what's causing the brooms in your hackberry, when the same symptom on a different tree means something more serious, and when pruning the clusters out is worth the trouble.

## What Witches' Broom Actually Looks Like

Witches' broom shows up as a ball or fan of thin, short twigs crowded together, usually 6 inches to a couple feet across, sometimes growing right out of a swollen knot on the branch. A tree can carry one broom or dozens. They're easiest to spot in late fall and winter once the canopy thins out, and on hackberry they tend to cluster in the upper crown.

A few things distinguish a true witches' broom from other tree oddities:

- The twigs inside a broom are shorter and denser than normal growth, often with smaller leaves.
- Brooms usually persist year after year and get bushier over time rather than fading.
- They grow from one point, unlike storm damage or a general flush of water sprouts spread across a branch.

## Why Boise Hackberries Get Witches' Broom

Hackberry is one of the most common shade and [street trees](/blog/street-trees-boise-who-owns/) in the Treasure Valley, and hackberry witches' broom is close to universal on mature trees here. According to Iowa State University Extension, the condition is caused by the combined activity of a tiny eriophyid mite and a powdery mildew fungus that both colonize the same bud [Iowa State University Extension](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/article/1995/6-23-1995/hack.html). The mite overwinters inside the bud, the fungus grows alongside it, and together they trigger the bud to sprout a tight ball of stunted twigs instead of a normal branch. Neither organism does much damage on its own. It's the pairing that produces the broom, which is why you rarely see the same clusters on a hackberry's neighbors, like elm or ash, growing in the same yard. ## Is Witches' Broom Actually Hurting Your Tree?

For hackberry, usually not much. Iowa State's extension entomologists describe the disorder as one that "seldom seriously injures trees," even on specimens carrying a heavy load of brooms. The bigger issue is cosmetic: a hackberry thick with brooms can look ragged, especially bare in winter, and some homeowners just don't like the look.

Two situations are worth paying closer attention to. First, a branch loaded with several dense brooms is carrying more weight in one spot than it was built for, and that wood can break more easily in wind or under snow load. Second, a tree with brooms scattered across most of its branches, especially alongside sparse leaf-out or dieback at the tips, may be dealing with more than just the mite and mildew complex. That combination is worth a second look rather than an assumption.

If your hackberry has a handful of brooms and otherwise looks full and healthy, you're looking at a cosmetic quirk, not a problem to solve. ## Other Trees, Other Causes

Hackberry's broom is the one you'll see most often in Boise, but the same bunched-twig look shows up on other species for different reasons, and those reasons matter for how you respond.

On ash, lilac, and a handful of other trees, witches' broom can be caused by a phytoplasma, a bacteria-like organism that lives in the tree's sap-conducting tissue rather than in a bud. University of Maryland Extension notes that phytoplasma damage tends to spread through the tree over time, and pruning out individual brooms doesn't stop it once the organism has moved beyond that branch [University of Maryland Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/witches-broom-trees). That same source also lists genetic mutation and general stress or injury as occasional, usually minor, causes of the same symptom on other species.

If you spot broom-like clusters on a tree that isn't a hackberry, especially one showing yellowing leaves or dieback elsewhere in the canopy, it's worth having someone look at the whole tree rather than assuming it's the harmless hackberry version. ## Witches' Broom on Conifers Is a Different Problem

If the dense clusters are on a pine, spruce, or Douglas-fir rather than a broadleaf tree, you're most likely looking at dwarf mistletoe, not a mite and mildew complex. Dwarf mistletoe is a parasitic plant that taps into a conifer's branch and triggers the same kind of abnormal, bushy growth, according to Colorado State Forest Service, which notes the parasite mainly targets ponderosa and lodgepole pine but can also infect Douglas-fir and other conifers [Colorado State Forest Service](https://csfs.colostate.edu/forest-management/common-forest-insects-diseases/dwarf-mistletoe/).

Unlike hackberry witches' broom, dwarf mistletoe is a real threat to the tree. It spreads slowly from branch to branch and tree to tree, and heavy, long-standing infections can reduce growth, weaken the crown, and eventually kill the tree. If you have a mature pine or Douglas-fir with broom-like clusters, that's a different call than a hackberry: it's worth an in-person look rather than a wait-and-see approach. ## Should You Prune Out a Witches' Broom?

It depends on the cause and what you're trying to fix.

| Cause | Typical host | How serious | Does pruning fix it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eriophyid mite + powdery mildew | Hackberry | Mostly cosmetic | Improves appearance; new brooms can still form elsewhere |
| Phytoplasma | Ash, lilac, others | Can spread through the tree over years | Limited once it's beyond one branch |
| Dwarf mistletoe | Pines, Douglas-fir, other conifers | Can weaken or kill over time | Only if the infected branch is removed, not just the visible tuft |
| Stress or old injury | Various | Usually minor | Rarely necessary |

For a hackberry, pruning out brooms is purely an appearance choice. Iowa State's extension guidance is blunt about it: there's no treatment that prevents the disorder, so cutting brooms out where you can reach them safely cleans up the look, but new ones can form on other branches in following years. If a broom-heavy limb is genuinely weak and hangs over a driveway, patio, or roof, that's a structural question worth having answered rather than a cosmetic one.

Picture a mature hackberry over a detached garage, with three or four fist-sized brooms clustered on one upper limb. The brooms themselves aren't the concern there; whether that particular limb is sound enough to carry snow load is. That's the kind of thing better judged in person than from a description.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will witches' broom kill my hackberry tree?

No, not on its own. Iowa State University Extension describes hackberry witches' broom as a condition that seldom seriously injures the tree, even when a large number of brooms are present. The main downside is appearance and, in heavy cases, a bit of extra weight on affected branches.

### Can I just leave the witches' broom clusters alone?

Yes, in most cases. If your hackberry looks full and healthy aside from the brooms, there's no health reason to remove them. Leaving them alone is a completely reasonable choice; pruning is about looks, not tree survival.

### Does hackberry witches' broom spread to other kinds of trees in my yard?

No. The mite and powdery mildew combination that causes hackberry's broom is specific to hackberry. It won't jump to your maple, ash, or pine, even if those trees are growing right next to an affected hackberry.

### Is witches' broom the same thing as mistletoe?

Not on hackberry. Hackberry's brooms come from a mite and fungus pair, while mistletoe is a separate parasitic plant that causes broom-like growth mainly on conifers like pine and Douglas-fir. Both produce a similar bushy cluster, but they're different organisms with different levels of risk to the tree.

### Can I tell hackberry witches' broom apart from a more serious problem myself?
