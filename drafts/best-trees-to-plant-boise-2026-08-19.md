# Best Trees to Plant in Boise: Species That Handle Our Soil and Climate

The best trees to plant in Boise are the ones that tolerate alkaline soil, because that's what kills young trees here far more often than cold does. Our valley soils run high pH, high pH locks up iron, and a tree that can't get iron spends a decade slowly yellowing while its owner waters it more and wonders what's wrong.

Pick for soil chemistry first, then for water, then for the thing you actually wanted, whether that's shade, screening, or spring flowers. Do it in that order and the tree mostly takes care of itself.

The single most useful filter: if a species is known for chlorosis in high-pH soil, it will be chlorotic here too.

## Why Alkaline Soil Is the Whole Story

Most Treasure Valley soils are alkaline, often meaningfully so. That matters because iron becomes chemically unavailable to roots as pH climbs, even when there's plenty of iron in the ground.

The symptom is chlorosis: leaves that go pale yellow while the veins stay green, worst on new growth at branch tips, progressing over years until the tree is thin, weak, and prone to everything else. It's the most common tree problem we see in Boise, and it is very often mistaken for underwatering, disease, or a fertilizer deficiency. Our guide to [leaves turning yellow in summer](/blog/tree-leaves-turning-yellow-summer/) covers how to tell chlorosis from the alternatives.

You can treat chlorosis with chelated iron, and a [tree health assessment](/blog/tree-health-assessment-boise/) at **$75 to $250** will tell you whether that's what you're dealing with. But treatment is an annual expense forever. Planting a species that doesn't need it is free.

Add two more constraints and you have the local picture: we're a high desert with limited rainfall, so anything planted here depends on irrigation for its first several years and many species need it permanently. And we get real winter cold plus intense summer sun, which is hard on thin-barked young trees.

## Large Shade Trees

These are the 40-foot-plus trees that make a yard livable in a Boise July. Give them room, which means checking [planting distance from property lines and fences](/blog/planting-distance-property-line-fence/) before you dig.

**Bur oak.** The most reliable large shade tree for this valley. Genuinely alkaline-tolerant, drought-tolerant once established, structurally sound, and long-lived. It's slow, which is the only argument against it, and it's the argument people regret listening to twenty years later.

**Chinkapin oak.** Another oak that handles high pH well, somewhat faster than bur oak, with a rounder habit and good fall color.

**Hackberry.** Underrated and extremely tough. Handles alkaline soil, drought, wind, and heat without complaint. Not a glamorous tree, but very few things kill it.

**Kentucky coffeetree.** Tolerant of alkaline soil and dry conditions, coarse and architectural in winter, and unusually free of pest problems here. Seedless male cultivars avoid the pods.

**Thornless honeylocust.** Widely planted for good reason, since it handles our conditions and casts light dappled shade that lawn will grow under. The caution is overplanting: a street of nothing but honeylocust is one pest problem away from being a street of nothing.

## Medium Shade and Street Trees

**Japanese pagoda tree.** Also called scholar tree. Handles heat, drought, and alkaline soil, and flowers in late summer when nothing else does.

**Littleleaf linden.** Dense, symmetrical, reliable, and tolerant of urban conditions. Needs more water than the oaks.

**Disease-resistant elm hybrids.** Modern selections bred for Dutch elm disease resistance bring back the classic vase-shaped street tree without the historical liability. Note this means named disease-resistant cultivars, not the volunteer Siberian elms that seed themselves into every fence line in the valley.

**Ginkgo.** Slow, tough, essentially pest-free, spectacular in fall. Plant a male cultivar unless you want the fruit, which you don't.

## Small and Ornamental Trees

For a front yard, a patio, under power lines, or a courtyard, where the mature size needs to stay modest.

**Japanese tree lilac.** One of the best small trees for this region. Alkaline-tolerant, cold-hardy, tops out around 20 to 25 feet, and carries big creamy flower panicles in early summer.

**Hot Wings tatarian maple.** Selected in the Intermountain West specifically for these conditions, so it doesn't get the chlorosis that plagues other maples here. Red samaras in summer are the show.

**Thornless hawthorn.** Winter King and similar selections give spring flowers, persistent red fruit into winter, and good tolerance of our soil.

**Disease-resistant crabapple.** Spring flowers and winter fruit for birds. Choose modern scab-resistant cultivars; older ones defoliate by August. Note that crabapples are in the same family as pears and apples, so [fire blight](/blog/fire-blight-treatment-boise/) is worth understanding before planting one.

**Eastern redbud.** Early spring color before anything else leafs out. Give it some afternoon shelter from the worst western sun.

## Evergreens

For screening, windbreaks, and winter structure. If screening is the whole goal, also read [fast-growing privacy trees for Boise](/blog/fast-growing-privacy-trees-boise/), and for agricultural-scale rows see [windbreak trees for the Treasure Valley](/blog/windbreak-trees-treasure-valley/).

**Rocky Mountain juniper.** Native, drought-tolerant, alkaline-tolerant, and largely indifferent to our weather.

**Ponderosa pine.** A foothills native, handles heat and drought, and gets large. Give it space.

**Austrian pine.** Tolerant and widely planted, though watch for needle disease in dense plantings and give it air circulation.

A note on **blue spruce**: it's everywhere here and it struggles more than its popularity suggests, particularly with needle cast diseases and heat stress on hot exposures. It isn't a mistake, but it isn't the automatic choice people treat it as. [Pine tree problems in Boise](/blog/pine-tree-problems-boise/) covers what tends to go wrong with our conifers.

## Fruit Trees

The Treasure Valley genuinely is good fruit country, with the caveat that fruit trees are a commitment rather than a decoration. They need annual pruning at **$100 to $400** per tree and a pest management routine, or you get a tree full of wormy fruit and disease.

Apples, pears, cherries, peaches, plums, and apricots all grow here. The two things to plan for before planting: [codling moth](/blog/codling-moth-fruit-trees-boise/), which is what puts worms in apples and pears, and [fire blight](/blog/fire-blight-treatment-boise/), which kills apple and pear branches from the tips back. Both are manageable, neither is optional. Fruit tree pruning in Boise covers the annual work.

Late spring frosts also matter here, and an apricot in a low spot will lose its crop more years than not.

## What Not to Plant

The full list is in [worst trees to plant in Boise](/blog/worst-trees-to-plant-boise/), but the alkaline-soil casualties deserve naming here because they're sold locally anyway:

- **Pin oak.** Notoriously chlorotic in high-pH soil. It will be yellow its entire life.
- **Red maple and silver maple.** Chlorosis-prone, and silver maple adds weak wood and aggressive surface roots.
- **Birch.** Wants consistently moist, cool, acidic soil, which is the opposite of what we have, and stressed birches attract bronze birch borer.
- **Ash.** Not a soil problem but a pest one. [Emerald ash borer](/blog/emerald-ash-borer-idaho/) has changed the calculation on planting new ash.
- **Lombardy poplar.** Fast, short-lived, disease-prone, and a future removal bill.
- **Siberian elm.** Seeds everywhere, weak wood, constant cleanup.
- **Cottonwood near buildings.** Fine along a ditch bank, a poor choice near a house. See [cottonwood removal](/blog/cottonwood-tree-removal-boise/).

## Getting a New Tree Established

The species choice is half of it. The other half is the first three years.

**Plant at the right time.** [The best time to plant trees in Boise](/blog/best-time-plant-trees-boise/) covers the fall and spring windows.

**Plant at the right depth.** The root flare should be visible at the surface. Planting too deep is the most common installation error and it kills trees slowly enough that nobody connects the cause.

**Water deeply and infrequently.** Lawn sprinklers do not water trees properly. [How to water trees in Boise](/blog/how-to-water-trees-boise/) covers what a young tree actually needs.

**Mulch correctly.** A wide, flat ring, never piled against the trunk. [How to mulch around trees](/blog/how-to-mulch-around-trees/).

**Stake only if necessary, and remove it.** [Tree staking](/blog/tree-staking-new-trees/).

**Protect the trunk.** Young thin-barked trees get [frost cracks and sunscald](/blog/frost-cracks-sunscald-trees/) on south and west exposures, and [deer and voles](/blog/deer-vole-protection-young-trees/) do their own damage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best shade tree to plant in Boise?

Bur oak, for durability and alkaline tolerance, if you can accept slow growth. For faster shade, hackberry and Kentucky coffeetree both handle our soil and drought well. Thornless honeylocust is the common fast choice and works, though the valley is already heavily planted with it.

### Why do trees turn yellow in Boise even when I water them?

Usually iron chlorosis from alkaline soil rather than a water problem. High pH makes soil iron unavailable to roots, producing yellow leaves with green veins, worst on new growth. Adding water doesn't help and often makes things worse. Chelated iron treats it; planting a tolerant species avoids it.

### What trees should I avoid planting in Boise?

Pin oak, red maple, silver maple, and birch all struggle badly in our alkaline soil. Ash faces emerald ash borer pressure. Lombardy poplar and Siberian elm are short-lived and problematic. Cottonwood belongs along waterways, not near buildings.

### What are the best low-water trees for the Treasure Valley?

Bur oak, hackberry, Kentucky coffeetree, Rocky Mountain juniper, and ponderosa pine all handle dry conditions once established. "Once established" means three years of consistent deep watering first, which every tree needs regardless of how drought-tolerant it becomes later.

### Can I plant a tree where I just removed one?

Yes, with care about the old root mass and grinding depth. [What to plant after a tree removal](/blog/what-to-plant-after-tree-removal/) covers site preparation, and offsetting the new tree a few feet from the old trunk position is usually easier than trying to plant into a grind hole full of chips.
