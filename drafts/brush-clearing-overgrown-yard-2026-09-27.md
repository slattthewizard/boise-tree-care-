# Brush Clearing and Overgrown Yard Cleanup: What Gets Cut, Chipped and Hauled

Brush clearing is the work of cutting back or removing dense growth, volunteer saplings, and tangled undergrowth from a yard or lot so the ground is usable again. A crew cuts the material, runs what it can through a chipper, and hauls off whatever is left, stumps included if you want them gone. It is different from a single tree removal: you are paying for volume and labor across an area, not one trunk.

If you just bought a place with a back corner nobody has touched in years, or you have a fence line thick with scrub that showed up on its own, this is usually the job you need. The price depends mostly on how much material there is and how hard it is to reach, not on any one plant.

## What Counts as Brush Clearing (and What Doesn't)

Brush clearing covers small-diameter growth: saplings, suckers, shrubs, vines, and thickets that formed without anyone planting them. It is not the same as removing one established shade tree, which is priced and scheduled as a standalone tree removal. On a lot with both, a crew usually handles the brush first to get access, then deals with any larger trees separately.

A rough line: if you can cut it with loppers or a hand saw, it is brush. If it needs a chainsaw and a plan for where it falls, it is tree work, even if it is growing in the middle of an overgrown mess.

## What a Crew Actually Does on Site

Most brush clearing jobs follow the same sequence:

1. **Walk the site** and mark anything you want kept, like an ornamental you planted that is just hidden by weeds.
2. **Cut** the brush, saplings, and small trees at or near ground level.
3. **Chip** what the chipper can handle, usually material under about 6 inches in diameter, on site.
4. **Haul off** anything too large to chip, plus the chips themselves if you do not want a pile left behind.
5. **Grind stumps** if you are having small stumps left from the brush taken out too, rather than left to resprout.

Wood chips can sometimes stay on site as mulch if you want them; ask before the crew loads out, since hauling everything away usually costs more than leaving chips on the ground.

Small stumps left behind by brush and volunteer saplings are usually a separate line item from the main clearing work. Grinding a stump under 12 inches across typically runs $75 to $150, and it climbs from there with diameter. If you have several small stumps scattered across the same lot, it is worth asking about a volume discount rather than paying the per-stump rate on each one. ## The Volunteer Growth Treasure Valley Yards Tend to Grow

A lot that has sat unmanaged for a season or two in the Boise area usually is not growing one thing. It is a mix, and a few species show up again and again along fence lines, ditch banks, and neglected back corners here:

- **Siberian elm**: a fast-growing tree that tolerates poor soil and drought and [drops huge quantities of seed that sprout readily](https://extension.usu.edu/forestry/resources/forest-products/ask-an-expert/siberian-elm-tree-id), turning up as thickets of unwanted saplings in disturbed ground.
- **Russian olive**: a [thorny, invasive tree that thrives in southern Idaho's dry climate](https://www.uidaho.edu/extension/publications/bul-0976) and crowds the banks of ditches and watercourses; a single cut rarely kills it, so it tends to resprout unless the stump or roots are treated or ground out.
- **Blackberry and wild rose canes**, various weedy shrubs, and grass and weed growth tall enough to hide fence lines and irrigation risers.

Removing volunteer brush like this on your own residential lot is not the kind of work Boise's tree permit rules are built around; those apply mainly to street trees and specific protected situations. If you are not sure whether something on your property falls under that, our guide on [when a tree removal permit is actually required in Boise](/blog/tree-removal-permits-boise/) walks through it.

## What Drives the Price

Brush clearing is priced by volume and access more than by species. A crew is weighing:

- How much ground is covered and how dense the growth is
- Whether a truck and chipper can get close, or everything has to be carried out by hand
- Whether stumps need to be ground or just cut flush
- What has to happen to the debris: chipped and left, chipped and hauled, or hauled whole

## When Brush Turns Into Real Tree Work

It is common to start a brush clearing conversation and find a few trees in the mix that are past the "cut it with a saw" stage. Common triggers for that upgrade:

- A volunteer tree has grown past 15-20 feet and has real trunk diameter
- Something is leaning into a fence, shed, or power line right-of-way
- A stump from a larger tree needs grinding, not just the small brush stumps

For a tree at that size, pricing shifts to our standard [tree removal](/tree-removal-boise/) rates rather than brush clearing rates. Under 30 feet typically runs $300 to $800, and 30 to 60 feet runs $800 to $2,500, depending on access and what is around it. If the tree is anywhere near a power line, do not let anyone touch it, including us; call Idaho Power and stay clear.

## DIY vs. Calling a Crew

A few dozen small saplings along a fence line, cut with hand tools over a weekend, is a reasonable DIY project if you are comfortable with a chainsaw and have somewhere to haul the debris. Where it usually stops being a weekend job:

- The area is large enough that hauling debris becomes its own project
- Thorny growth like Russian olive covers ground you actually need to walk through
- There is poison ivy, wasp nests, or unstable footing mixed into the brush
- Anything in the mix is tall enough or heavy enough to fall unpredictably when cut

A crew with a chipper turns a multi-weekend hauling job into a single-day cut-chip-haul cycle, which is usually where the cost makes sense even for a job you could technically do yourself. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does brush clearing cost in the Boise area?

It depends entirely on volume and access, so we do not publish a flat brush clearing rate. A small fence-line job is a different price than an acre of neglected lot. Call and describe what you have, or ask for a free on-site estimate so we can give you an actual number.

### Will cutting brush like Russian olive kill it, or will it grow back?

Cutting alone usually is not enough. [Research on Russian olive control](https://www.uidaho.edu/extension/publications/pnw-0755) shows the species regrows readily from cut stumps and roots, so a crew will typically grind the stump or root ball on anything you want gone for good rather than just cutting it flush.

### Can I leave the wood chips on my property instead of hauling them away?

Usually yes, and it can lower the cost since hauling is part of what you are paying for. Ask about this before the job starts so the crew knows to leave chips as mulch instead of loading them out.

### Do I need a permit to clear brush and small volunteer trees on my own property?

Generally no for ordinary brush and volunteer saplings on private residential land; Boise's permit rules are built around street trees and specific protected situations, not unmanaged brush. If a tree on the lot looks established rather than volunteer growth, check our permit guide before removing it.

### Is brush clearing the same service as lot clearing for new construction?

They overlap but are not identical. Brush clearing is usually about reclaiming an existing yard or lot for regular use. Lot clearing for new construction is a larger-scale version of the same cut-chip-haul work, often with grading and permit considerations layered on top.

### What is included if I hire a crew instead of doing it myself?

Cutting the growth, chipping what can be chipped on site, hauling off the rest, and stump grinding if you want small stumps removed rather than left to resprout. You are also paying for someone else to deal with the debris hauling and disposal, which is usually the most time-consuming part of a DIY job.
