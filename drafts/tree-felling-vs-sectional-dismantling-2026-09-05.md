# Tree Felling vs Sectional Dismantling: Why Access Changes the Price

Two silver maples of about the same size, both close to 50 feet tall, sitting two miles apart. One came down for around $1,000. The other ran close to $2,400. Same species, same height, same number of people on the crew.

The difference was fourteen feet of side yard and a gate too narrow for a chipper. That is the whole story of tree felling versus sectional dismantling, and it is the reason two quotes on what looks like the same tree can be a thousand dollars apart.

Tree felling means dropping the tree in one piece. Sectional dismantling means taking it apart from the top down and lowering the pieces on ropes. Height sets the rough price tier. Access decides where inside that tier your tree lands, and sometimes pushes it into the next one.

## What Tree Felling Actually Means

Felling is the method most people picture. A cutter makes a wedge-shaped notch on the side facing the direction of fall, then a single back cut from the opposite side, leaving a strip of uncut wood called the hinge. The hinge steers the tree as it goes over. The whole trunk lands in one piece and gets cut up on the ground.

It is fast. A clean fell on a medium tree can be over in a few minutes, and most of the day's labor goes into cutting up and hauling wood rather than into getting the tree down.

It also needs room. A safe drop zone is at least as long as the tree is tall, with margin on both sides for a lean that is not quite what you measured from the driveway. A 55-foot tree wants roughly 55 feet of clear ground in the fall direction, free of the house, the fence, the shed, and the neighbor's greenhouse.

In the Treasure Valley that usually means acreage. Open ground outside Nampa and Caldwell, an unbuilt lot on a new construction site, a windbreak row along a field edge. On a standard Boise city lot with a garage, a fence line, and a neighbor 20 feet away, the room simply is not there.

## Sectional Dismantling: Taking the Tree Down in Pieces

When there is no drop zone, the tree comes apart instead. A climber ties in and works up the stem, or the crew uses a lift where the yard allows one, and the tree is removed from the top down in manageable pieces.

Most of those pieces cannot be dropped either. They get rigged, which means tied to a rope that runs through a pulley set higher in the tree and back down to someone on the ground who controls the descent. Each piece is cut, caught by the rope, lowered to a small landing spot, untied, and dragged clear.

That is where the money goes. A limb that would take two seconds to drop takes several minutes to rig and lower. Repeat that 40 or 60 times on a mature maple and the day fills up on its own.

Everything downstream slows too. If the chipper cannot get through the gate, every branch is hand carried to the street. If the trunk sections cannot be rolled out, they get cut smaller than the crew would like and carried out in armloads.

## Why Access Changes the Price More Than Height Does

Height puts your tree in a price band. Access is what moves you around inside it. Removal in the Treasure Valley runs roughly $300 to $800 for a small tree under 30 feet, $800 to $2,500 for a medium tree of 30 to 60 feet, and $2,500 to $5,000 or more once you are past 60 feet. Those are typical ranges, not quotes, and the real number depends on site conditions. You can work the variables through the [tree removal cost calculator](/cost-calculator/).

Here is what an estimator is actually measuring while they walk your property.

**Gate and side yard width.** A chipper needs about a 36-inch gate at minimum, and plenty of older North End and Boise Bench properties do not have one. No chipper access means hand carrying, and that is the most common reason a quote lands higher than the homeowner expected.

**Where the truck can park.** A crew that backs a chip truck to within 20 feet of the tree works faster than a crew hauling brush 150 feet down a driveway.

**The landing area.** A patio, a pool, a septic lid, a raised bed, or an irrigation ditch all shrink the space where pieces can safely come down, which means smaller cuts and more of them.

**Overhead wires.** Any service line running to the house forces slower work. Anything touching an Idaho Power distribution line is not our work at all, and you should call [Idaho Power](https://www.idahopower.com) before anyone climbs.

**Ground conditions.** A soft, wet, or sloped yard can rule out a lift entirely and put the whole job on a climber, which costs hours.

## When Felling Is Off the Table No Matter What

Some trees get dismantled even on a wide open lot.

Dead or decayed wood is the first reason. A hinge holds only if the fibers are sound. Where the trunk is hollow, cracked, or soft, there is nothing reliable to steer the fall, and the tree can sit back on the saw or twist off the stump. [Hazardous tree removal](/blog/hazardous-tree-removal-boise/) covers the warning signs worth photographing before anyone quotes.

A heavy lean the wrong way is the second. A tree can be pulled or wedged a modest amount off its natural lean, but there are limits, and testing them next to a house is not a gamble worth taking.

Street trees are the third. Boise operates a street-tree permit regime, and the tree in the strip between the sidewalk and the curb is often not yours to remove on your own decision. Read [tree removal permits in Boise](/blog/tree-removal-permits-boise/) before you schedule anything near the curb.

Contact with a power line is the last, and it is not a judgment call. Idaho Power handles vegetation on its distribution lines, and no private crew should be cutting there.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is tree felling cheaper than sectional dismantling?

Yes, when the site allows it. Felling puts a tree near the bottom of its size range because most of the day goes to cleanup rather than to climbing and rigging. Sectional dismantling on the same tree usually lands in the middle to upper part of that range, and on a genuinely tight site it can push into the next tier. The real figure depends on your gate, your drop zone, and what sits under the branches.

### Can I fell a tree myself in my own back yard?

You can cut a tree on your own property in most cases, but a felling cut near a structure is exactly where homeowner tree work goes wrong. Hinges fail, leans get misread, and a saw pinched in a back cut leaves you with a half-cut tree you can neither finish nor walk away from. Street trees are a separate question and may need a permit. If the tree can reach a house, a fence, or a line, it is not a do-it-yourself job.

### Does a dead tree have to be taken down in sections?

Usually, yes. A felling cut relies on sound wood in the hinge to steer the fall, and dead or decayed fiber will not hold that load predictably. Crews normally dismantle standing dead trees in short pieces, which takes more hours than felling the same tree would have when it was alive. The longer a dead tree stands, the more its removal tends to cost.

### Does the removal method change the stump price?

No. Stump grinding is priced on the diameter of the stump and on how many stumps are ground during the same visit, not on how the tree above it came down. Added to a removal it typically runs $150 to $500, and volume discounts apply when several stumps are ground on one trip.
