# Arborvitae Turning Brown: Winter Burn, Drought or Something Worse

Most brown arborvitae in the Treasure Valley come down to one of five things: normal fall needle shed, winter windburn, summer drought stress, spider mites, or dog urine at the base. A smaller share point to a root or soil problem that has been building for years under a hedge row.

Where the brown shows up tells you which one you're dealing with. Brown needles tucked inside the plant while the outer growth stays green is usually nothing to worry about. Brown tips on one side of the row, brown patches low to the ground, or whole plants fading top to bottom are different stories, and they call for different fixes.

This guide walks through each cause, how to tell them apart, and the one pruning rule that decides whether a brown arborvitae ever fills back in. Juniper hedges in the valley run into the same problems for the same reasons, so most of this applies to those too.

## Is It Just Normal Fall Needle Drop?

Arborvitae hold their needles for a year or two, then shed the oldest ones. [Iowa State University Extension](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/why-inner-growth-my-arborvitae-turning-brown) notes that it's normal for evergreens, including arborvitae, to shed their oldest, innermost needles each fall, and that the drop happens uniformly from top to bottom while the tips stay green. If that's what you're seeing between late August and October, the plant is doing what it's supposed to do.

The tell is location. Normal shed happens on the inside of the plant, close to the trunk, on needles a year or more old. A real problem shows up on new growth, at the branch tips, or across an entire section rather than scattered evenly through the interior. ## Winter Burn and Wind Damage

Evergreens don't go fully dormant. Arborvitae keep losing small amounts of water through their foliage all winter, even in the cold. When the ground freezes, roots can't pull up replacement water, and dry winter wind combined with bright winter sun pulls moisture out of the needles faster than the plant can replace it. [Wisconsin Horticulture Extension](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/winter-burn/) describes this as a common evergreen problem in open, unprotected spots, worst on the south, southwest, and west sides of a plant where winter sun is most direct.

Boise's winters bring exactly that mix: dry air, wind events, and plenty of clear cold days. Arborvitae rows along a fence line or a foothills-facing slope, with no wind break, tend to scorch first. The damage usually doesn't show fully until late winter or early spring, well after the wind that caused it. See our [winter tree care guide](/blog/winter-tree-care-boise/) for how to protect evergreens before the cold sets in.

Whether burned tips recover depends on what's underneath them. If you scratch a small patch of bark on a browned branch and find green tissue, buds there can still break and fill in. If the tissue is brown all the way through, that branch is done and won't regrow, which is exactly why cutting technique matters (more on that below).

## Summer Heat and Drought Stress

The same plant that scorches in a dry winter can also struggle in a hot, dry summer. Arborvitae planted against a west-facing wall or a black vinyl fence deal with reflected heat on top of direct sun, and in our high-desert summers that combination dries out shallow roots fast, especially in the alkaline, often clay or compacted soils common around Treasure Valley subdivisions. Our [drought stress guide](/blog/drought-stress-trees-boise/) covers the warning signs in more depth, and our [guide to watering trees in Boise](/blog/how-to-water-trees-boise/) covers how much and how often.

A few things worth checking before you assume the worst:

- Drip lines shift or clog over a season; walk the row and confirm each plant is actually getting water.
- Sprinkler heads get blocked by the hedge's own growth as it fills out.
- New plantings and anything in its first two summers need more frequent, deeper watering than an established hedge.

Drought-stressed arborvitae usually brown from the tips inward and often on the side that gets the most afternoon sun, which is a different pattern than the uniform interior browning of normal fall shed.

## Dog Urine at the Base

If the brown is concentrated in one low section of the hedge, often near a gate, a corner, or wherever a dog spends time in the yard, urine is a common cause. The nitrogen and salts in urine burn foliage and roots right at ground level, killing that patch while the rest of the plant stays green above it. It's rarely the whole hedge, just the section closest to where the dog stands or lies down repeatedly. Flushing the area with water after the fact and fencing off access to the base of the hedge are the two most reliable ways to stop it from recurring.

## Spider Mites

Spider mites feed by piercing needles and sucking out the chlorophyll, which first shows up as fine yellow stippling and later as bronzing or outright browning, often starting on the interior growth. [Washington State University Extension](https://hortsense.cahnrs.wsu.edu/fact-sheet/arborvitae-spider-mites/) notes that these infestations tend to get worse in dry, dusty conditions, which describes a typical Boise summer well. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/spruce-spider-mite) lists arborvitae as one of the mite's regular hosts alongside juniper, spruce, and other conifers.

A simple check: hold a sheet of white paper under a suspect branch and strike the branch firmly. If you see tiny specks fall onto the paper and start crawling, that's mites, not just dust. A strong spray of water on the foliage every few days can knock populations down; for a hedge that's badly infested, our [tree pest guide](/blog/tree-pests-boise/) covers identification and next steps in more detail.

## Root and Soil Problems

Older hedge rows sometimes decline for reasons that have nothing to do with weather or bugs. Arborvitae planted too deep, set in heavily compacted clay along a driveway or walkway, or growing where drainage is poor can develop root systems that simply can't support the plant anymore. This kind of decline tends to be slow, affects one or two plants in a row before others, and doesn't respond to watering changes the way drought stress does. ## How to Trim Arborvitae the Right Way

This is the rule that matters most if you're trying to save a hedge: arborvitae only grow new foliage from green, actively growing wood. Unlike a maple or a lilac, they don't carry dormant buds under the bark of old, bare stems. Cut back into brown, leafless wood and that spot stays bare, permanently, no matter how much you water or fertilize afterward.

That means:

- Only cut where you can still see green foliage below the cut line.
- Avoid removing more than about a quarter to a third of a branch's foliage at once.
- Prune in late winter or early spring, just before new growth starts, rather than late summer or fall.
- If you're trying to fill in a winter-burned or thin section, light, repeated shaping over a couple of seasons works better than one hard cut.

If a hedge has been let go for years and the brown has crept well past the green line on most plants, trimming alone won't fix it. ## When a Hedge Row Is Past Saving

Some arborvitae rows are too far gone to bring back. Signs that removal, not more trimming, is the right call:

- More than half the row is brown from the base to the top, not just the tips or one side.
- Individual plants have bare, hollow centers with no green foliage anywhere on the branch.
- The row is decades old, was never replaced or thinned, and multiple plants are declining at the same time.
- What's left standing is more gap than hedge, and shaping it further won't hide that.

At that point the honest advice is to take the row out and start over rather than keep trimming a hedge that will never look full again. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will brown arborvitae branches turn green again?

Only if there's still green, living tissue under the brown. Scratch a small patch of bark; if it's green underneath, that branch can still push new growth. If the wood is brown all the way through with no green tissue, it's dead and won't recover no matter how you care for it afterward.

### Should I cut off the brown parts right away?

Not immediately after winter burn. Wait until new growth starts in spring so you can see which branches are actually recovering before you cut, since some tissue that looks bad in March can still leaf out. Once you do prune, cut only back to green growth, never into bare brown wood.

### Is arborvitae browning caused by a disease?

Usually not. Most browning in the Treasure Valley traces back to winter burn, drought stress, spider mites, dog urine, or a root problem, not a disease. True fungal or bacterial issues on arborvitae are less common here than the environmental causes above, but if branches are dying in a scattered pattern with no clear cause, it's worth having someone look at it in person.

### Can I save just a few plants in a hedge row instead of replacing the whole thing?

Often, yes, if the decline is limited to a plant or two rather than the whole row. Replacing individual plants in an otherwise healthy hedge is common and usually cheaper than starting over. The math changes once more than half the row is affected, since new plants will take years to catch up to their older neighbors in height.

### How do I stop winter burn from happening again next year?

Water arborvitae deeply through fall right up until the ground freezes, since well-hydrated plants going into winter handle wind and sun better. Where possible, plant new rows out of the harshest west and south wind exposure, or use a temporary windbreak in the first few winters until the roots are established.

### Are juniper hedges affected by the same problems?

Yes. Juniper and arborvitae are both conifers that handle winter and summer stress the same way, share the same spider mite species, and follow the same rule about only regrowing from green wood. If you're seeing brown juniper instead of arborvitae, the causes and fixes in this guide apply just as directly.
