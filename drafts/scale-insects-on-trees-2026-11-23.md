# Scale Insects on Trees: How to Identify Them and When Treatment Works

Scale insects are tiny sap-feeders that live under a bump-like cover on twigs, branches and leaves, so they rarely look like insects at all. If you can scrape a crusty bump off a branch and find a soft body under it, you have scale. Getting rid of scale on trees mostly comes down to timing: the mobile young stage, called crawlers, is the one window when sprays work well, and the rest of the year you are fighting a shell.

The good news is that scale is usually slow. Most trees carry some, and a light infestation on a healthy tree often needs nothing more than a closer look and a bit of patience.

## What scale insects look like

Scale hides in plain sight. Purdue Extension's [guide to scale insects on shade trees and shrubs](https://extension.entm.purdue.edu/publications/E-29/E-29.html) says infestations often go unnoticed until branches "mysteriously" die, and that yellowed leaves and bumpy twigs can be the first clue. Their trick for checking: flip a suspicious bump with your thumbnail. If there's a soft body underneath, it's scale. If it's just dry bark or a lichen patch, it isn't.

Adults barely move once they settle. The only mobile stage is the crawler, which Purdue describes as flattened, under 1/32 inch, and looking like dust on the plant. You will almost never spot one by eye, which is why timing a treatment by calendar or by sticky tape (more on that below) works better than looking.

## Soft scale vs armored scale

This split matters because the two groups behave differently, and so does the cleanup.

[University of Maryland Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/introduction-scale-insects) puts it this way: soft scale have a pliable wax coating attached to the body, feed on phloem sap, and produce honeydew. Armored scale have a stiffer, shell-like cover that isn't attached to the body, stay put once settled, feed on individual plant cells, and don't produce honeydew.

So a quick field rule:

- **Sticky drips, shiny leaves, black film below the tree:** think soft scale (or aphids).
- **Crusty gray or brown bumps, no stickiness, branches slowly thinning out:** think armored scale.

Armored scale is the sneakier one. With no honeydew to give it away, you often find it only after a branch has died back.

## Scale you might see on Treasure Valley trees

We're only going to name what the Utah State University sources list, since Utah's trees and climate are the closest match to ours.

[Utah State University Extension's page on oystershell scale](https://extension.usu.edu/planthealth/research/oyster-shell-scale) lists ash, aspen, cottonwood, lilac, maple, poplar and willow as common hosts in Utah. It's an armored scale: females are about 1/8 inch long and wear a shell that looks like a tiny oyster shell. If you have an aspen that is thinning or dying back in spots, our [quaking aspen guide](/blog/quaking-aspen-boise/) covers the other usual suspects before you blame scale.

For soft scales, [USU's soft scale page](https://extension.usu.edu/planthealth/ipm/notes_orn/list-treeshrubs/soft-scales) names elm, linden, maple, oak, honeysuckle and willow among the hosts, plus a few conifers like juniper and yew. If you have a maple or linden dripping sticky stuff in July, soft scale is one candidate. Aphids are the other, and the two get confused constantly. ## Honeydew and sooty mold

Soft scale excretes honeydew, a sugary liquid that coats whatever is underneath. Purdue notes it attracts ants, flies and wasps, and that black sooty mold grows on it and can shade leaves and reduce growth. Maryland Extension adds that sooty mold doesn't infect the plant itself, but it can shade the leaves and weaken the tree over time.

Practically: sooty mold is a symptom, not the disease. Wash the sticky mess off the car and patio, and deal with the insects making it. Cleaning the mold off leaves does nothing if the scale is still feeding.

## The crawler stage: when treatment actually works

Everything extension services say about control points to the same moment. Maryland Extension calls crawlers "the most vulnerable" stage. Once they settle and grow a cover, most sprays can't get through it.

Timing differs by species. For oystershell scale, USU says eggs overwinter under the old female's shell and hatch over about two weeks, starting in late May or early June in northern Utah. Utah's degree-day tool (the TRAPS model) predicts it, and USU suggests wrapping double-sided sticky tape around infested twigs starting in mid-May and checking every other day. When crawlers show up on the tape, that's your spray window. For soft scales, USU describes crawlers feeding on leaf undersides through summer, so the timing is later and varies by species.

If you want to track it yourself, sticky tape on an infested twig is cheap and it works. Crawlers are pale cream and nearly flat, and USU says you need a 20x hand lens to see them.

## How to get rid of scale on trees

Start with the least aggressive option that fits the size of the problem.

**Light infestation on a small tree or shrub.** Scrub infested limbs with a stiff brush or mesh sponge, which USU says removes adults and old waxy caps. Prune out a badly encrusted branch. Water the tree properly, because heavy scale usually means the tree was stressed first (see [how to water trees in Boise](/blog/how-to-water-trees-boise/)). Maryland also warns that extra fertilizer promotes soft growth that scale likes, so don't "feed it better" as a fix.

**Crawler sprays.** Extension sources list horticultural oil, insecticidal soap and other labeled products as crawler-stage options. Homeowners can apply products labeled for home use on their own plants, as long as they follow the label exactly. Skip anything not labeled for the tree or the pest, don't spray plants that are in bloom or being visited by bees (USU says so directly), and expect to repeat: USU suggests reapplying in 10 to 14 days if the label allows. One honest note: on a 40-foot tree, a garden sprayer won't reach the canopy, and that is where most people give up.

**Dormant oil.** It's useful for scale that overwinters on bark, but it's a different job than a crawler spray. USU says spring dormant oil gives only partial suppression of oystershell scale and is less effective than crawler sprays, especially on large trees. Our [dormant oil guide](/blog/tree-spraying-dormant-oil/) explains what it does and doesn't do and when the window is.

**Systemics.** USU lists soil-applied systemic options for hard scales but notes some cost a lot and some products (imidacloprid on oystershell scale) don't work for that species. That's the point where we'd say get a second opinion before buying anything.

Large infestations can take more than one season to knock down, per Maryland Extension. One spray rarely ends it.

## Let the natural enemies work

Scale has real predators. USU lists ladybird beetles, parasitic wasps and predatory mites for oystershell scale, and lady beetles and lacewing larvae for soft scales. Maryland adds that small holes in a scale cover mean that scale was parasitized and is dead.

The catch is that broad-spectrum insecticides kill the helpers along with the pest, and Purdue says that undermines long-term control. For a light infestation, doing less is often the better call.

## When scale damage actually matters

We'd worry in these situations:

- Branches are dying back or the canopy is thinning on one side, and you can find encrusted twigs.
- The bark is scaly or cracking on branches. USU says heavy oystershell infestations can cause dieback, decline or even tree death over several years, especially on small trees or shrubs.
- The tree is small, young or already stressed by drought or root problems.

We wouldn't worry about a few bumps on an otherwise vigorous mature tree, or about honeydew alone. It's a nuisance, not usually a threat to the tree. If branches are already dead, [tree trimming](/tree-trimming-boise/) to remove them and thin out the worst-infested wood helps, and an [ISA-certified arborist](/arborist-boise/) can tell you whether scale is the cause or just along for the ride.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between soft scale and armored scale?

Soft scale have a pliable wax coating, feed on sap, and produce sticky honeydew. Armored scale wear a harder shell-like cover that isn't attached to the body, feed on plant cells, and don't make honeydew. Soft scale tends to show itself through sticky drips and sooty mold, while armored scale often goes unnoticed until branches start dying back.

### What do scale insects on trees look like?

Small, immobile bumps on twigs, branches or leaf undersides, usually gray, brown or waxy depending on the species. Oystershell scale is about 1/8 inch long and shaped like a tiny oyster shell. If you flip a bump with your thumbnail and find a soft body under it, that's scale rather than bark or lichen.

### When is the best time to treat scale?

During the crawler stage, when the newly hatched young are mobile and have no protective cover. For oystershell scale in northern Utah that starts around late May or early June. Putting double-sided sticky tape on infested twigs lets you see exactly when crawlers appear. Repeat the application if the label allows.

### Will dormant oil get rid of scale?

It can help, but it's not a cure by itself. Dormant oil suffocates overwintering scale on bark, and USU says it gives only partial suppression of oystershell scale compared with crawler sprays. See our dormant oil guide for timing and when to skip it.

### Can scale kill a tree?

Rarely on a healthy, established tree. Heavy infestations can cause dieback and decline, and USU says oystershell scale can kill trees over several years, mostly small trees or shrubs. Stress makes it worse, so watering properly and fixing underlying problems matters as much as spraying.

### Why is my tree sticky and covered in black film?

That's usually honeydew from soft scale or aphids, with sooty mold growing on it. The mold is a symptom rather than the disease. Look at twigs and leaf undersides to find the insect, treat that, and wash surfaces below the tree to clear the stickiness.
