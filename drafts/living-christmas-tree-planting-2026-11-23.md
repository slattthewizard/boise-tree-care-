# Living Christmas Tree: How to Keep It Alive Indoors and Plant It in Boise

A living Christmas tree can survive the holidays and end up in your yard, but only if you treat the indoor stay as a short visit. Extension guidance generally says to keep a live tree inside for about a week to ten days, ease it in and out through an unheated garage or porch, and plant it as soon as the soil allows. In a Boise winter, the part that trips people up is the ground: if you haven't dug the hole before it freezes, you'll be waiting until spring.

Here's how to buy a tree that's worth planting, get it through the house, and put it in the ground where it will actually live.

## Before you buy: decide where it will go

A living tree is a landscape tree that happens to wear lights for a week. The ones sold for Christmas are usually spruces, firs, or pines that grow into big trees. [UNH Extension](https://extension.unh.edu/blog/2019/11/living-christmas-trees) points out that popular Christmas tree species can reach 30 to 50 feet, and tells buyers to check the mature height before planting. Dwarf Alberta spruce is its suggestion for tight spaces.

So before you pay, pick the spot. You want full sun for most conifers, soil that drains, and a lot of clearance. The Treasure Valley Tree Selection Guide lists mature sizes that make the point: Colorado spruce at 45-55 ft high and 25-35 ft wide, white fir at 35-45 ft by 15-25 ft, Serbian spruce at 50-60 ft by 20-25 ft. That's a big tree next to a house.

Don't plant it where it'll crowd the house, the fence, or overhead lines in 20 years. Our posts on [how far to plant a tree from a property line or fence](/blog/planting-distance-property-line-fence/) and [trees too close to a house](/blog/tree-too-close-to-house-boise/) cover the spacing in detail, and if there's a power line anywhere overhead, read [trees near power lines in Boise](/blog/tree-near-power-lines-boise/) first.

## Which species do well in the Treasure Valley, and which struggle

Boise's soil is the deciding factor more often than the cold. Our [best trees to plant in Boise](/blog/best-trees-to-plant-boise/) post explains why. The City of Boise's [Treasure Valley Tree Selection Guide](https://www.cityofboise.org/media/4078/tree-selection-guide_second-edition.pdf) is the most local source we found, and its conifer entries sort out like this:

- **Tolerates alkaline soil:** Serbian spruce, white spruce (described as doing very well in most Idaho landscapes), and Austrian pine.
- **Adaptable to most soil, with pest cautions:** Colorado spruce. The guide calls it a successful home landscape tree, and also lists spruce bud scale, spider mites, aphids, and cytospora canker, which can kill lower branches.
- **Prefers acidic soil:** Norway spruce prefers drained acidic soils, and Douglas fir is listed as not tolerant of alkaline soils.
- **Fussier about drainage:** White fir does well in well-drained soil and does poorly in clay.

We can't tell you what your yard's pH is. A soil test can, and it's cheap compared with a 40-foot tree. If you've got heavy clay or the soil is known to be alkaline where you are, a tolerant species is the safer bet. See [blue spruce dying from the bottom up](/blog/blue-spruce-dying-bottom-up/) if you want to know what the spruce problems above look like in practice.

## How to keep it alive indoors

The enemy here is warmth. A tree that's dormant in December will start waking up in a heated living room, and a tree that's woken up gets hurt when it goes back out into the cold. UNH Extension says longer exposure to indoor warmth can end winter dormancy, and recommends 7 to 10 days indoors.

A routine that works:

1. **Stage it first.** UNH suggests buying a week or two ahead and keeping the tree in an unheated space (shed, garage, porch) with the roots moist. It says this isn't strictly necessary, but it reduces stress. A garage that stays above freezing works for most Boise homes.
2. **Bring it in late.** Put it in the coolest spot you have, away from heat vents, fireplaces, and sunny south windows.
3. **Set it in a tub.** The National Christmas Tree Association (NCTA) advises wrapping the root ball in plastic or setting it in a tub while indoors, and keeping the ball slightly damp, not flooded.
4. **Use cool lights.** The NCTA says any lights shouldn't give off heat. LEDs are the usual answer.
5. **Take it back out slowly.** Before it goes outside, the NCTA says to let it sit in a sheltered area for several days so it isn't going from a warm house straight into freezing weather. The garage again.

Go easy on the water, though. The NCTA also notes that a flooded tree may not survive. Moist, not soaked.

## How heavy is the root ball?

Heavier than you think. The NCTA says a six-foot balled-and-burlapped tree can weigh as much as 250 pounds. Don't try to carry it alone, and don't lift it by the trunk. [Arbor Day Foundation](https://www.arborday.org/planting-your-tree/how-plant-balled-burlapped-trees) warns that lifting by the trunk can separate the root ball from the trunk, so move it by the ball (a hand truck, a tarp to drag it on, or a second person).

A potted tree is lighter, but the soil still weighs a lot when it's wet.

## Pre-dig the hole before the ground freezes

This is the step that decides whether the tree gets planted in January or in April. Digging frozen ground is slow and often not worth it.

Do it in late fall, before hard freezes set in:

- **Width:** Iowa State University Extension says to dig 2 to 3 times wider than the root ball, with sloped sides that are wider at the top than the bottom.
- **Depth:** The same Iowa State page says to dig 2 to 3 inches shallower than the root ball's height (about two-thirds the height if drainage is poor), so the top of the ball sits 2 to 3 inches above the surrounding soil. The Treasure Valley guide says the same depth the tree grew at in the nursery, with the top of the ball level with or slightly above the ground. Either way, the trunk flare should end up at or above grade, not buried.
- **Rough sides:** The Treasure Valley guide wants the sides rough and uneven so roots can push into the native soil.
- **Keep the soil:** Iowa State recommends backfilling with the original soil, with no compost or peat mixed in. Store that soil somewhere it won't freeze solid, like the garage (our suggestion, not theirs), or cover the pile.
- **Cover the hole.** The NCTA suggests mulching the planting spot to keep the ground from freezing. A sheet of plywood plus a thick layer of wood chips or leaves is a simple way to keep it workable. Mark it so nobody steps in.

Call before you dig if there's any chance of buried utilities. Idaho has a free 811 locate line.

## Planting day

When the tree comes out of the garage, and the ground is workable:

1. Carry it by the ball and set it in the hole so the top of the ball is level with or slightly above the surrounding soil.
2. Check it's straight from a couple of angles before you backfill.
3. Cut and remove twine. For burlap, the Treasure Valley guide says to cut it halfway down the root ball, and if the tree is in a wire basket to take it out completely if you can, or at least the upper half. Iowa State says to remove the burlap from the top third to half of the ball. Plastic or vinyl burlap should come off entirely. The NCTA and Arbor Day both say that.
4. Backfill with the original soil, firm gently, and build a small basin around the edge.
5. Water it in thoroughly.
6. Spread 2 to 4 inches of mulch (Arbor Day) and keep it about 4 inches off the trunk.

About staking: the Treasure Valley guide says you don't always need it and to stake only if it's windy, the ball is fractured, or the trunk isn't stable. The NCTA suggests staking through the first growing season. We'd go with the local guide: only if the tree needs it. Our post on [staking new trees](/blog/tree-staking-new-trees/) explains how to do it without strangling the trunk.

## What if the ground is frozen?

Sources differ, and it's worth being straight about that. The NCTA says to plant as soon as possible if the soil isn't frozen. UNH says not to plant right after the holidays even if you can dig, because the tree is unlikely to survive planting that late in the season, and to store it in an unheated structure, water periodically, and plant in spring once the soil has thawed and dried.

For Boise, our view is to split the difference. If the hole is pre-dug and the soil is workable within a week or two after you take it outside, plant. If you're looking at a hard freeze, keep it in a sheltered garage or on the north side of the house out of the wind, mulch the ball heavily, check it for moisture, and plant in spring. Our [best time to plant trees in Boise](/blog/best-time-plant-trees-boise/) post explains why late fall and spring are the windows and why midwinter planting is a gamble.

## Watering and winter care

Don't let the ball dry out in the garage, and don't drown it. Check it every week or so. Once it's in the ground, the winter rules are different from summer: the soil needs to be moist before it freezes, but frozen ground doesn't take up water. Our guides on [how to water trees in Boise](/blog/how-to-water-trees-boise/) and [winter tree care in Boise](/blog/winter-tree-care-boise/) cover the schedule, including evergreen browning from winter drying. For a newly planted conifer in the Treasure Valley, plan on watering through its first couple of growing seasons. The Treasure Valley guide says watering regularly during the first couple of years is critical to establishment.

## What to do with a cut tree afterward

A cut tree has no roots and can't be replanted. The City of Boise's [Christmas tree page](https://www.cityofboise.org/departments/public-works/curb-it/curb-it-sorting-guide/compost/christmas-trees/) says residents with a compost cart can put a live (not artificial) tree in it after removing all decorations and lights and cutting it to fit with the lid closed. Whole trees left beside the cart may not be collected. Without a cart, the city page describes a free drop-off at the Ada County Landfill for a limited post-holiday window, and says flocked trees go in the trash. Dates change from season to season, so check that page for the current ones.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long can a living Christmas tree stay indoors?

About 7 to 10 days is the common extension guidance, and shorter is better. UNH Extension says longer exposure to warmth can end the tree's winter dormancy, which can injure it when it goes back outside. Keep it in the coolest room, away from vents, and move it through a garage or porch on the way in and out.

### Can I plant a living Christmas tree right after Christmas?

Only if the ground isn't frozen and you've given the tree several days in a sheltered spot first. Sources disagree: the NCTA says plant as soon as possible, while UNH says planting that late in winter is unlikely to work and recommends waiting for spring. In Boise, a pre-dug hole makes the early option realistic.

### How big a hole does a balled-and-burlapped tree need?

Iowa State University Extension says 2 to 3 times as wide as the root ball and 2 to 3 inches shallower than its height, so the top of the ball sits slightly above the surrounding soil. Make the sides rough, backfill with the original soil, and keep the trunk flare at or above ground level.

### Which living Christmas tree species grow best in the Treasure Valley?

The City of Boise's tree selection guide lists Serbian spruce, white spruce, and Austrian pine as tolerant of alkaline soil, and Colorado spruce as adaptable but pest-prone. Douglas fir is listed as not tolerant of alkaline soil, and Norway spruce prefers acidic soil. A soil test tells you more than a species list does.

### Do I need to remove the burlap when planting?

Take off twine and any plastic or vinyl burlap completely. For natural burlap, the Treasure Valley guide says to cut it halfway down the ball, and Iowa State says to remove the top third to half. If there's a wire basket, take out the upper half at least, or all of it if you can.

### Can I replant a cut Christmas tree?

No. A cut tree has no roots, so it can't be planted. Only a living tree sold with its root ball in burlap or a pot can go in the ground. For a cut tree, check the City of Boise's Christmas tree page for compost cart and drop-off options.
