# Norway Maple: How to Identify It, What Goes Wrong, and Whether to Keep It

If a maple in your yard has a dense, rounded crown and leaves that look a lot like sugar maple, there's a good chance it's a Norway maple. The quickest check is to snap a leaf off at the stalk: Norway maple oozes milky white sap, and sugar maple doesn't. It's a tough, fast-growing shade tree, and it's also one with a long list of downsides, from bare lawn underneath to girdling roots to a bad reputation in parts of the country.

Honestly, most of the time a healthy, mature Norway maple is worth keeping if it's not causing a specific problem. A few situations tip the other way, and we'll get to those.

## How to Tell Norway Maple From Sugar Maple

The two trees are easy to confuse from the street. Both have five-lobed leaves and turn yellow to orange in fall. A few details separate them.

- **The sap test.** Break a leaf off and look at the end of the stalk. According to [University of Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2024-07-05-norway-maple-woes-why-its-my-do-not-plant-list), Norway maple leaves release milky white sap at the stem, and it shows in the leaf veins too. The same page warns that drought can slow the flow, so try a few leaves on a young shoot if the first one looks dry.
- **Leaf shape.** The [New York Invasive Species Information](https://nyis.info/species/norway-maple/) page describes Norway maple leaves as usually wider than they are tall, with five prominent lobes.
- **Buds.** The same page notes blunt bud tips on Norway maple, compared with the pointed buds of sugar maple.
- **Bark.** [Clark University's Hadwen Arboretum](https://www.clarku.edu/hadwen-arboretum/trees/norway-maple/) describes Norway maple bark as grayish-brown and smoother than other mature North American maples.

Purple-leaved maples are a giveaway too. Cultivars like 'Crimson King' are Norway maples, and [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/acer-platanoides/) says the milky-sap test works on them as well.

## Why So Many Norway Maples Got Planted

Norway maple became a default street and yard tree for a few boring reasons. It's hardy, it grows quickly, it casts dense shade, and it puts up with hard conditions. The Clark University arboretum page says it became a popular replacement after Dutch elm disease took out American elms in the 1950s and 60s, and the [Woody Invasives](https://woodyinvasives.org/woody-invasive-species/norway-maple/) page describes it as widely planted as a street tree to replace those elms.

It also suits our dry, alkaline soils better than some maples. [Utah State University Extension](https://extension.usu.edu/yardandgarden/research/maples-in-the-landscape) calls it well adapted to the Intermountain West and tolerant of alkaline soil and arid climate, and notes it's probably the most commonly planted maple in the region. That's why you see so many of them in older Boise, Meridian and Nampa neighborhoods.

## Size and Growth

Size depends on the cultivar. Utah State's table lists some compact selections at 20 to 25 feet, a number of common ones around 40 feet tall, and larger types like 'Emerald Queen' at about 65 feet tall and 40 feet wide. NC State describes the species as growing 40 to 50 feet tall or taller with a rounded crown. If you inherited a Norway maple and don't know the cultivar, assume it will get big and plan around that.

## Norway Maple Problems

### Dense shade, shallow roots and bare lawn

This is the one most homeowners notice first. NC State Extension says the tree prevents other plants from growing under it because of its shallow root system, and that the dense canopy and shallow roots make it very challenging to grow anything under the drip line. Utah State also calls it somewhat shallow-rooted, with roots that can interfere with sidewalks and other plants.

If grass keeps thinning out beneath yours, don't assume it's a watering problem you can fix with more sprinklers. Our guide on [why grass won't grow under trees](/blog/grass-wont-grow-under-trees/) covers what actually helps. Often the better answer is mulch, a ground-cover bed, or shade-tolerant plantings instead of fighting the tree for turf.

### Girdling roots

Norway maples have a reputation for roots that circle the base of the trunk and slowly choke it. The Illinois Extension piece says girdling roots often start in the nursery pot, can be pruned off at planting, and that established trees may need a certified arborist to excavate and remove them. Signs to watch for: a trunk that goes straight into the ground like a post instead of flaring out at the base, one-sided thin canopy, or a visible root crossing the trunk right at soil level. We cover the full diagnosis and fix in our article on [girdling roots](/blog/girdling-roots-fix/).

### Verticillium wilt

Utah State says nearly all maples are susceptible to verticillium wilt, a soilborne fungus that clogs the tree's water-carrying tissue and causes browning leaves and branch dieback, and that there's no known cure. Illinois Extension says it is often fatal. If one side of your tree suddenly wilts in summer, read our guide to [verticillium wilt in maples](/blog/verticillium-wilt-maple/) before assuming drought.

### Scorch, anthracnose and other leaf trouble

Illinois Extension notes that Norway maple leaves can scorch and crisp in hot, dry weather, which won't kill the tree on its own but leaves it more open to other problems. Anthracnose, a leaf fungus, shows up in cool wet springs and usually isn't a big deal for a healthy tree. Consistent deep watering does more for a Norway maple in July than almost anything else you can do. See [how to water trees in Boise](/blog/how-to-water-trees-boise/) for the schedule.

### Seeds and invasiveness

Norway maple drops loads of winged seeds, and Utah State says they may become weedy in some situations. In parts of the country, it's a bigger deal than weedy. NC State says the tree has been labeled invasive in several northeastern states, and the New York Invasive Species page says it's considered invasive in the states shaded on its range map, where dense shade keeps native seedlings from regenerating. Clark University's arboretum adds that Massachusetts bans its sale, as do some other states.

None of that makes your backyard tree a crime. It does mean you shouldn't plant a new one if you've got a choice, and it's worth pulling seedlings that pop up in flower beds and along fence lines before they get established.

## Pruning a Norway Maple

Utah State recommends minimal pruning, done in early spring while the tree is dormant, limited to dead, diseased or crossing branches. Maples bleed sap if you cut them at the wrong time of year, which looks alarming but is mostly harmless. Our article on [when to prune maple trees](/blog/when-to-prune-maple-trees/) goes through the timing options and why bleeding happens.

The bigger point: don't top it. Chopping a Norway maple back to stubs leaves big wounds and triggers a thicket of weak sprouts. If yours has grown too big for the space, the fix is selective reduction cuts by someone who knows what they're doing, not a haircut. ## Keep It or Replace It?

Keep it if the tree is healthy, has good structure, and isn't causing damage to anything that matters. A mature Norway maple gives real shade, and a big old tree is worth more to a property than a sapling you plant to replace it.

Think about replacing it if:

- The trunk shows signs of girdling roots that can't be corrected.
- Verticillium wilt has taken a large part of the crown.
- The roots are lifting walks, patios or a driveway, and root pruning would hurt the tree.
- The tree is leaning, hollow, or has large dead limbs over a house or play area.
- You're planting fresh and have a choice. We'd pick something else.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I tell a Norway maple from a sugar maple?

Break a leaf off at the stalk. Norway maple leaks milky white sap from the stem, while sugar maple's sap is clear. Norway maple leaves also tend to be wider than they are long, and its buds are blunt where sugar maple's are pointed. Purple-leaved 'Crimson King' is also a Norway maple.

### Is Norway maple invasive?

In parts of the Northeast and Midwest, yes. NC State Extension says it's labeled invasive in several northeastern states, and Massachusetts bans its sale. Utah State only notes that the seeds may become weedy in some situations. Where it's established already, it's wise to pull seedlings, but an existing tree isn't an emergency.

### Why won't grass grow under my Norway maple?

Dense shade and a shallow, thirsty root system make it a hard place for turf. NC State Extension says it's very challenging to grow anything under the drip line. Mulch the area, or plant a shade-tolerant ground cover instead of reseeding grass every spring.

### Does Norway maple get verticillium wilt?

Yes. Utah State says nearly all maples are susceptible, and there's no known cure. Look for sudden wilting or browning on one section of the crown. A heavily infected tree may eventually need to come down.

### When should I prune a Norway maple?

Early spring while the tree is still dormant is Utah State's recommendation, and pruning should be minimal: dead, diseased or crossing branches. Sap flow from fresh cuts looks dramatic but is mostly cosmetic. Avoid topping, and have a pro handle large limbs.

### Should I cut down my Norway maple?

Only if there's a reason: girdling roots, advanced disease, structural defects, or damage to hardscape. A healthy mature tree in a spot where it fits is usually worth keeping. If you're not sure, an on-site look from an arborist will give you a straight answer.
