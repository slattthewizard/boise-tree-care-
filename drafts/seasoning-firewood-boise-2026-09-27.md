# Seasoning Firewood From a Backyard Tree: How Long Each Species Takes in Boise's Dry Air

Most firewood needs 6 to 12 months of seasoning before it burns well, and in Boise's dry summer air that clock runs on the fast end, as long as the wood is split, stacked off the ground, and getting real airflow. Green wood straight off a saw is not ready no matter how good the tree looked standing up. It still holds a lot of water in the cells, and that water has to leave before the wood will light easily or burn clean.

If a tree just came down in your yard, whether from a storm or a planned removal, you have a decision to make before you stack anything: is this species worth the shed space, or is it better hauled off? Ash, locust, and maple are worth seasoning. Cottonwood, willow, and poplar mostly are not. This guide covers how long seasoning actually takes here, which of the trees common around the Treasure Valley are worth burning, how to stack a pile so it dries instead of just sitting there, and the wrinkle pine and spruce owners need to know about before they let a round sit too long. ## What "Seasoning" Firewood Actually Means

A freshly cut log is 60% to 100% water by weight, depending on species. Seasoning is just letting that water evaporate out of the wood until it burns efficiently instead of hissing, smoking, and struggling to stay lit. Wood does not need to be bone dry, it needs to drop to roughly 20% moisture content or lower, which is the level most extension services and stove manufacturers point to as the practical target for good combustion. Wetter wood wastes a large share of its heat just boiling off moisture, and it builds up more creosote in a chimney along the way.

Splitting speeds this up dramatically. Bark holds moisture in, so a round left whole can take twice as long to season as the same wood split into standard-size pieces. If a tree just came down, split it as soon as you reasonably can rather than letting whole rounds sit.

## How Long Seasoning Takes in Boise's Climate

| Wood type | Typical seasoning time here | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Softwoods (pine) | 6-9 months | Low density dries fast but burns fast too |
| Medium hardwoods (elm, maple) | 8-12 months | Split small for faster drying |
| Dense hardwoods (ash, locust, oak) | 10-14 months | Worth the wait, burns long and hot |

Boise's low humidity and hot, dry summers are an advantage for seasoning wood compared to a humid climate, where the same wood can take an extra season to reach burn-ready moisture. Split and stacked in spring, a lot of medium-density wood is ready by the following winter. Split in fall, plan on it not being ready until the winter after that. Dense hardwood cut in fall usually needs a full year before it is genuinely ready to burn well.

## Best and Worst Firewood From Trees Common in the Treasure Valley

Not every tree that comes down in a Boise yard is worth stacking. Some species burn hot and long; others are barely worth the trailer space.

**Good firewood, worth seasoning:**
- Ash: splits easily, burns hot and steady, a valley favorite
- Honey locust: very dense, long burn time, takes longer to season
- Maple: solid mid-range firewood, seasons a bit faster than ash

**Low-value firewood, usually not worth the storage space:**
- Cottonwood
- Willow
- Poplar

**Usable with caveats:**
- Pine and spruce: fine for shoulder-season fires or a fast hot burn, but see the beetle warning below before you let a fresh-cut round sit too long. For more on what tends to go wrong with the valley's evergreens, see [why pine trees struggle in Boise](/blog/pine-tree-problems-boise/).

According to [Utah State University Extension's wood heating comparison](https://extension.usu.edu/forestry/resources/forest-products/wood-heating), ash and black locust rate as excellent firewood, while willow rates poor and poplar rates low in heat content. Cottonwood rates only fair, and its heat output per cord runs roughly half that of ash, so it takes twice the wood to get the same warmth.

## Is Cottonwood Good Firewood?

Not really, at least not compared to what else the valley offers. Cottonwood is a low-density wood, so a cord of it holds far less heat energy than a cord of ash or locust of the same size, and you will burn through it faster to get the same warmth. It also tends to season with a noticeable smell some people find unpleasant. If a cottonwood came down on your property and you already have denser wood on hand, it is often more practical to have the cottonwood hauled away than to season a full cord of it. Willow shares the same problem and rates even lower for heat value. ## How to Stack Firewood So It Actually Dries

The stack matters as much as the species. A pile that traps moisture will not season no matter how long it sits.

1. Split the wood into pieces roughly 4-6 inches across. Splitting exposes more surface area to air.
2. Stack off the ground on pallets, rails, or scrap 2x4s. Wood sitting on dirt or grass pulls moisture back up and rots from the bottom.
3. Orient the bark side up on the top row if you are not using a cover, since bark sheds water like a shingle.
4. Cover only the top of the stack, and leave the sides open. A fully wrapped tarp traps humidity inside the pile and slows seasoning instead of helping it.
5. Leave gaps between rows or use a single-row stack where possible. Airflow through the pile is what actually pulls moisture out, not sun exposure alone.
6. Face the stack toward prevailing wind and afternoon sun if you have a choice of spots, and keep it a few feet off the house.

A single, well-stacked row seasons noticeably faster than a wide, tight pile, because the wind actually reaches every piece instead of only the outer layer.

## How to Tell When Firewood Is Actually Ready to Burn

Looks can be deceiving. Wood that looks dry on the outside can still be wet in the center. A cheap moisture meter (sold at most hardware stores) is the only reliable way to know for sure. Split a piece open, press the probes into the freshly exposed inner face, and read the number, testing a few different pieces from around the pile. [University of New Hampshire Extension](https://extension.unh.edu/blog/2017/12/how-can-i-tell-if-my-firewood-seasoned-okay-burn) puts the target for properly seasoned firewood at below 20% moisture content; below that, wood lights easier, burns hotter, and leaves less creosote.

A few rough signs without a meter, none of them as reliable as a real reading:

- Checking (radial cracks) visible on the cut ends
- Noticeably lighter than when it was cut
- Bark loosening or falling off on its own
- Two split pieces knocked together make a sharp crack, not a dull thud
- No hissing or bubbling from the ends when it burns

## The Ips Beetle Risk With Green Pine and Spruce

If a pine or spruce came down or was pruned heavily on your property, do not let the fresh-cut rounds or slash sit stacked through a Boise summer. Pine and spruce are both host trees for the Ips engraver beetle, and [University of Idaho Extension](https://www.uidaho.edu/extension/food/ipm/garden-pests/ips-beetle) recommends removing freshly cut evergreen wood away from any standing pine or spruce, since a stack of green logs near live trees can let the beetles spread once they emerge. The same source notes that chipping or debarking fresh-cut wood destroys the beetle. The safer move is to split and stack conifer wood promptly away from standing evergreens, or have it hauled off, rather than leaving whole green rounds sitting for months.

## Air Quality Rules Before You Burn

Even properly seasoned wood is still combustion, and the Treasure Valley gets winter temperature inversions that trap smoke close to the ground instead of letting it disperse. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does firewood need to season before it burns well?

Most species need 6 to 12 months split and properly stacked, with dense hardwoods like ash and locust on the longer end and softwoods like pine on the shorter end. Boise's dry summers speed this up compared to a humid climate.

### Can I burn firewood that has only seasoned for a few months?

You can, but it will smoke more, burn less efficiently, and build up more creosote in a chimney than properly seasoned wood. If you are in a hurry, choose a lower-density species like pine over a dense hardwood, since it sheds moisture faster.

### Is cottonwood good firewood?

No, cottonwood is low-density and burns fast without much heat output, and it tends to smell unpleasant while burning. It is usually better to have it hauled off than to season a cord of it.

### Do I need a moisture meter to season firewood correctly?

You do not need one, but it is the only reliable way to confirm wood has actually reached the roughly 20% moisture level that burns well. Visual signs like cracked ends or loose bark are useful clues, not proof.

### Why shouldn't I stack green pine or spruce and just let it sit?

Fresh-cut and stressed conifer wood attracts Ips engraver beetles, and a stacked pile of green rounds can turn into a breeding site that puts nearby healthy trees at risk. Split and season conifer wood promptly, or have it hauled away, instead of letting whole green rounds sit through the summer.

### Are there rules about burning wood in Boise during the winter?

Idaho's environmental regulators issue air quality advisories for the Treasure Valley during winter inversions, and local ordinances can curtail burning on the worst air days. Check current conditions before a planned burn rather than assuming it is fine.
