# Powdery Mildew on Trees: What It Is and Whether You Need to Treat It

Powdery mildew is a fungus that coats leaves in a white to gray powder, usually from late summer into early fall. On established trees and large shrubs it is almost always cosmetic: it looks bad, but it rarely kills anything. In most yards the right response is better air flow and a bit of patience, and fungicide is the exception rather than the plan.

That said, the white coating can look alarming, and a few plants do suffer from it. Here's how to recognize it, why it shows up in a dry place like the Treasure Valley, and when it's worth doing anything at all.

## What powdery mildew looks like

The University of Nebraska-Lincoln's [NebGuide on powdery mildew](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g2021/2010/html/view) describes it as superficial white to gray powdery growth on leaves, stems, flowers or fruit. It starts as small patches and can spread to cover the whole leaf. The youngest tissue gets hit first.

Utah State University Extension's [powdery mildew page](https://extension.usu.edu/planthealth/ipm/ornamental-pest-guide/diseases/powdery-mildew) adds that it appears most often on the tops of leaves, but can also occur on leaf undersides, young stems, buds and flowers. You can usually rub the powder off with a finger, which separates it from other leaf problems. Severe cases can lead to yellowing, browning, distorted new growth and early leaf drop.

If your leaves are yellowing without any white coating, that's probably something else. Our post on [tree leaves turning yellow in summer](/blog/tree-leaves-turning-yellow-summer/) covers water stress, root problems and the other usual causes.

## Which trees and shrubs get it

Quite a few. Nebraska lists lilacs, roses, rhododendrons, dogwoods and willows among the common woody hosts. Alabama Cooperative Extension's [guide to controlling powdery mildew on ornamentals](https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/landscaping/controlling-powdery-mildew-on-ornamentals/) names crabapple, elm, lilac, oak, sycamore, saucer magnolia and flowering dogwood, along with roses and several others.

The most useful detail in those sources: each powdery mildew fungus is usually specific to one host or group of hosts. White powder on your lilac doesn't mean the maple next to it is in danger. We'd also be careful about assuming any particular tree is vulnerable just because it's on a list. Plenty of plants on those lists go years without a problem.

Lilac is the one most Treasure Valley yards notice. If yours gets the white coating in late summer, the [lilac pruning guide](/blog/how-to-prune-lilacs/) covers the thinning cuts that help with air movement.

## Why you get it even in a dry climate

This surprises people. You'd expect a fungus to want wet weather, and plenty of leaf diseases do. Powdery mildew is different.

Nebraska says these fungi don't need free water on the leaf to germinate and infect, and that free water can actually inhibit spore germination. What they want is humidity and mild to warm temperatures. Alabama Extension says spores germinate and infect quickly on dry leaf surfaces when temperatures are mild and relative humidity is at least 95%. That's not the humidity of the air in general, but the humidity right around the leaf, which a crowded or shaded canopy can create all by itself.

USU's page says powdery mildews are common in warm, dry climates. Put those together and a Treasure Valley summer makes sense. Hot, dry days, then cooler nights, with air that stays still in a dense, shaded canopy. Alabama Extension says outbreaks are heaviest where days are warm, nights are cool and rainfall is light, in humid, shaded spots.

In practice, the same tree can have a clean sunny side and a mildewed shady side. That's your clue to look at the site rather than the weather.

## Conditions that make it worse

- **Shade and crowding.** Nebraska says it's most common in shaded, overcrowded areas.
- **Poor air movement.** Dense interior growth traps humid air around the leaves.
- **Lush new growth.** USU says young, succulent growth is the most susceptible.
- **Too much nitrogen.** Excess fertilizer pushes that soft growth. USU recommends avoiding late-summer nitrogen, and Alabama says to keep growth slow and even with light, infrequent applications. Our [guide to fertilizing trees in Boise](/blog/fertilize-trees-boise/) explains when feeding actually helps a tree and when it just feeds the mildew.

## How much harm does it do

Usually very little to an established tree. Alabama Extension says it mainly reduces beauty and occasionally slows growth, that plant death is rare, and that it causes little damage to established trees. Repeated severe infections can slowly weaken small shrubs and tree seedlings. Nebraska calls it rarely fatal, though some plants can take significant damage.

Nebraska's guide goes as far as saying yearly infections on something like a lilac can simply be ignored if the look doesn't bother you.

The cases where we'd pay more attention are small or newly planted trees, and plants that get hit hard every single year.

## Cultural controls that actually help

This is where extension sources put most of their weight, and we agree with them. None of it costs much.

1. **Open the canopy.** Alabama Extension recommends pruning overhanging growth to improve air circulation and sunlight, and Nebraska says to provide good air circulation. Thinning cuts that let light and air into the interior are the move. Done lightly, it's what we cover in [crown thinning, raising and reduction](/blog/crown-thinning-raising-reduction/). Don't over-thin: stripping out too much foliage stresses a tree and brings its own problems.
2. **Give plants space and light.** Alabama's advice is to space plants widely in open sunny areas, and Nebraska says to avoid putting susceptible plants in shade. That's a planting decision, so it's easiest to get right the first time.
3. **Go easy on nitrogen.** See above. A lawn fertilizer program running at full strength under a lilac can push the exact growth mildew likes.
4. **Clean up in fall.** Both Nebraska and Alabama recommend raking and discarding infected leaves, since the fungus overwinters on plant debris.
5. **Choose resistant varieties.** Alabama calls resistant varieties the best method of disease control. If you're planting a crabapple or a lilac, ask the nursery which cultivars are listed as resistant. That's also the cheap fix for a plant you'd otherwise fight every year.

For the bigger picture on what else attacks valley trees, the [common tree diseases in Boise](/blog/tree-diseases-boise/) post is a good companion.

## When fungicide is worth it

Rarely. Nebraska says fungicides are usually unnecessary because the plant's overall health is often unaffected. USU lists the disease as mainly an aesthetic problem on ornamentals and suggests tolerating it. A lilac that's white in September isn't worth a spray program.

Where it can make sense: a small plant that gets hit hard every year, or something valuable where the damage is more than cosmetic. If you go that route, the sources agree on a few points:

- Timing matters. USU says to apply when the first small white spots appear, because treatment is less effective once the whole leaf is colonized.
- Cover all plant surfaces, per Nebraska, and repeat according to the label.
- Test on a few leaves first for plant damage, and watch the temperature limits on the label. USU says sulfur should only be applied below 90 degrees F.
- Active ingredients named in the extension sources include sulfur, potassium bicarbonate, chlorothalonil and copper, among others.

Homeowners can use products labeled for home use on their own plants, following the label exactly. The label is the law, and that includes which plants it can go on. We'd skip it for any tree you can't reach from the ground.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will powdery mildew kill my tree?

Almost never on an established tree. Extension sources describe it as mainly a cosmetic problem that can occasionally slow growth. Repeated severe infections can slowly weaken small shrubs and young trees, so those deserve more attention. A mature tree with white leaves in late summer is usually fine.

### Why do I have powdery mildew when it hasn't rained?

Powdery mildew doesn't need wet leaves. Spores germinate on dry leaf surfaces when humidity around the leaf is high and temperatures are mild, and free water can actually slow them down. Dry days with cool nights and a dense, shaded canopy create exactly those conditions.

### How do I get rid of powdery mildew on a tree?

Mostly you reduce what lets it thrive: thin the canopy for air movement, move or avoid planting susceptible plants in shade, go easy on nitrogen, and rake up infected leaves in fall. Fungicide is a backup option, and only worth considering for plants that really suffer.

### Is powdery mildew on lilac a problem?

Usually no. Nebraska Extension says yearly infections on lilacs can be ignored if the look doesn't bother you, and USU calls powdery mildew mainly an aesthetic problem on ornamentals. Thinning the shrub for air movement and raking leaves in fall are the usual steps.

### Can I spray for powdery mildew myself?

Yes, with products labeled for home use on that plant, following the label exactly. Spray when the first white spots appear, since it works less well once the whole leaf is covered. Large trees are hard to cover from the ground, which is one reason we'd treat the site first.

### Does powdery mildew spread to other plants?

Usually only to plants of the same type. Extension sources say each powdery mildew fungus is typically specific to one host or group of hosts, so mildew on your lilac isn't likely to jump to a nearby maple or rose.
