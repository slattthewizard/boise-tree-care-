# Tent Caterpillars and Fall Webworms: Ugly Nests, Usually Harmless Trees

That silk-wrapped mess in your tree is almost certainly tent caterpillars or fall webworms, two of the more common [tree pests in Boise](/blog/tree-pests-boise/), and in most cases your tree will be fine. The two pests show up at different times of year and build their webs in different spots on the tree, which is the fastest way to tell them apart.

Neither one is usually worth spraying on a healthy, established shade tree. The bigger question is whether you are looking at a one-time nuisance or a repeat problem on a tree that is already struggling with something else.

Most people find these webs after walking past a tree they see every day and suddenly noticing a clump of silk they swear was not there last week. That fast appearance is normal for both pests. Once a batch of eggs hatches, the caterpillars build and expand their shared web quickly, so it can look like it appeared overnight even though the tree has been carrying eggs for weeks or months.

## Two Pests, Two Very Different Times of Year

If the web showed up in spring, right around when the leaves came out, you are looking at tent caterpillars. If it showed up in late summer or early fall on a tree that already leafed out weeks ago, that is fall webworm.

Location on the branch is the other tell. Tent caterpillars build in the crotches, the V-shaped forks where branches meet. Fall webworms build out at the branch tips, wrapping the web around the leaves at the very end of a limb rather than in the fork behind them.

## Spring Webs in the Branch Forks: Tent Caterpillars

Tent caterpillars overwinter as egg masses glued to twigs, then hatch as the tree's buds break in spring. The young caterpillars spin a small silk tent in a branch fork and expand it as they grow. During the day they leave the tent in groups to feed on nearby foliage, then file back to the same tent most evenings, which is why the nest looks busier at dusk than at noon.

Early on, the tent is small and easy to miss among new spring leaves. By the time most homeowners notice it, it has grown into a dense, grayish-white mass wedged into the fork. The caterpillars themselves are hairy and usually marked with a row of pale spots down the back, moving in a loose column when they head out to feed.

A single spring's worth of feeding does not usually do lasting harm. According to the [Pacific Northwest Pest Management Handbook](https://pnwhandbooks.org/insect/hort/landscape/hosts-pests-landscape-plants/birch-betula-tent-caterpillar), a joint publication of Idaho, Oregon, and Washington State University Extension, healthy trees typically grow new leaves back by midsummer after a tent caterpillar infestation. The bigger risk is a tree that gets defoliated several years in a row, which can weaken or kill a tree that is already unthrifty, and small or newly planted trees are more vulnerable than an established shade tree.

## Late-Summer Webs at the Branch Tips: Fall Webworms

Fall webworm is the most common web-building caterpillar in North America and feeds on more than 100 kinds of deciduous trees and shrubs, according to a [Pacific Northwest Pest Management Handbook](https://pnwhandbooks.org/insect/hort/landscape/hosts-pests-landscape-plants/ash-fraxinus-fall-webworm) entry on the pest. Instead of a fork nest, the caterpillars enclose the leaves at a branch tip in a loose, dirty-looking web that fills up with dead leaves and droppings as they feed inside it.

Because this damage shows up late in the growing season, after the tree has already done most of its year's work making sugar through photosynthesis, it rarely does real harm to an established tree. [University of Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2025-09-12-webs-trees-life-and-impact-fall-webworms) horticulturists describe fall webworm damage as mostly a cosmetic problem on mature trees, since the leaves being eaten were close to finished for the season anyway.

Fall webworm turns up on fruit trees too, and it is worth telling apart from [codling moth](/blog/codling-moth-fruit-trees-boise/), which is a different pest entirely: codling moth larvae tunnel inside the fruit itself rather than webbing up the leaves, so a wormy apple with no web in sight is a codling moth problem, not this one.

## Tent Caterpillars vs. Fall Webworms at a Glance

| | Tent Caterpillars | Fall Webworms |
|---|---|---|
| When you see the web | Spring, as leaves emerge | Late summer into early fall |
| Where on the branch | Forks and crotches | Branch tips |
| How the web looks | Tighter, silk mass in a fork | Loose, baggy, often dirty with frass |
| Caterpillar behavior | Leave tent by day to feed, return at night | Feed inside the enclosed web |
| Typical risk to a healthy shade tree | Low for one season; watch for repeat years | Low, since damage comes late in the season |

## Do You Need to Spray a Mature Shade Tree? Usually Not

For a healthy, established tree, most Extension guidance lands in the same place: tolerate it. [Utah State University Extension's](https://extension.usu.edu/planthealth/ipm/ornamental-pest-guide/arthopods/moths/western-tent-caterpillar) guidance on western tent caterpillar notes that severe damage to ornamental and shade trees is uncommon and recommends tolerating the pest rather than treating it in most home landscape situations.

Fall webworm is even less worth spraying. Their web is water-resistant, so a typical backyard sprayer cannot get product through it to reach the caterpillars inside without high-pressure equipment, and a long list of natural predators and parasitic wasps usually keeps populations in check on their own.

Where spraying can make sense is a small, newly planted tree, or a caterpillar population heavy enough to strip a vulnerable tree bare early in the season. That is a case-by-case call, and if you are not sure which category your tree falls into, that is exactly what a [tree health assessment](/blog/tree-health-assessment-boise/) is for.

## Removing a Nest You Can Reach, and What Never to Do

If a tent or web is low enough to reach safely from the ground with a pole pruner, cutting out the branch section it is on and dropping it in the trash is a reasonable do-it-yourself step, especially for a spring tent caterpillar nest before the caterpillars have spread out to feed. Do this in the early morning or evening when more of the caterpillars are inside the tent rather than out feeding.

Never burn a nest while it is still in the tree. It is a common old piece of advice, and it is a bad one: an open flame in the canopy risks scorching or killing the branch, and depending on how dry the season has been, it risks a lot more than that. Idaho's hot, dry summers and the wildland interface along the foothills are not the place to be improvising with fire in a tree.

For anything up high, in a fork you cannot safely reach from the ground, or spread across enough of the canopy that a pole pruner will not cut it, that is a job for a crew with the right gear rather than a ladder. ## When Repeated Defoliation Actually Matters

One buggy-looking spring or one messy late-summer web is not, by itself, a reason to worry. What changes the picture is a tree that gets stripped by the same pest two or three years running, especially if it is also dealing with something else, like [drought stress](/blog/drought-stress-trees-boise/) during one of Boise's hot, dry summers, recent transplant shock, or root damage from construction or compacted soil nearby.

A tree juggling repeat defoliation on top of an existing stressor has less stored energy to recover and can start showing thinner canopy, dieback in the smaller branches, or slower leaf-out the following spring. Signs that a tree needs an actual look rather than another wait-and-see season:

- The same tree had a heavy web or tent problem last year too, not just this one
- Leaf-out next spring is noticeably thinner or later than the tree's neighbors
- You are also seeing dead branch tips, peeling bark, or sap in unusual spots
- The tree was already stressed from drought, a recent move, or nearby digging
- Several branches have no new growth by early summer

That combination is worth a second look rather than assuming it will bounce back on its own. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are tent caterpillars and fall webworms the same insect?

No. They are different caterpillars with different life cycles. Tent caterpillars show up in spring and build in branch forks, while fall webworms show up in late summer or fall and build out at the branch tips. The names get mixed up a lot because both build a visible silk web, but the timing and web location tell them apart.

### Will tent caterpillars or webworms kill my tree?

Usually not, if the tree is healthy and established and this is a one-season event. Trees typically push new leaves back out within weeks to a couple of months. The real risk is repeated defoliation over several years, especially on a small tree or one already stressed by drought, transplant shock, or root problems.

### Should I spray my tree for tent caterpillars or fall webworms?

For a mature, healthy shade tree, most Extension guidance says it is not necessary. Fall webworm webbing is also water-resistant, which makes typical sprays hard to get through anyway. Spraying is more likely to make sense on a small or newly planted tree, or a heavy infestation that is stripping foliage early in the season.

### Can I remove a nest myself?

If you can reach it safely from the ground with a pole pruner, cutting out that branch section and disposing of it in the trash works well, particularly for a spring tent caterpillar nest. Never use a ladder to reach a high nest, and never burn a nest while it is still attached to the tree. Anything out of safe reach is better left to a crew with the right equipment.

### What time of year will I see these webs in Boise?

Tent caterpillar webs typically appear in spring, right around when the tree leafs out, tucked into branch forks. Fall webworm webs show up later, from late summer into early fall, wrapped around the leaves at the branch tips on a tree that has already been fully leafed out for months.
