# What to Do With Stump Grindings: Mulch, Fill or Haul Away

Stump grindings are the pile of shredded wood and dirt a grinder leaves behind once a stump is gone. You have three real options: spread a thin layer as mulch somewhere else in the yard, haul it away, or work it into the hole as a small part of a much larger topsoil fill. What you should not do is leave the whole pile packed into the hole and expect grass or a new plant to thrive there.

That last point trips up a lot of homeowners, because it looks like the grindings are "filling the hole" for free. They are not a substitute for soil, and left in place they cause two separate problems: the wood ties up nitrogen as it breaks down, and the pile keeps settling for months afterward. Both are fixable if you know what to do with the material before you plant or reseed.

This guide covers each option, when to use it, and how Boise's clay-heavy, alkaline soil changes the timeline.

## Why You Can't Just Leave the Grindings in the Hole

A stump grinder does not remove wood, it shreds it and mixes the shredded fiber into the top layer of soil where the stump used to be. That mix looks like dirt but behaves very differently once you try to grow something in it.

The University of Illinois Extension's Good Growing program explains the core issue plainly: wood material mixed in with the soil can tie up nitrogen as it breaks down, making it hard to grow lawn or anything else where the tree once stood ([extension.illinois.edu](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2025-07-18-what-do-tree-stump-guide-tree-stump-removal)). Soil microbes that digest wood need nitrogen to do it, and they pull that nitrogen from the surrounding soil faster than new grass or young roots can compete for it. The result is yellow, stunted growth right over the old stump, sometimes for a full season.

Iowa State University's Yard and Garden program adds a second reason to clear the shavings out first: the leftover material changes the soil's structure and its ability to hold moisture and nutrients, which is exactly what new turf or plants need to be consistent ([yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/how-remove-tree-stumps)). In Boise's compacted, often clay or caliche soils, that inconsistency shows up as a soft, spongy patch that drains differently than the lawn around it, which is its own headache on top of the nitrogen problem.

## Using Grindings as Mulch the Right Way

The grindings are not waste. Fresh wood shavings make decent mulch, just not in the spot where the stump was ground and not tilled into soil anywhere.

Oregon State University Extension's Ask Extension service is specific about why placement matters: wood chips left on the surface as mulch do not tie up soil nitrogen the way they do when they get mixed into the soil, and the smaller the particle size, the more likely tilled-in chips are to cause a nitrogen shortfall ([extension.oregonstate.edu](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/ask-extension/featured/wood-chips-mulch)). Left on top, soil organisms break the wood down slowly and gradually work it in over months or years, without robbing nearby roots of nitrogen in the meantime.

A few rules keep surface mulch working in your favor:

- Spread it 2 to 4 inches deep, no more, around trees, shrubs or garden beds elsewhere in the yard.
- Keep it a few inches back from trunks and stems. Piled against bark, it traps moisture and invites rot and pests.
- Skip it around brand-new transplants and seedlings until they are established. Fine wood particles right at the root zone of a young plant can still compete with it early on.
- Do not rake it into the topsoil of a vegetable bed or flower bed. Surface only.

If you have more grindings than you have mulch beds to cover, it is fine to haul the rest away rather than force it into places it does not belong.

## Filling the Hole: Topsoil, Not Grindings

Once the loose shavings and larger wood chunks are cleared out of the hole, fill it with quality topsoil rather than backfilling with the grindings themselves. For a shallow hole, topsoil alone is usually enough. For a deeper one, a common approach is a coarser fill dirt for the lower layers and topsoil reserved for the top few inches, where new roots will actually grow.

Mound the fill slightly above the surrounding grade. Wood fiber and loosened soil settle as they compact and any remaining organic matter decomposes, and a hole that looked level on day one can dip an inch or two within a season. Expect the area to keep settling for several months, so plan on checking back and topping it off rather than treating it as a one-time job.

If your yard has several old stumps ground at once, this is also the point to double check whether your service already grinds a couple of inches below grade, since a shallower grind leaves more root flare in the way and a deeper grind leaves you a bigger hole to fill. ## Should You Plant Grass or Sod Over the Spot?

Not right away. Grass seed or sod laid directly over fresh grindings, even after you have added topsoil, tends to grow unevenly for the same nitrogen tie-up reason covered above: any leftover wood fiber mixed through that top layer is still competing with the new roots.

A more reliable sequence:

1. Clear out the grindings and loose wood chunks.
2. Fill and lightly compact with topsoil, mounded slightly to allow for settling.
3. Water and let the area settle for a few weeks before you judge whether it needs more soil.
4. Overseed or lay sod once the patch is level and firm, not sunken or spongy underfoot.

## A Boise-Specific Note on Timing

Our clay and often compacted soils drain slower than sandy soil, which means a hole backfilled with topsoil here can hold more moisture right after filling than the same hole would in a lighter soil. That is normal, but it also means giving the area a little extra time to settle and dry between watering the new fill and seeding grass on top of it. If irrigation runs through that part of your yard, check that a sprinkler head is not soaking the exact spot where the fill is still settling, since a consistently soggy patch will slow down both settling and any new grass you put down.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use stump grindings as mulch around my other plants?

Yes, as a thin surface layer 2 to 4 inches deep around established trees, shrubs or beds, kept a few inches back from trunks and stems. Do not till or dig the grindings into the soil, and avoid using them right at the base of young transplants until those plants are established.

### Will grindings left in the hole hurt my grass?

Yes, if they are still mixed through the top layer of soil. As the wood breaks down, it ties up nitrogen that grass roots need, which typically shows up as yellow or stunted growth right over the old stump. Clearing the grindings out and filling with topsoil avoids the problem.

### How long should I wait before planting grass over a ground stump?

Clear the shavings, fill with topsoil, and let the area settle for a few weeks with regular watering before seeding or laying sod. Check that the patch is level and firm, not sunken, before you plant. Deeper holes can keep settling for months, so a second top-off is common.

### Does stump grinding include hauling away the grindings?

It depends on the company and sometimes the specific job. Some quotes include clearing the material, others leave it on-site unless you ask for hauling as an add-on. Confirm this detail when you get your quote rather than assuming either way.

### Can stump grindings be composted instead of hauled to the landfill?

Fresh wood shavings can go into a compost pile or a municipal composting program in many areas, which keeps them out of the trash while avoiding the nitrogen problem that comes with digging them straight into garden soil. Check what your local yard waste program accepts.

### What if the hole keeps sinking after I fill it?

That is normal for the first several months as loose fill and any remaining wood fiber compact and settle. Keep an eye on it and add more topsoil as needed until it holds level with the surrounding grade before you consider the job finished.
