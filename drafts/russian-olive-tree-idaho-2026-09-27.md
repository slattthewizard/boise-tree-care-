# Russian Olive Trees in Idaho: Thorns, Spread and Getting Rid of One for Good

If you have a Russian olive on your property, the two things you already know are the thorns and how fast it spreads. Cutting it down is the easy part. Keeping it from coming back from the stump and the roots is where most DIY attempts fail, and why the tree keeps showing up along canals, ditch banks and fence lines all over the valley.

This guide covers how to identify a Russian olive, why it spreads so aggressively here, what actually works to kill one, and what removal typically costs.

## What a Russian Olive Actually Looks Like

Russian olive (Elaeagnus angustifolia) is a small to medium tree, usually 15 to 30 feet tall, with narrow silvery-gray leaves and rough, dark, often peeling bark. In late spring it produces small, fragrant yellow flowers, followed by clusters of dusty-yellow, olive-shaped fruit in late summer and fall. The branches carry stiff woody thorns, often an inch or two long, which is usually the detail that makes someone stop and look the tree up in the first place.

It was planted on purpose for decades. Nurseries sold it as a fast, tough windbreak and screening tree that tolerated poor, salty, compacted soil, which is exactly the kind of soil common along Treasure Valley canals and ditch banks. That same toughness is why it has spread well beyond where anyone planted it.

## Why It Shows Up Along Canals and Ditches

Russian olive is especially suited to the seasonally wet, disturbed ground along waterways, according to [University of Nevada Reno Extension](https://extension.unr.edu/publication.aspx?PubID=3949), which is why you see so many growing wild along canal and lateral banks, irrigation ditches and low spots that flood in spring. A dense stand can crowd a ditch bank, narrow the channel and make it harder for a canal company crew to get equipment in for maintenance. Add the thorns and the fact that it tolerates the salty, compacted soil in those spots better than most native species, and it is easy to see why it outcompetes what used to grow there.

## How Russian Olive Spreads

Two things keep this tree ahead of anyone trying to control it, and neither one is obvious until you know to look for it.

The first is birds. University of Nevada Reno Extension notes that birds and other animals eat the olive-shaped fruit and carry the seed to new ground, which is how a single planted tree turns into a scattered population along a creek, canal or fence row within a few years. There is no fence or barrier that stops that.

The second is resprouting. [Ohio State University Extension](https://cfaes-contenthub.org.ohio-state.edu/fact-sheet/controlling-non-native-invasive-plants-ohios-forests-autumn-olive-and-russian-olive) warns that simply cutting a Russian olive off at the base "will cause prolific sprouting and increase the number of stems" rather than killing it. University of Nevada Reno Extension adds that regrowth from the crown or from underground lateral roots is common in cut trees. If you have ever cut one back and watched it return thicker the next year, with a ring of new shoots ringing the base, that is exactly why.

That resprouting habit is the same pattern we cover in our article on [tree suckers and root sprouts](/blog/tree-suckers-root-sprouts/): a stressed or cut root system throws up new growth as a survival response, and mowing or hand-pulling the sprouts alone rarely keeps up with it.

## Getting Rid of a Russian Olive for Good

Because cutting alone tends to backfire, an effective removal usually has three parts:

1. **Cut the tree down.** For a mature Russian olive this means a controlled, sectioned [tree removal](/tree-removal-boise/), especially with thorny branches to work around and often a fence, ditch or driveway nearby limiting where wood can safely come down.
2. **Treat or grind the stump.** A cut stump left untreated will usually resprout. Options are a stump treatment at the time of cutting or grinding the stump out below grade so there is no live wood left to push new shoots. See our page on [stump grinding](/stump-grinding-boise/) for how that process works.
3. **Watch for suckers the following season.** Even a well-handled removal can throw a few root suckers the next spring, particularly if any roots were left in the ground. Catching them early and cutting them back keeps the tree from reestablishing.

If several Russian olives are growing along the same stretch of ditch or fence line, plan on treating them as a group. Removing one and leaving three thorny neighbors standing just means the seed source is still right there.

## Is Russian Olive Actually Illegal to Keep in Idaho?

Not currently. Russian olive does not appear on [Idaho's official noxious weed list](https://invasivespecies.idaho.gov/plants), which is different from several neighboring states that do regulate it. That does not mean it is harmless, just that no state law forces you to remove one. [The City of Boise](https://www.cityofboise.org/departments/parks-and-recreation/open-space/weed-warriors/resources/) lists it among the non-native species its Foothills restoration volunteers work to control, and conservation and extension sources consistently describe it as an aggressive species that displaces native cottonwoods and willows along waterways. Some local irrigation or canal companies also have their own rules about woody growth on their right-of-way, separate from state weed law, which is another reason to check with the canal company before cutting anything near a ditch bank.

## Should You Just Leave It Alone?

If the tree is small, isolated, not near a structure, fence or waterway, and you are not bothered by the thorns, there is no rule forcing you to remove it. The case for removing it gets stronger when it is spreading into new ground each year, crowding a ditch bank, growing thorns within reach of a walkway, or you are trying to establish other landscaping nearby that it keeps shading or crowding out with root competition.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I tell a Russian olive from a native tree?

Look for narrow, silvery-gray leaves with a dusty, almost metallic sheen on both sides, rough dark bark, stiff thorns along the branches, and small yellow olive-shaped fruit in late summer. Native cottonwoods and willows in the same wet areas have broader green leaves and no thorns.

### Will cutting a Russian olive down kill it?

No, not by itself. Cutting the trunk without treating or grinding the stump usually triggers a burst of new shoots from the base and roots, leaving you with a bushier, multi-stemmed tree instead of a dead one.

### Is Russian olive a noxious weed in Idaho?

It is not currently on Idaho's official state noxious weed list, unlike some neighboring states. It is still widely regarded as an invasive, non-native species by conservation and extension sources, and some local canal or irrigation companies may restrict woody growth on their right-of-way separately from state weed law.

### Can I remove a Russian olive growing along a canal or ditch myself?

You can, but check who actually controls that strip of ground first. Canal, lateral and irrigation easements are often owned or managed separately from the adjoining property, and cutting trees on that ground without checking can create a dispute even if the tree itself is a nuisance species.

### Why does my Russian olive keep sending up new shoots after I cut it?

That is root and crown resprouting, a normal survival response in a tree that has been cut or stressed. It usually means the stump and root system are still alive. Grinding the stump or treating it at the time of cutting is what stops the regrowth.
