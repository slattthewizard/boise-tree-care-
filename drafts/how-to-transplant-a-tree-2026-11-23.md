# How to Transplant a Tree (and When Not To)

To transplant a tree, dig it up while it's dormant (early spring or after leaf drop in fall) with as much of the root ball as you can keep intact, replant it quickly at the same depth it grew before, and water it carefully for the next couple of years. That works well for small trees with trunks around 2 inches thick or less. Past that size, the root ball gets heavy enough that moving it is a job for equipment and a crew, and the honest answer for a big established tree is usually to leave it where it is or replace it.

This guide covers what's realistic for a homeowner, how to time the move in a cold-winter, dry-summer climate like the Treasure Valley, and what to do before, during and after the dig.

## Which trees you can realistically move

Size is the first question, and it decides everything else. Most of a tree's absorbing roots are fine, fibrous roots far out from the trunk, and a spade only captures a fraction of them. The bigger the tree, the larger the share of its root system you leave behind in the ground.

[Iowa State University Extension](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/how-transplant-small-trees) tells home gardeners to move only trees with a trunk diameter of 2 inches or less, measured 6 inches above the ground, and to hand larger trees to a landscape contractor or nursery professional. Its companion [transplanting guide](https://naturalresources.extension.iastate.edu/encyclopedia/transplanting-trees-and-shrubs) adds that root balls over 24 inches across generally need mechanical equipment. A ball that size is a heavy lump of soil, which is why we'd call it the practical limit for two people with a tarp and a wheelbarrow.

Under an inch is easy. Between 1 and 2 inches is doable with a helper and some planning. Over 2 inches, hire it out or skip the move. Anything already stressed or diseased is a poor bet at any size.

## Best time to transplant in a cold-winter, dry-summer climate

Move trees while they're dormant. Iowa State's guide says early spring is generally the best time, with fall as the second window: hardwoods after they drop their leaves, and conifers from late August through September.

For the Boise area, spring has one big advantage and one big catch. The advantage is a full growing season to rebuild roots before winter. The catch is that spring can turn hot and dry fast, and a tree with a chopped-up root system can't keep up with a 90-degree afternoon. Fall moves get the opposite deal: cool soil and less water demand, but less time before the ground freezes. Our guide to the [best time to plant trees in Boise](/blog/best-time-plant-trees-boise/) walks through that tradeoff in more detail, and the same logic applies to a move.

Whichever season you pick, avoid summer and frozen ground.

## Root prune the tree ahead of the move

If you can plan ahead, root pruning is the single best thing you can do for a tree you're about to move. You cut the roots in a circle around the trunk months before the move so the tree grows a dense cluster of new feeder roots inside the future root ball, instead of losing them all at once.

[Clemson Cooperative Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/transplanting-established-trees-shrubs/) gives a simple schedule: if you plan to move in fall, root prune in March; if you plan to move in spring, root prune in October. It also warns that root pruning should only be done after deciduous leaves drop in fall or before bud break in spring, because doing it at other times can badly damage the plant.

To root prune:

1. Mark a circle the size of the root ball you'll eventually dig (see the sizing table below).
2. Cut straight down along that line with a sharp spade. Iowa State suggests going 9 to 12 inches deep and using loppers on larger roots, since spade cuts can loosen the ball.
3. Backfill the trench with the soil you removed and water it in.

Then leave the tree alone.

## Root ball size: how big is big enough

The ball has to be large enough to hold most of the fibrous roots, and bigger trunks need much bigger balls. Iowa State's minimum sizes look like this:

| Trunk diameter (caliper) | Minimum tree height | Minimum ball diameter |
|---|---|---|
| 1/2 inch | 3 ft | 12 in |
| 1 inch | 5 ft | 16 in |
| 1 1/2 inches | 6 ft | 20 in |
| 2 inches | 7 ft | 24 in |
| 3 inches | 10 ft | 32 in |

Iowa State says conical and broadleaf evergreens need a ball at least one size larger than this. Clemson's table runs a little bigger for shade trees (a 2-inch tree gets a 28-inch ball about 19 inches deep), so when you're unsure, go bigger. A ball that's too small is the most common reason small-tree transplants fail.

## Digging and moving the tree

Dry soil crumbles off roots, so if the ground is dry, water the tree deeply three or four days before you dig.

1. Mark the circle on the ground at the size you chose and cut straight down around it.
2. Dig a trench outside the line, working down to about three-quarters of the ball depth. Iowa State describes digging 1 1/2 to 2 feet deep for small trees.
3. Undercut the ball at an angle with a sharp spade, cutting through any roots underneath.
4. Tip the ball onto a square of burlap or a tarp, wrap it tight, and tie it off. Don't lift a tree by its trunk, ever.
5. Slide the wrapped ball onto a tarp or into a wheelbarrow and move it. Keep the ball intact and out of the sun and wind.

Have the new hole ready before you start digging, so the ball isn't drying out while you finish it.

## Replant at the right depth

The new hole should be 2 to 3 times the width of the root ball, but no deeper than the ball is tall. Iowa State says the hole depth should be about 1 to 2 inches less than the height of the soil ball, so the tree sits slightly high rather than slightly low.

Set the tree on undisturbed soil so it can't settle. The root flare, the place where the trunk widens as the roots begin, should be at or just above the surrounding soil. [Utah State University Extension](https://extension.usu.edu/yardandgarden/research/planting-landscape-trees) notes that a buried root collar can decay and encourage girdling roots. Clemson says the same thing in plain numbers: the collar should sit no more than an inch below the soil surface.

Planting too deep is the mistake we'd bet on if a transplant dies. Our article on [girdling roots and how to fix them](/blog/girdling-roots-fix/) explains what happens to a tree whose flare has been buried for years.

Backfill with the native soil you dug out, not a bag of potting mix. Remove the burlap from the top and sides of the ball as far as you can, and cut off any twine or wire around the trunk. Then water thoroughly to settle the soil and push out air pockets.

## Aftercare: watering, mulch, staking and pruning

Plan on a couple of seasons of attention.

**Water.** Utah State says to water thoroughly at planting, because so much of the root system is lost in the move, and to keep the soil about a foot down moist, watering every two to four days through the first summer. That's a starting point, not a schedule to follow blindly. Push a screwdriver or your finger into the soil near the ball. If it's dry a few inches down, water. If it's soggy, wait. Our [tree watering guide for Boise](/blog/how-to-water-trees-boise/) covers frequency and how long to run a hose.

**Mulch.** Spread 3 to 4 inches of wood chips over the planting area, kept a couple of inches back from the bark, per Utah State. See [how to mulch around trees](/blog/how-to-mulch-around-trees/) for the details, including why mulch volcanoes do damage.

**Staking.** Most small transplants don't need it. If wind is a problem and the ball rocks, use soft straps and loosen them as the tree settles. Our guide to [staking new trees](/blog/tree-staking-new-trees/) shows how, and when to take them off.

**Pruning.** Take out only dead or broken branches at first. Pruning heavily to "balance" the roots is an old habit that removes the leaves the tree needs to rebuild them.

## Signs of transplant shock

Even a perfect move sets the tree back. [Purdue Extension](https://www.extension.purdue.edu/extmedia/bp/bp-31.html) lists the usual signs: wilting, leaf rolling, yellowing or bronzing between the veins or along leaf edges, smaller leaves, shorter new shoots, and in worse cases dieback. Purdue also says reduced growth in the first year is common and that symptoms can last two years or more.

Don't panic over slow growth or a few scorched leaves. Check the basics first: soil too dry or too wet, planted too deep, anything tied around the trunk. A tree with no green under the bark on any branch is a different story.

## When the tree is too big to move

If the trunk is well over 2 inches thick, the options are to keep the tree where it is, pay a crew with a tree spade to move it, or take it out and plant something new. For most big shade trees we'd lean toward the last one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How big of a tree can I transplant myself?

Iowa State Extension advises home gardeners to move only trees with a trunk diameter of 2 inches or less, measured 6 inches above the ground. Above that, the root ball is heavy enough that equipment is usually needed, and the share of roots you can keep gets smaller. For anything bigger, hire a professional or consider replacing the tree.

### What is the best month to transplant a tree?

Early spring, while the tree is still dormant and before buds open, is generally the best window. Fall after leaf drop is the second choice. In the Boise area, avoid mid-summer heat and frozen ground. For evergreens, Iowa State's fall window is late August through September.

### How big should the root ball be?

It depends on trunk diameter. Iowa State lists a 16-inch ball for a 1-inch tree and a 24-inch ball for a 2-inch tree. Evergreens need a ball one size larger. If in doubt, go bigger, because a ball that's too small cuts off too many feeder roots and is the usual reason a transplant fails.

### How often should I water a transplanted tree?

Water deeply at planting, then keep the soil about a foot down moist. Utah State Extension suggests watering every two to four days through the first summer. Adjust for your soil and weather: check the soil before each watering, and cut back if the ground stays soggy. Most trees need extra water for the first two years.

### How long does transplant shock last?

Purdue Extension says reduced growth in the first year is common and symptoms can last two years or more. Wilting, scorched edges, smaller leaves and shorter shoots are typical. Keep the watering steady, avoid fertilizing and heavy pruning, and give the tree time. If branches show no green under the bark, it may not recover.

### Can I move a mature tree?

Usually not as a DIY job. Large trees need a crew with a tree spade or crane, and survival still isn't guaranteed. For most big shade trees, removing and replanting a young tree is the more reliable route.
