# Ginkgo Tree: What to Know Before You Plant One (or Live Under One)

A ginkgo tree is one of the toughest shade trees you can put in a Treasure Valley yard: it handles heat, compacted soil and city conditions, it rarely has pest trouble, and it turns a clean gold in fall. The catch is that ginkgoes are either male or female, and only the females drop the fleshy, foul-smelling fruit. If you are buying one, buy a named male cultivar. If you already have a female, you can live with it or replace it, but there is no reliable way to make it stop.

Most of the rest of the story is good news, with a couple of quirks (slow early growth and an all-at-once leaf drop) that surprise people who have not grown one before.

## How to identify a ginkgo

Ginkgo leaves are the giveaway. They are fan-shaped, usually with a notch in the middle of the outer edge, and they grow on short spur shoots along the branches in tight clusters. No other common landscape tree has a leaf like it. The bark on older trees is gray and deeply ridged, and young trees often look sparse and a little awkward, with few branches and an open, lanky shape.

Mature trees are big. According to [Oregon State University's Landscape Plants database](https://landscapeplants.oregonstate.edu/plants/ginkgo-biloba), typical trees reach 50 feet or more, with a pyramidal shape, and male cultivars vary quite a bit in form, from roughly 40 by 30 feet for 'Autumn Gold' to about 60 by 20 feet for 'Princeton Sentry'. If you are planting near a house, driveway or overhead lines, the narrow selections are worth a look. ## Female ginkgo tree smell: what it is and why it matters

Ginkgo is dioecious, which means individual trees are male or female. Males make small pollen structures. Females make plum-sized seeds with a fleshy outer layer, and that outer layer is the problem. Oregon State describes the ripe female fruit as extremely messy and malodorous, and [North Carolina State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ginkgo-biloba/) notes that the seeds from female trees have foul-smelling flesh, which is why grafted male trees are usually preferred for landscapes.

Honestly, the smell is the reason ginkgoes have a reputation. In fall the fruit drops, gets stepped on, tracks onto walkways and decays on pavement, and a mature female over a driveway or sidewalk can make the whole area unpleasant for weeks. It is a bigger deal on a small lot or over a patio than in a large yard where the fruit lands on lawn or mulch.

## Male vs female ginkgo: how to avoid buying a female

You want a male. The reliable way to get one is to buy a named male cultivar that was propagated by grafting or cuttings from a known male tree. North Carolina State notes that most ornamental ginkgoes sold in the U.S. are male for exactly this reason, and Oregon State says seedless male cultivars are sold commercially. Names you will see on nursery tags include 'Autumn Gold', 'Princeton Sentry' and 'Magyar'.

What does not work is buying an unnamed seedling and hoping. A young ginkgo shows no outward sign of its sex, and seedlings can take many years to reveal it. In one [Ask Extension question](https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=841981), a homeowner's ginkgo of roughly 25 years had only just fruited for the first time, and the extension expert's advice was to plant a male tree. That is the risk in a nutshell: you can have a tree for a couple of decades before you learn which kind it is.

A few practical habits help:

- Look for the cultivar name on the tag and keep the tag or a photo of it.
- Ask the nursery whether the tree is a grafted or cutting-grown male selection, not just "ginkgo."
- Be a little skeptical of a bargain ginkgo with no cultivar name.

## Ginkgo tree growth rate

Ginkgoes are slow to get going. Expect a sparse, modest-looking young tree for the first several years while the roots get established, then steadier growth after that. North Carolina State rates the overall growth rate as medium, and the early years are the slow part, so treat it as a tree you plant for your future shade more than for next summer's. We would not choose a ginkgo if you need quick shade on a bare new-construction lot, and the [fast-growing trees in this guide](/blog/fast-growing-privacy-trees-boise/) are a better fit for that.

The upside of the slow start is a sturdy, long-lived tree. Water it deeply and regularly through the first couple of summers, mulch the root zone, and be patient.

## Urban conditions, pests and problems

This is where ginkgo earns its place. North Carolina State calls it an excellent choice for urban settings, tolerant of drought, heat, air pollution and moderate salt, and it lists no insect or disease problems for the species. Oregon State calls it durable for difficult sites and notes that it transplants easily, and that it is hardy to USDA Zone 3, which comfortably covers a Boise winter.

That makes it a sensible candidate for a parking strip or a tight, hot spot where other trees struggle. If the tree is in the planting strip between the sidewalk and the street, check who is responsible for it first. ## Fall color and the sudden leaf drop

The fall display is the other reason people plant them. Oregon State describes the color as usually bright yellow to gold, and says the tree often drops most of its leaves in a short window, sometimes overnight, leaving a yellow carpet underneath. If you walk out one morning and find a bare ginkgo standing in a ring of gold leaves, nothing is wrong. That is normal behavior for the species.

The practical side is that all the leaves come down at once, so cleanup is a single big raking job instead of weeks of small ones.

## Pruning a ginkgo

Ginkgoes need very little pruning. Their branching is open and fairly orderly, and the main job in the early years is to select a single leader and a few well-spaced branches. Our article on [structural pruning for young trees](/blog/structural-pruning-young-trees/) walks through how to do that, and it applies to a ginkgo as much as to anything else.

After that, remove dead, broken or crossing branches and leave the rest alone. Heavy cuts are not necessary and do not suit the tree's natural shape. ## What to do about an existing female ginkgo

If you have inherited a female, you have three realistic choices.

**Live with it.** Rake or sweep the fruit up as it drops, ideally with gloves, and bag it for the trash before it gets stepped on. The mess is seasonal, and a tree over lawn or planting beds is far easier than one over concrete. Some people also find the fruit can irritate skin, and North Carolina State notes the species may cause contact dermatitis, so gloves are a good idea.

**Do not count on changing its sex.** There is no spray, pruning cut or treatment that stops a female from fruiting. An Ask Extension expert answering a question about a seeding ginkgo said you cannot stop the seeding once it has started and suggested living with it or replacing it with a certified male cultivar.

**Replace it.** If the fruit is over a driveway, patio or walkway, or the smell has made the yard unusable for part of the year, removal and replacement with a named male is a reasonable call. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do all ginkgo trees smell bad?

No. Only female ginkgoes make the fleshy fruit that smells when it drops and breaks down. Male trees produce pollen instead and have no fruit, which is why named male cultivars are the standard recommendation for yards and streets. The leaves and bark do not have the odor.

### Can you tell if a young ginkgo is male or female?

Not reliably. A young tree shows no outward sign, and seedlings may take many years to flower or fruit, so the answer can wait decades. The dependable approach is to buy a named male cultivar that was grafted or grown from cuttings, and to keep the tag.

### How fast does a ginkgo tree grow?

Slowly for the first several years, then at a moderate pace once the roots are established. North Carolina State Extension rates the growth rate as medium overall. Plan on a modest young tree for a while, and keep it watered and mulched during its first summers to help it along.

### Why did my ginkgo drop all its leaves at once?

That is normal for the species. Ginkgoes turn gold and often shed most of their leaves within a short window in fall, sometimes overnight. It is not a sign of disease. If the leaves drop in summer while still green, that points to a stress problem instead.

### Do ginkgo trees need much pruning?

No. They have a naturally open, orderly structure, so the main work is choosing a leader and good branch spacing while the tree is young. After that, take out dead, broken or crossing branches as needed and skip heavy cuts. Large or high limbs on a mature tree are a job for a professional.

### Can I stop a female ginkgo from making fruit?

There is no reliable treatment that stops a female from fruiting. Extension guidance says you either live with the fruit, cleaning it up as it falls, or replace the tree with a named male cultivar. If the fruit is over a driveway or patio, replacement is often the cleaner long-term answer.
