# Galls on Trees: Oak Apples, Leaf Bumps and Knobs on Branches

Those round bumps on your oak leaves, or the knobby growths along a twig, are almost always galls, and almost always harmless. A gall forms when a tiny insect, mite, or the tree's own reaction to one triggers extra plant tissue to grow around it, kind of like a scar with a purpose. Most galls are a cosmetic issue, not a health threat, and spraying does little to stop them once you can see them.

There are a couple of exceptions worth knowing, especially a soil-level growth called crown gall, which is a different problem with a different cause. This guide walks through what galls actually are, the types you are most likely to spot on trees in the Treasure Valley, and when a gall is actually worth a call instead of a shrug.

## What Is a Gall, Exactly?

A gall is abnormal plant growth that forms in response to an organism feeding or laying eggs in the tissue. Oak trees carry the most kinds of galls of any tree group, caused mostly by tiny wasps called cynipid gall wasps, but mites, aphids, and midges cause galls on other species too. The insect's egg-laying triggers growth-regulating chemicals that make the plant build a chamber of tissue around the invader, almost like a cocoon.

The gall maker lives inside, feeding on the gall tissue itself, safe from most predators and weather. It is a one-sided relationship: the insect benefits, and the tree pays a small tissue cost, but a healthy tree can carry dozens of galls without any real damage.

## Common Types of Tree Galls to Recognize

Galls come in a wide range of shapes, sizes, and colors depending on the species involved and the part of the tree affected. Not every type below shows up equally often in Idaho yards, but these are the ones homeowners ask about most.

| Gall type | Where it shows up | Typical look |
|---|---|---|
| Oak apple gall | Oak leaves, attached by a thin stalk | Round, tan to reddish, 1-2 inches, papery or spongy inside |
| Oak leaf blister/vein gall | Oak leaf surface or veins | Small raised bumps or blisters, green turning brown |
| Gouty or horned oak twig gall | Oak twigs and small branches | Hard woody swellings, can circle the twig |
| Woolly aphid gall | Cottonwood, poplar leaf stems | Cottony or fuzzy pouch-shaped swelling |
| Bladder or spindle gall | Maple leaves | Small red or yellow-green pointed bumps on the leaf surface |
| Willow pinecone gall | Willow twig tips | Layered, resembles a small pinecone |

If you are not sure which kind you are looking at, a photo is usually enough for a quick read over the phone; you do not need to identify the exact species to know whether it is a problem.

## Are Galls Bad for the Tree?

For the vast majority of cases, no. Galls are a cosmetic issue, and [NC State Extension notes that oak apple galls in particular do not typically harm the tree](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/oak-apple-galls), so management is often unwarranted. A tree with a heavy year for oak apple galls or leaf blister galls will look rough for a season, especially if a branch or two drops extra leaves, but the tree itself keeps growing normally. Gall makers are specialists that evolved alongside their host tree; killing the tree would kill their home too, so most have no interest in doing real damage.

The exceptions are galls that form in large numbers on young or already stressed trees, or galls that circle and girdle a twig tightly enough to cut off water and nutrient flow past that point. Twig dieback from a heavy gall load is the main thing to watch for, and it is still usually limited to a few twig tips rather than the whole tree.

## Crown Gall: The One Type Actually Worth Watching

Crown gall is different from the insect galls above. It is caused by a soil bacterium, *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*, that enters through wounds near the base of the trunk or on roots, often at a graft union on fruit trees, roses, and some ornamentals. Instead of a leaf bump, it produces a rough, woody, tumor-like swelling right at or just below the soil line.

Crown gall can interfere with water and nutrient movement in young or newly planted trees, especially if the gall wraps most of the way around the trunk base. Established, healthy trees usually tolerate a crown gall without much trouble, but a young tree with a large crown gall is worth having looked at. [UC IPM's guidance on crown gall](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/crown-gall/) is clear that spraying does not treat it; management is about prevention, healthy planting practices, and physically removing badly affected wood, not a chemical fix.

## Do You Need to Spray for Galls?

No, and spraying an oak or maple that already has visible galls will not remove them. By the time you can see a gall, the insect is already sealed inside plant tissue, protected from any contact spray. Penn State Extension's guidance on oak galls is direct on this: [chemical control is seldom recommended](https://extension.psu.edu/galls-on-oak), since insecticide timing has to hit the adult insect before egg-laying, a narrow window that is not practical for a homeowner to catch.

For nearly every gall situation on a home landscape tree, the right move is simply to leave it alone and let the tree carry it through the season.

## When Pruning Out Galls Makes Sense

Pruning is worth considering in a few specific situations:

- A young or ornamental tree where the galls are heavy enough to look bad and the tree is small enough that removing a few twigs will not set it back
- Twig or branch galls that are visibly girdling and killing the tip beyond them
- Gall-heavy twigs on a tree you are already pruning for shape or size

Cut back to healthy wood below the gall, ideally during dormant season for most deciduous species, and dispose of the trimmings rather than leaving them piled at the base of the tree. If you are not sure how to make a clean cut without stressing the tree, our guide on [how to prune a tree properly](/blog/how-to-prune-a-tree/) covers the basics. On a large, established shade tree, chasing every gall with pruning cuts usually does more harm than the galls themselves; [routine trimming](/tree-trimming-boise/) on a normal schedule is enough to keep a tree looking good without turning it into a project.

## Galls vs. Other Things That Look Similar

A few other issues get mistaken for galls:

- Insect egg masses or scale insects sit on the surface rather than growing out of the tissue, and usually look more like a crust or cluster than a smooth bump.
- Moss and lichen grow on the bark surface rather than out of it, and are usually [harmless in their own right](/blog/moss-lichen-tree-bark/), just a sign of a slow-growing or shaded branch.
- Fungal cankers are sunken, cracked, or discolored areas rather than raised, rounded growths, and are more often a genuine health concern. If you are seeing dieback alongside odd bark texture, it is worth reading up on [common tree diseases in the Boise area](/blog/tree-diseases-boise/) to see whether the symptoms match.
- Chewed leaves, sticky honeydew, or sawdust-like frass point to a different pest than a gall maker; our rundown of [common tree pests in Boise](/blog/tree-pests-boise/) covers the usual suspects.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What causes the round balls on my oak tree's leaves?

Those are almost certainly oak apple galls, caused by a tiny cynipid wasp laying an egg in the leaf tissue. The tree grows a round, papery chamber around the developing larva. They look dramatic but rarely hurt the tree, and no treatment is needed.

### Will galls kill my tree?

Almost never on a healthy, established tree. Galls are mostly cosmetic, and even a heavy gall year usually costs the tree a bit of leaf area or a few twig tips at most. The exception is a young or already stressed tree with a heavy gall load or a girdling twig gall, which is worth having checked.

### Should I spray my tree to get rid of galls?

No. Once a gall is visible, the insect inside is already sealed off from any surface spray, so treating an established gall does nothing. Timing a spray to catch the adult insect before it lays eggs is difficult for a homeowner to hit and is not worthwhile for typical cosmetic galls.

### Is crown gall the same thing as the bumps on my oak leaves?

No. Leaf and twig galls on oaks are caused by insects and mites and are usually harmless. Crown gall is a bacterial disease that forms a woody swelling at the base of the trunk or on roots, most often on fruit trees or at a graft union, and is worth a look, especially on a young tree.

### Can I just cut off branches with galls?

On a small or ornamental tree, yes, pruning out a few galled twigs during dormant season is a reasonable way to clean up the look. On a large shade tree, it is usually not worth chasing every gall, and normal seasonal trimming is enough.

### Do galls attract other pests to my tree?

Not typically. Gall makers are specialists that feed only on the gall tissue they create, and the gall itself does not usually draw in unrelated insects. If you are seeing separate signs of chewing, sticky residue, or boring damage, that points to a different pest worth identifying on its own.
