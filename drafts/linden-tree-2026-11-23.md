# Linden Tree: Varieties, Size, Pros, and Problems

A linden tree is a medium-to-large shade tree with heart-shaped leaves, a tidy crown, and clusters of fragrant yellow flowers in early summer. Lindens are a common street and park tree because they're tough, adaptable, and good-looking. The trade-offs are sticky aphid honeydew, a few structural quirks, and a size that surprises people who plant one too close to the house.

"Linden" and "basswood" are mostly the same group of trees (genus Tilia). Which one you have changes how big it gets and what to watch for, so it's worth knowing the main types.

## Common Types of Linden Tree

Three lindens turn up most often in yards and along streets here.

**Littleleaf linden (Tilia cordata).** This is the one most people mean. The [Morton Arboretum](https://mortonarb.org/plant-and-protect/trees-and-plants/little-leaved-linden/) lists it at 60 to 70 feet tall and 30 to 40 feet wide, with heart-shaped leaves 1.5 to 3 inches long that turn clear yellow in fall. [Utah State University Extension](https://extension.usu.edu/treebrowser/catalog/linden-littleaf-european) calls it quite popular throughout Utah and the U.S., with a pyramidal crown and high tolerance of alkaline soil. Cultivars like 'Greenspire' are common nursery picks.

**American linden or basswood (Tilia americana).** Same family, bigger leaves. Utah State says the leaves run 5 to 8 inches long, much larger than littleleaf, and that the species is free of most serious pests. Utah State's author also prefers the species over the commonly planted 'Redmond' cultivar, noting stronger branch attachment and a wider canopy.

**Silver linden (Tilia tomentosa).** Leaves have silvery hairs underneath. Utah State's silver linden entry says it's more heat and drought tolerant than other lindens and "should be planted more in Utah."

If you're not sure which one you have, check the leaf. Small, glossy, rounded leaves mean littleleaf. Dinner-saucer leaves mean American. Silvery undersides mean silver.

## Why Lindens Are Such a Common Street and Shade Tree

They handle the things city sites throw at a tree. Morton describes littleleaf linden as tolerant of pollution, Utah State rates both littleleaf and American linden high for alkaline soil (useful here, where our soils run high in pH), and transplanting success is rated high. The form is neat and symmetrical, the shade is dense, and the flowers are a bonus.

There's a catch worth knowing. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/tilia-cordata/) says littleleaf linden has often been planted inappropriately as a street tree, which can lead to stunted growth, leaf scorch and windthrow. Utah State rates salt tolerance low and says littleleaf doesn't do well with intense sun, heat or drought. In Boise, that means a linden jammed into a narrow strip with no irrigation will struggle. If it's a city-managed tree in the planting strip, see our guide on [who owns and maintains street trees in Boise](/blog/street-trees-boise-who-owns/) before you do anything to it.

## How Big Does a Linden Get?

Plan on a big tree. Littleleaf runs 60 to 70 feet tall per Morton and Utah State, at a medium growth rate. NC State lists a spread of 35 to 50 feet. American linden is rated high for mature height on the Utah State page. Plant one at least a full canopy radius from the house, driveway and overhead lines, because pruning a linden to hold a size it was never going to hold is a recurring chore.

## The Upsides

- Dense shade that cools a house in summer.
- Fragrant creamy-yellow flowers, usually in late June to early July in Utah State's description. NC State says the nectar-rich blooms attract bees.
- Clean yellow fall color on littleleaf.
- Tolerates our alkaline soil and, once established, doesn't need much fuss.

## Linden Tree Problems

### Aphids and sticky honeydew

For most linden owners, this is the big complaint. Oregon State's [aphids on trees](https://solvepestproblems.oregonstate.edu/plant-problems/aphids-trees) guide says linden trees attract aphids and other sucking insects, which excrete honeydew, a sticky sugar-water that drips onto pavement, cars and furniture below, and that sooty mold grows on it. Utah State lists aphids as a problem on both littleleaf and American linden.

Honeydew is a mess more than a threat. Oregon State says you can leave aphids alone if the honeydew and sooty mold are tolerable, though high populations can cause wilting or shoot dieback. A strong stream of water on the leaves is its recommended low-risk first step, and if you do use an insecticide, it advises early-season timing, never during bloom, because it can harm pollinators. Spraying a large tree is also difficult, and the page suggests leaving it to a professional applicator. Always follow the product label.

If sticky drips on your car are the issue and you're not sure what's causing them, our article on [sap dripping from trees](/blog/sap-dripping-from-tree/) explains how to tell honeydew from real sap and slime flux.

### Japanese beetles

Lindens are a favorite food of Japanese beetles, and Morton lists them alongside aphids as potential problems. We cover the signs, timing and what's worth doing in our article on [Japanese beetles and Boise trees](/blog/japanese-beetle-boise-trees/), so we won't repeat it here.

### Suckers at the base

Lindens, American linden especially, tend to send up shoots from the base of the trunk. Utah State says suckers can be a problem on American linden. Left alone, they turn into a clump of thin stems competing with the main trunk. Cut them off at the point they grow from, not partway up, and do it every year. Our guide to [tree suckers and root sprouts](/blog/tree-suckers-root-sprouts/) covers cutting technique and why they keep coming back.

### Weak branch attachment

Utah State notes that some littleleaf cultivars have weak branch attachment, and that's a good argument for pruning a linden while it's young. Two stems of equal size competing at a fork is the classic trouble spot. Our guide to [structural pruning for young trees](/blog/structural-pruning-young-trees/) walks through choosing a single leader and spacing the main branches, which is far cheaper and easier at 8 feet than at 40.

## Pruning and Maintenance

On a young linden, the work is light and early: pick a dominant leader, remove competing stems and suckers, and keep low branches only as long as you need them. On a mature tree, plan on occasional dead-wood and crossing-branch removal and a periodic check of the unions. Lindens are tidy enough that they rarely need heavy cuts, and heavy cuts are what you want to avoid anyway. NC State says littleleaf can tolerate heavy pruning, but that's a statement about survival, not about what makes a good-looking, safe tree.

Linden flowers draw a lot of bees while they're open, and that's normal foraging, not a swarm or a nest. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between a linden and a basswood?

They're the same genus, Tilia. "Basswood" usually means American linden (Tilia americana), the big-leaved native species, while "linden" is often used for the European types like littleleaf. Utah State lists American linden leaves at 5 to 8 inches long, versus 1.5 to 3 inches for littleleaf.

### How tall does a littleleaf linden get?

Morton and Utah State both put it at 60 to 70 feet tall, with a spread around 30 to 40 feet per Morton and up to about 50 feet per NC State. It grows at a medium rate, so it doesn't shoot up overnight, but it does end up large. Give it room.

### Why is my linden tree sticky underneath?

Almost certainly aphid honeydew. Oregon State explains that linden aphids excrete a sticky sugar-water that drips on surfaces below, and sooty mold often follows. It's a nuisance rather than a death sentence. A water spray on the foliage is a good low-risk start.

### Are linden trees a good choice for Boise?

They can be, with a decent site. Utah State rates littleleaf and American linden high for alkaline soil, but notes littleleaf dislikes intense sun, heat and drought, and has low salt tolerance. Plant where it can be irrigated and has room. Silver linden is the more heat- and drought-tolerant choice per Utah State.

### Why does my linden keep growing shoots from the base?

That's suckering, and it's common on American linden. Utah State lists suckers as a possible problem. Cut them off at the base each year. Don't leave them, since they'll turn the tree into a multi-stem clump over time.

### Can a linden be pruned heavily?

It survives it, but we wouldn't. NC State says littleleaf tolerates heavy pruning, yet topping or hard cutbacks leave big wounds and weak regrowth. A better approach is light structural pruning while the tree is young and selective cuts on a mature one.
