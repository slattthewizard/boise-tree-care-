# Catalpa Trees: Big Leaves, Long Pods and What Owners Should Know

If you have a catalpa tree, you already know it does not look like anything else in the yard. Heart-shaped leaves the size of dinner plates, orchid-like white flower clusters in late spring, and long bean-like pods that hang on well into winter. It is also one of the messier, weaker-wooded trees you can own, and that is the part most new owners find out the hard way.

Northern catalpa (Catalpa speciosa) shows up often in older Boise neighborhoods because it grows fast, tolerates compacted and alkaline soil, and puts on a big flower show each spring. The tradeoff is brittle wood, a long litter season, and a caterpillar that can strip it bare some summers. That is not a reason to remove a healthy catalpa. It is a reason to know what is normal and what is worth a call.

This guide covers what a catalpa actually is, the mess and wood-strength issues owners should expect, the catalpa worm, verticillium wilt, and how pruning decisions differ from a lot of other yard trees.

## What Is a Northern Catalpa?

Northern catalpa is a fast-growing native tree that typically reaches 40 to 60 feet tall with a broad, irregular crown, according to the [Morton Arboretum](https://mortonarb.org/plant-and-protect/trees-and-plants/northern-catalpa/). The giveaways are the leaves (heart-shaped, often 6 to 12 inches across), the upright clusters of white, trumpet-shaped flowers with purple and orange throat markings that open in late spring, and the seed pods that follow: slender, cigar-like capsules that can run well over a foot long and hang on the tree through winter.

Catalpa is adaptable to poor sites, which is exactly why it turns up so often as an older street or yard tree in the Treasure Valley. It tolerates compacted, clay, and alkaline soil better than a lot of species, and it does not need much babying once established.

## The Mess: Flowers, Pods and Sap

Ask anyone who parks a car under a mature catalpa and they will tell you the same thing: it is a messy tree, in stages, most of the year.

- The flower clusters drop in late spring, along with a sticky residue that can land on cars, patios, and anything parked underneath.
- The oversized leaves start coming down in summer and into fall, and because they are so large they do not disappear into the lawn the way smaller leaves do.
- The long seed pods drop from fall through winter, often over an extended stretch rather than all at once.
- Twigs and small branches shed year-round more than most species, a side effect of the brittle wood covered below.

## Late to Leaf Out, Early to Drop

If your catalpa is bare while every other tree on the block has leafed out, that is not automatically a health problem. Catalpa is one of the last trees to leaf out each spring and one of the first to drop its leaves in fall. That is just how the species runs its growing season. If May comes and goes with no leaves and neighboring catalpas nearby are also still bare, give it a little more time before assuming something is wrong. A catalpa with no leaves well into June, or one that leafs out and then wilts, is a different story and worth a look.

## Brittle Wood and Storm Risk

The biggest structural fact to know about a catalpa: the wood is weak and brittle compared to most shade trees, and branches break in wind more readily than you would expect from the tree's size. The Morton Arboretum and [University of New Hampshire Extension](https://extension.unh.edu/blog/2018/06/northern-catalpa-tree) both describe weak wood and storm breakage as a defining trait of the species, not an occasional problem.

That matters here because the Treasure Valley gets its share of summer thunderstorms and wind events. A catalpa with a long, heavy limb over a driveway, patio, or roofline is worth a look before wind season, not after a branch is already down. Watch for cracks at branch unions, limbs with noticeably thin attachment points, and any branch that has already partly split. None of that is a DIY call on a tree this size.

## Catalpa Worms: The Caterpillars That Strip the Leaves

Catalpa is the exclusive host plant for the catalpa sphinx caterpillar (Ceratomia catalpae), also called the catalpa worm. Females lay large egg clusters on the underside of leaves, and the caterpillars feed in groups before spreading out across the canopy, according to [Kansas State University Extension Entomology](https://blogs.k-state.edu/kansasbugs/2025/09/02/catalpa-caterpillar-catalpa-sphinx/). In a bad year, a heavy population of older caterpillars can defoliate a catalpa almost completely by late summer.

It looks alarming, but a healthy, well-watered tree usually pushes out a second flush of leaves later in the season and comes through fine. Trees already stressed by drought or disease have a harder time recovering from a full defoliation on top of that. For a light infestation, handpicking caterpillars is a reasonable first step; for a tree that gets stripped repeatedly year after year, it is worth having someone look at the tree's overall health rather than only treating the caterpillars.

One side note longtime Idaho gardeners may not know: catalpa worms are a prized fishing bait in parts of the country, which is part of why some people leave a catalpa standing on purpose.

## Verticillium Wilt and Other Disease Risk

Catalpa is one of the tree species susceptible to verticillium wilt, a soil-borne fungus that enters through the roots and clogs the tree's vascular system. Symptoms typically show up in early to mid-summer as sudden yellowing and wilting on one section of the crown, sometimes spreading to whole limbs, according to [Colorado State University's PlantTalk program](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/insects-diseases/1400-6-verticillium-wilt-catalpa-trees/). There is no effective chemical cure once a tree is infected; management is limited to removing dead wood, avoiding root injury, and keeping the rest of the tree as healthy as possible.

Verticillium wilt symptoms can look a lot like drought stress or transplant shock from a distance, which is why a tree that suddenly wilts on one side is worth having diagnosed rather than guessed at. ## Pruning, Pollarding and When to Replace or Remove

Catalpa handles hard pruning better than most yard trees. Left alone, its lower branches tend to droop toward the ground, and it is one of the few species that tolerates pollarding (cutting a tree back hard to the same points each dormant season) without the long-term decline that pollarding causes in most trees. That is different from topping, which is never a good idea on any species, catalpa included; see our page on [why topping is bad for Boise trees](/blog/tree-topping-bad-boise/) for the difference. Most catalpas do fine with standard [crown thinning or crown raising](/blog/crown-thinning-raising-reduction/) to clear weak or crossing limbs and lift the canopy over a driveway or walkway, done in late winter while the tree is dormant.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is my catalpa dying if it has not leafed out by mid-May?

Not necessarily. Catalpa is known for being one of the last trees to leaf out in spring and one of the first to drop leaves in fall, so a bare catalpa in early to mid-May while other trees are already green is often just normal timing for the species. If it is still bare well into June, or if it leafs out and then wilts, that is worth having looked at.

### Are catalpa worms actually dangerous to the tree?

They can strip a catalpa nearly bare in a bad year, since the caterpillars feed in large groups and a healthy tree has no other host plant to fall back on for comparison. Most established, well-watered trees push out new leaves later in the season and recover. A tree that gets defoliated repeatedly, year after year, is a better candidate for a health check than for repeated caterpillar treatment alone.

### Can I hard-prune or pollard my catalpa without hurting it?

Catalpa is one of the few species that tolerates hard, repeated cutback pruning (pollarding) reasonably well when it is done correctly during dormancy and maintained on a regular schedule. That is different from topping a tree, which damages structure and health on any species and is not something we recommend. If you want a shaped, contained catalpa, talk to a pro about pollarding specifically rather than a one-time hard cut.

### Why does my catalpa drip sticky sap on my car?

That sticky residue usually comes from the spent flower clusters and associated plant material dropping in late spring, not from an insect problem in most cases. It is a normal, if annoying, part of owning a mature catalpa and is one more reason people avoid parking directly underneath one during bloom season.

### What does verticillium wilt look like on a catalpa?

Typically sudden yellowing and wilting on one section of the crown or a few limbs, showing up in early to mid-summer, sometimes progressing to more of the tree. It can be confused with drought stress from a distance. There is no reliable chemical cure, so management is mostly about removing dead wood and keeping the tree as healthy as possible.

### Should I remove a catalpa just because it is messy?

Mess alone is not a removal reason. Catalpa's litter (flowers, sap, pods, oversized leaves) is a known tradeoff of the species, not a sign of a problem. Removal makes more sense when the wood is failing, the tree has advanced disease, or its size and brittle-wood risk are pointed at something that matters, like a house or a driveway.
