# Deadwood Removal: Why Dead Branches Should Come Out Before They Fall Out

A dead branch does not creak or lean to warn you first. It holds on through wind and snow load until the wood finally lets go, sometimes on a calm day for no obvious reason. That is why arborists treat deadwood removal as routine maintenance, not an emergency reaction.

If you are looking at a tree with bare, brittle branches mixed in with healthy growth, the short answer is: yes, cut them out, especially anything over an inch or two thick and anything hanging over a driveway, patio, fence, or roof. Small twig dieback deep in the canopy is usually fine to leave.

Here is how to tell the difference, what a professional does when they clean out a crown, and what it typically costs in the Treasure Valley.

## What Counts as Deadwood

Deadwood is any branch that has died back while the rest of the tree is still alive. It is different from a dead tree, where the whole thing is gone. A single mature tree can carry dead branches for years without being in danger itself.

Signs a branch is dead rather than dormant:

- No leaf buds along the branch when everything nearby has them
- Bark that is loose, cracked, or missing in patches
- Wood that snaps dry instead of bending when you flex a small twig
- No green layer under the bark if you scratch a small test spot near the base
- Bark beetle holes or fungal growth on the branch itself

## Spotting It in Summer vs. Winter

Summer is the easiest time to see deadwood on Boise's deciduous trees. Live branches leaf out; dead ones stay bare and stick out against the green canopy, often gray or brittle-looking against healthy bark. If a branch has no leaves by early June while the rest of the tree has fully leafed out, treat it as dead.

Winter is harder because everything is bare. Look instead at bark condition and branch flexibility. A live twig bends before it breaks; a dead one snaps. Bark on a dead branch tends to be duller and looser than bark on a live one right next to it. Evergreens show it differently: interior needle drop each fall is normal, but a fully brown, needle-bare branch on an otherwise green conifer is dead wood.

## A Little Deadwood Is Normal. A Lot Is a Symptom

Every mature tree sheds branches internally as the canopy shades them out. A handful of small dead twigs scattered through a healthy tree is not a red flag by itself.

What changes the picture is scale and location. If a large share of the crown is dead, if the deadwood is concentrated on one side, or if it showed up quickly rather than building up over a few seasons, that usually points to a stress cause worth investigating: [drought stress](/blog/drought-stress-trees-boise/), root damage, girdling roots, a disease, or a borer problem. In that case, deadwood removal is a symptom to remove, not the underlying issue to fix. Our guide on [how to save a dying tree](/blog/how-to-save-dying-tree/) walks through what to check next.

## The Size Threshold: When a Dead Branch Actually Matters

Not every dead twig needs a work order. What actually matters is diameter and target.

- **Under about half an inch, deep in the canopy:** low priority. This kind of interior dieback happens on almost every mature tree and rarely falls with enough force to hurt anything.
- **One to two inches, anywhere over a walkway, patio, parking spot, or roof:** worth removing on a normal maintenance schedule. This is the size range that does real damage to a windshield or a skylight.
- **Over two inches, or any size directly over where people sit, park, or walk regularly:** remove it. A limb this size can cause real injury or property damage, and it does not need a storm to come down, just enough wind or the weight of a bird landing wrong.
- **Any dead limb already cracked at the union or hanging by bark:** treat as urgent. This is a "hanger" and it can fall any time, including with no wind at all.

## Crown Cleaning: The Standard Practice Behind Deadwood Removal

When an arborist removes deadwood as a planned service, the industry term is crown cleaning. It is one of the standard pruning types defined under ANSI A300, the national tree care standard professional crews and specs are written against. According to the [International Society of Arboriculture's guide to pruning](https://www.treesaregood.org/Tree-Owner-Resources/Pruning-Your-Trees), cleaning "is the removal of dead, dying, diseased, weakly attached, and low-vigor branches from the crown of a tree."

Crown cleaning is not the same job as crown thinning, crown raising, or crown reduction, which remove or shorten live wood for different reasons. Our [crown thinning vs. raising vs. reduction guide](/blog/crown-thinning-raising-reduction/) covers those. A crown clean can be done on its own or combined with one of those other cuts in the same visit.

## The Real Risk of Leaving Dead Wood Up

Dead wood is unpredictable in a way live wood is not. A live branch usually gives some notice: it sags, it splits partway, it leans further each season. Dead wood has already lost the moisture and flexibility that would show that kind of warning, so it tends to fail suddenly, at the point where it is already weakest, which is often the exact spot most likely to hit something below.

The realistic risks are property damage to a roof, vehicle, fence, or shed, and injury to anyone standing, sitting, or walking underneath when it lets go. The [ISA's guidance on tree risk](https://www.treesaregood.org/Tree-Owner-Resources/identifyinghazardtrees) lists dead branches among the signs a tree owner should watch for, and it is exactly the kind of thing our [windstorm damage checklist](/blog/windstorm-tree-damage-checklist/) tells you to look for after a summer blow-through. Over a driveway or a kid's swing set, that risk is worth acting on well before the branch looks urgent.

## DIY vs. Calling a Pro

Removing a low, thin, easily reached dead branch with hand pruners or a pole saw from the ground is a reasonable weekend task for most homeowners. That covers a lot of the small interior deadwood on a young or medium tree.

It stops being a DIY job once a ladder, a chainsaw, or working above shoulder height is involved. Dead wood is more brittle and less predictable under a saw than live wood, branches at height often need to be roped down in sections rather than dropped whole, and a ladder plus a running chainsaw is a combination that goes wrong fast. If the branch is thick, high, or anywhere near where the cut piece could land on a person, a car, or a structure, that is a job for [tree trimming](/tree-trimming-boise/) by someone trained to rig it down safely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is deadwood removal the same as tree trimming?

It overlaps but is not identical. Deadwood removal, or crown cleaning, specifically targets dead, dying, and weakly attached branches. General tree trimming can include that plus shaping live growth, raising the canopy for clearance, or reducing size. A trim visit often includes deadwood removal as one part of the work.

### Will removing dead branches hurt my tree?

No. Removing dead wood does not stress a tree the way removing live wood can, because the branch is already dead and contributing nothing to the tree's growth. Cutting it out actually reduces the tree's risk of decay spreading from a dead stub into healthy wood nearby.

### How often does a tree need deadwood removed?

There is no fixed schedule that fits every tree. Fast-growing or stressed species can build up deadwood within a year or two, while a healthy, slow-growing tree might go several years between visits. A periodic look, especially after a hot summer or a heavy windstorm, is more useful than a set calendar.

### Can I just leave small dead twigs alone?

Small dead twigs deep inside a healthy canopy, away from anything below, are generally low risk and can be left. Priority goes to anything over an inch or two thick, anything already cracked or hanging, and anything positioned over a walkway, parking area, patio, or roof.

### Does a lot of deadwood mean my tree is dying?

Not automatically, but it is worth a closer look. A tree can carry some dead wood for years and stay healthy. A sudden increase in deadwood, or deadwood concentrated on one side of the crown, is more often a sign of an underlying stress or disease issue than normal aging, and it is worth having checked rather than just trimmed away.

### Is deadwood removal covered by a tree health assessment?

A [tree health assessment](/blog/tree-health-assessment-boise/) looks at the whole tree, deadwood included, and identifies whether the deadwood is normal aging or a symptom of a bigger problem. If work is scheduled after the assessment, the assessment fee is often waived. Either way, once the cause is identified, removing the existing deadwood is usually part of the recommended work.
