# Fire Damaged Trees: Will a Burned Tree Survive?

Many fire damaged trees live, including some that look terrible in the first few weeks. A scorched crown alone doesn't mean a dead tree, and green needles don't mean a safe one. The honest answer usually takes a full growing season. The one thing you shouldn't wait on is safety: a tree that's leaning, hollowed by fire, or burned at the base can fall long before you know whether it would have lived.

## Safety first: check for hazards before you check for life

Fire can burn out the base of a trunk or consume roots without much showing up top. According to the [University of Nevada Cooperative Extension fact sheet on residential trees after wildfire](https://naes.agnt.unr.edu/PMS/Pubs/1510_2004_96.pdf), a tree where fire burned deeply into part of the trunk will be unstable, survival is unlikely, and it should be removed as a hazard.

Look for a trunk burned deeply on one side, roots exposed or charred at the base, large limbs burned through or hanging, and any new lean. Keep people, cars and pets out from under a tree like that, and don't wait for spring if it's over a house, driveway or path. Our guides on [hazardous tree removal](/blog/hazardous-tree-removal-boise/) and [tree risk assessment](/blog/tree-risk-assessment-boise/) explain how an ISA-certified arborist rates the likelihood of failure.

One more thing worth knowing: the U.S. Forest Service's [Pacific Northwest post-fire tree assessment marking guide](https://research.fs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/2024-01/firelab-508-compliant-2pg-field-guide-postfire_tree_assessment.pdf) says outright that its survival rubric is not for identifying hazard trees. Whether a tree will live and whether it's safe to stand under are two separate questions.

## Three signs that predict survival

Forest researchers judge fire injury by crown scorch, bark char and beetle activity. The Forest Service guide describes all three, and Oregon State's [Will my tree live? Assessing postfire survivability](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9557-will-my-tree-live-assessing-postfire-survivability) turns them into species-by-species thresholds.

**Crown scorch** is the share of the tree's pre-fire living crown that the fire killed. It's an estimate, because you have to picture the crown as it was before the fire. Scorched needles turn brown or orange and may stay on the branch, so what matters is whether the buds and branches behind them survived. The thresholds vary a lot by species: Oregon State lists more than 65 percent of crown volume for Douglas-fir but only more than 20 percent for western redcedar. Those are Pacific Northwest forest conifers, so read them as proof that species matters, not as a verdict on your yard tree.

**Bark char** stands in for what happened to the cambium, the thin living layer just under the bark. The Forest Service guide says to estimate the share of the trunk's circumference that's charred near the root collar, not how high the char climbs. On thin-barked trees char reliably signals dead cambium. On thick-barked trees it's much less reliable, according to Oregon State.

**Beetle activity** shows up as boring dust or pitch tubes. The Forest Service guide says that if attacks cover more than half the circumference of the trunk, the tree will die regardless of fire injury. Our post on [bark beetles in Boise](/blog/bark-beetles-boise/) shows what the signs look like.

## The cambium check, step by step

This is the one hands-on test a homeowner can do. Go easy, because every sample is also a wound.

1. Pick a spot near the base of the trunk, on a side that looks burned.
2. Cut away a small patch of bark, about quarter-sized, down to the wood. The Nevada sheet says a quarter to a half inch through the bark.
3. Look at the layer just under the bark. Montana State describes healthy cambium as creamy or slightly off-white and moist or pitchy, and heat damage as darker brown tissue.
4. Repeat at a few spots around the trunk, since what you care about is how much of the circumference is dead.

[Montana State University Extension's photo guide for assessing wildfire severity](https://www.montana.edu/extension/forestry/publications/fact-sheets/FF_BurnSeverityAssessment_PK.pdf) explains why: heat can cook the cambium on one side and spare the other, and damage that runs all the way around kills the tree. It says ponderosa pine and western larch may survive with half the circumference damaged, while other species may die with only a quarter damaged. The Nevada sheet gives the rough cutoff for most trees as more than half. Montana State also warns that sampling itself can harm a tree, so sample small areas on a few trees.

Twigs work the same way. Peel back a little bark on a burned twig, and if the layer under it is green or white and moist, the twig may still be alive.

## Species matter more than most people expect

Bark thickness is the biggest reason two trees in the same fire end up differently. Oregon State lists ponderosa pine, Douglas-fir, western larch and incense cedar as thick-barked and fire resistant, and western hemlock, western redcedar, spruce and lodgepole pine as thin-barked and easily killed. Montana State adds that for Douglas-fir, spruce and fir even minor crown scorch often signals eventual death, and those trees can die from fire injury for five or more years afterward.

Hardwoods play by different rules. The Nevada sheet lists maple, cottonwood, aspen, birch, locust, willow and others as trees that resprout after fire, so a maple that lost its whole crown may still push shoots from the trunk or base. A burned maple deserves patience. A burned blue spruce usually doesn't get as much.

Age and prior health count too. The Nevada sheet says trees already stressed by drought, injury, disease or insects are unlikely to survive a fire. ## Why you should wait a growing season

For a burned tree that isn't an immediate hazard, the best advice is the least satisfying: wait. The Nevada sheet says an immediate inspection won't give an absolute answer, since the definitive one comes when, or if, buds and shoots develop the following spring, and it says not to hurry to prune every burned limb. Oregon State notes trees may take several years to die from fire injury, and the Forest Service rubric is built to predict death within three years.

- **First few weeks:** make the site safe, water, and do the basic checks.
- **Following spring:** watch bud break. Green, moist buds and twigs that bend are good signs. The Nevada sheet says an evergreen with less than 10 percent green foliage or less than half its buds alive is unlikely to survive.
- **Late summer:** repeat the cambium check on any doubtful trunk.
- **Second winter:** a tree that's still declining or showing beetle activity probably isn't recovering.

Don't cut a tree down in week one just because it looks grim, unless it's a hazard. And don't leave a clearly failing one standing for two years hoping.

## What to do in the first weeks

- Rope off the area under questionable limbs, and leave high or heavy pruning to a professional.
- Test the soil. The Nevada sheet warns burned soil can turn water-repellent: pour a cup of water on it, and if it beads up, the tree isn't getting what you think you're giving it.
- Once the soil absorbs water, soak slowly and deeply under and just past the canopy. Water-stressed trees are more prone to beetles. Our post on [watering trees in Boise](/blog/how-to-water-trees-boise/) covers the basics.
- Cut only what's dead and dangerous. Leave marginal limbs until you see what leafs out.
- Keep vehicles and equipment off the roots, and don't stack cut wood under surviving pines, since beetles can breed in it and move on.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will a burned tree survive?

Often, yes, if the cambium under the bark is still alive around most of the trunk and enough buds survive to leaf out. Thick-barked species survive better than thin-barked ones. You usually can't be sure until the following spring, and some fire-sensitive conifers die over several years even after looking fine at first.

### How do I check if a fire damaged tree is still alive?

Cut a small, quarter-sized patch of bark off near the base of the trunk, down to the wood. Moist, creamy white or green tissue underneath is alive; brown, dark and dry tissue is dead. Check several spots around the trunk, since damage extending all the way around the circumference usually kills the tree.

### Does a scorched tree with brown needles mean it's dead?

Not necessarily. Scorched needles can turn brown while the buds and branches behind them stay alive, especially on thick-barked pines. Extension guides tie survival to the percent of crown killed, which varies by species, so brown needles alone aren't a verdict. Wait for spring growth before deciding.

### How long should I wait before removing a fire damaged tree?

If it's a safety hazard, don't wait. Otherwise, give it at least one full growing season to see whether buds and shoots develop, and re-check the cambium late in the summer. A tree that is still declining or attracting beetles by the second winter is unlikely to recover.

### Why do bark beetles attack fire damaged trees?

Extension guides note that fire-damaged and water-stressed trees are more susceptible to bark beetle attack. Boring dust or pitch tubes around more than half the trunk's circumference means the tree will die regardless of fire injury, and cut wood shouldn't be stacked under living pines.

### Can I prune a burned tree myself?

Light cleanup of small dead twigs is fine, but hold off on heavy pruning until you see what leafs out, because live wood can look dead for weeks. Anything high, over a structure, or requiring a chainsaw on a weakened tree is a job for an ISA-certified arborist, since fire-damaged wood can fail without warning.
