# Anthracnose on Sycamore and Ash: Spring Leaf Drop That Looks Worse Than It Is

If your sycamore or ash dropped a pile of brown, curled leaves in late spring, the tree probably has anthracnose. It's a fungal leaf disease that shows up after a cool, wet spring and causes early leaf loss, and it looks alarming under a tree that was fine two weeks earlier.

The good news: anthracnose almost never kills a healthy sycamore or ash. Most trees push out a second set of leaves within a few weeks and look normal again by midsummer. The exception is a tree that gets hit hard several springs in a row, where the twig dieback finally starts to matter.

This guide covers what anthracnose looks like on each species, why it happens, what actually helps, and when the leaf drop is a sign of something more serious.

## What Anthracnose Actually Is

Anthracnose is a fungal disease, not an insect or a nutrient problem. It infects young leaves and twigs while they're still soft in spring, then spreads fastest during stretches of cool, wet weather because the fungus needs moisture on the leaf surface to establish. Spores splash and blow from old infected twigs and last year's fallen leaves onto the new growth, so a tree that had a bad year is more likely to have a bad year again if the leaf litter underneath it never gets cleared. Warm, dry weather shuts the cycle down, which is why symptoms fade once the Treasure Valley's summer heat sets in.

One detail that confuses people: the fungus that infects ash is not the same species as the one that infects sycamore, maple, or oak. Each is specific to its host tree. So a sycamore with anthracnose is not a risk to the ash next to it, and vice versa.

## What It Looks Like on Sycamore vs. Ash

The two trees show the disease a little differently.

- **Sycamore:** Young leaves turn brown and crinkled, with dead patches that follow the veins or the leaf margin. Buds and new twigs can die back too, which sometimes leaves a cluster of small shoots at the tip of a damaged branch the next year.
- **Ash:** Brown to black blotches appear along the veins of the leaflets, the leaves curl inward, and they drop while still mostly green rather than turning yellow first like normal fall color, a pattern [Iowa State University Extension](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/my-green-ash-trees-have-been-dropping-leaves-spring-why) describes in its own spring leaf-drop guidance for ash.

Both species can lose a large share of their spring leaves within a couple of weeks when conditions are right for the fungus. It's dramatic to look at, but it's a leaf problem, not a root or trunk problem, in most cases.

## Why Cool, Wet Springs Make It Worse

Anthracnose fungi overwinter in fallen leaves and infected twigs, then release spores as buds break in spring. If that period stays cool and rainy, the spores land on wet leaf surfaces and infect easily, and repeated rounds of rain keep reinfecting new growth. A dry, warm spring barely gives the fungus a foothold, which is why some years are bad and others aren't.

Boise's spring weather swings year to year, and a wetter-than-usual April or May is enough to trigger a visible outbreak on susceptible trees, especially older sycamores and green or white ash.

## Is It Anthracnose, or Something Else Dropping Your Leaves?

Spring leaf drop in Boise has more than one cause, and they don't all call for the same response. A late hard freeze right after leaf-out can scorch tender new leaves within days, which looks similar to anthracnose at a glance but shows up all at once after a single cold snap rather than building gradually over a wet, cool stretch of weeks. [Frost damage](/blog/frost-cracks-sunscald-trees/) also tends to hit the whole canopy evenly, while anthracnose is usually worse on the lower and inner branches where humidity lingers longest after rain.

Timing is the other clue. Anthracnose is a spring problem that fades as the weather warms, so leaf drop that starts in June or July, once the season has already turned hot and dry, is more likely drought stress, a root problem, or a pest than anthracnose.

## Will Your Tree Recover This Year?

In most cases, yes. Once the weather turns warm and dry, the tree stops losing new leaves and pushes out a second flush to replace what it dropped. [Iowa State University Extension](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/encyclopedia/anthracnose-shade-trees) notes that a severely defoliated tree will produce a new set of leaves to replace what it lost, and that the disease is a minor problem on otherwise established, healthy shade trees. By July, a tree that looked half-bare in May is often back to a full canopy, just a few weeks behind schedule.

A single bad spring, even a severe one, rarely does lasting harm to an otherwise healthy sycamore or ash. ## What to Do About It: Raking and Pruning for Airflow

You can't stop anthracnose once spring weather has already triggered it, but a few habits reduce how bad next year is:

1. **Rake up and remove fallen leaves** rather than leaving them under the tree or composting them on site. The fungus overwinters in that leaf litter, so clearing it cuts down on spores available to reinfect the tree next spring.
2. **Prune out dead twigs and thin crowded growth** during dormant season, ideally winter, to improve airflow and light through the canopy. Denser canopies stay damp longer after rain, which favors the fungus. [Crown thinning](/blog/crown-thinning-raising-reduction/) done correctly opens up that airflow without stripping the tree, and our guide on [how often trees need trimming](/blog/how-often-should-trees-be-trimmed/) covers a normal schedule.
3. **Keep the tree otherwise healthy.** Trees under [drought stress](/blog/drought-stress-trees-boise/) or growing in poor soil handle a bad anthracnose year worse than a well-watered, vigorous tree. See our [tree care calendar](/blog/tree-care-calendar-boise/) for what your tree needs each season.

None of this cures an active infection. It lowers the odds of a repeat next spring.

## When Injections and Sprays Are Sold, and Whether They're Worth It

Some companies sell trunk injections or foliar fungicide sprays for anthracnose, timed to bud break in early spring. Whether that's worth doing depends on the tree and the history, not a blanket answer.

A single rough spring on an otherwise healthy tree usually doesn't justify spraying or injecting anything. The tree will refoliate on its own. Treatment makes more sense for a high-value specimen tree that has been hit hard several years running, where repeated defoliation is visibly thinning the canopy or killing twigs faster than the tree can replace them. [Utah State University Extension](https://extension.usu.edu/planthealth/ipm/notes_orn/list-treeshrubs/anthracnose) notes that specimen sycamores in that situation sometimes need a systemic fungicide injection to save the tree, timed to bud break. Even then, a fungicide applied after leaves are already showing symptoms won't reverse the damage; it only protects new growth going forward, and usually needs to be repeated the following spring to matter.

## When Repeated Years Add Up

The cases worth watching are the trees that get anthracnose bad several springs in a row. Repeated twig killing forces the tree to keep sending out new shoots from the same damaged spots, and on sycamore in particular that can build into a dense cluster of small branches at old wound sites. [Washington State University's Hortsense program](https://hortsense.cahnrs.wsu.edu/fact-sheet/sycamore-sycamore-anthracnose/) describes this regrowth pattern as a witches'-broom effect, one of the visible signs of a tree that has been fighting the disease for multiple seasons. Over several years, that pattern combined with dying twigs can leave a tree looking thin and stressed even in a normal summer.

That's different from a single dramatic leaf drop, and it's worth a real look rather than guessing from the sidewalk. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can anthracnose spread from my ash tree to a sycamore nearby, or the other way around?

No. The fungus that causes anthracnose on ash is a different species from the one that infects sycamore, maple, or oak, and each only infects its own host. A bad year on one tree doesn't put the other species at risk from that same infection.

### Should I remove and dispose of the fallen leaves right away?

Yes, that's one of the more useful things you can do. Rake up dropped leaves promptly and dispose of them away from the base of the tree rather than leaving them to break down where they fell, since the fungus overwinters in leaf litter and reinfects the tree the following spring.

### Will my tree leaf back out this year, or is it done for the season?

Most sycamores and ash trees push out a second flush of leaves once the weather turns warm and dry, usually within a few weeks of the initial drop. By midsummer the canopy is typically full again, just later than usual.

### Does anthracnose ever mean the tree needs to come down?

Rarely, and not from a single bad spring. It becomes a real concern only after several consecutive years of heavy infection cause enough twig and branch dieback to weaken the tree structurally. Even then, the usual fix is targeted pruning of dead wood, not removal, unless an arborist finds separate structural problems.

### How do I tell anthracnose apart from a pest problem on the same tree?

Anthracnose shows up as brown or black blotches that follow the leaf veins, plus twig dieback, and it tracks closely with cool, wet spring weather. Chewed edges, sticky residue, visible insects, or holes in the bark point to a pest instead. If you're not sure which you're looking at, a photo and a quick on-site look usually settles it.

### Is it worth spraying my tree as a preventive measure every spring?

For most trees, no. Anthracnose is usually a cosmetic, short-term problem that clears up on its own by summer. Routine yearly spraying makes more sense only for a high-value tree with a documented pattern of severe infection several years running, and it works best as prevention before symptoms appear, not as a cure after the fact.
