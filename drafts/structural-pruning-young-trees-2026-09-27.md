# Structural Pruning for Young Trees: The Cheapest Tree Work You Will Ever Buy

Structural pruning is light, targeted cutting on a young tree, usually in its first several years in the ground, aimed at building one strong trunk and well-spaced main branches. It costs far less than fixing a badly shaped tree later, and it is one of the best things you can do to keep a tree from splitting apart in a windstorm years down the road.

Most homeowners never hear the term until a tree they planted has already grown a problem: two trunks fighting for the top spot, a branch crossing and rubbing another, or a lopsided crown leaning over the driveway. All of that is normal on an untrained young tree, and all of it is cheap and quick to correct while the branches are still pencil-thin. Wait until those branches are wrist-thick, and the same fix means bigger wounds, more stress on the tree, and a bigger bill.

This guide covers what structural pruning actually changes, when to start, what a visit looks like, and how to tell whether your young tree needs it now.

## What Structural Pruning Actually Means

Structural pruning is not a haircut for looks. It is a small number of deliberate cuts made to guide how a young tree grows for the rest of its life. The goals are the same on almost every species:

- **One dominant leader.** A single main trunk growing straight up, not two or three trunks of similar size competing for the top.
- **Well-spaced scaffold branches.** The permanent main limbs spread out along the trunk, up and around, instead of bunched at one height or all pointing the same direction.
- **No codominant stems.** Two stems of nearly equal size growing from the same point are a weak union. Left alone, bark can grow inward between them instead of wood grafting them together, which is why these joints split more often than others. [Colorado State University Extension](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/structural-pruning-of-young-shade-trees/) puts it bluntly: in structural pruning, there is zero tolerance for codominant trunks.
- **No crossing or rubbing branches.** Where two limbs cross, the constant rubbing wears through bark and opens the wood to decay and pests.

A structural pruning visit removes or shortens the branches that would grow into these problems, while leaving enough leaf area for the tree to keep growing well. It is small-diameter work: most cuts are pencil to finger thick, done with hand pruners or loppers rather than a chainsaw.

## Why It Matters More on a Young Tree Than an Old One

A young tree is still deciding its own shape. Every cut you make (or skip) becomes part of the tree's permanent structure as the wood grows and thickens around it. A weak branch union that starts as a small defect on a 3-year-old tree does not fix itself. It gets bigger, heavier, and harder to correct every year the tree is left alone, and by the time that limb is 6 inches across, removing it leaves a wound the tree may never fully close over.

## When to Start, and How Long It Takes

Structural pruning is a young-tree project. It generally starts once a newly planted tree has settled in and is putting on active growth, and continues with light visits every year or two through roughly the tree's first decade in your yard. [Iowa State University Extension](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/pruning-young-trees) recommends committing to a pruning check each spring for the first five years, with the shaping work spread progressively over that time rather than done all at once. After that window, the trunk and main limbs have usually set their basic shape, and pruning shifts toward general trimming and maintenance rather than training.

Because it happens over several short visits instead of one big job, structural pruning is spread out and inexpensive compared to a single mature-tree trim. Each visit is short: a homeowner walking the tree with a pruning crew, pointing out concerns, while a few small cuts get made.

## What Structural Pruning Fixes (and Prevents)

| Problem left untrained | What happens if it is not corrected young |
|---|---|
| Codominant trunks | Weak, bark-included union; splits in wind or heavy snow |
| Crossing, rubbing branches | Bark wounds, entry points for decay and pests |
| Branches bunched at one height | Weight concentrated in one spot, uneven crown |
| Overly low limbs kept permanently | Limits clearance, forces bigger cuts later |
| No clear single leader | Two or more "tops" compete, one usually loses and dies back |

Every row in that table is a quick snip on a young tree and a full-size removal cut, sometimes a whole-tree removal, on a mature one.

## Structural Pruning vs. Regular Trimming

People often use "pruning" and "trimming" to mean the same thing, and on a mature tree they mostly do. On a young tree, structural pruning is a specific, smaller job aimed at future shape, while general trimming (covered on our [tree trimming page](/tree-trimming-boise/)) is more about removing dead wood, thinning a crowded crown, or cutting back growth that has gotten out of hand. A young tree usually needs structural pruning more than it needs trimming. There is rarely dead wood to remove yet, and the crown is not dense enough to thin. What it needs is guidance: pick the leader, space the scaffolds, cut the competition.

If your tree is well past the young-tree stage, structural pruning is off the table and what it needs instead is [crown thinning, raising, or reduction](/blog/crown-thinning-raising-reduction/). ## Signs Your Young Tree Needs Structural Pruning Now

You do not need to identify every defect yourself, but a few things are easy to spot from the ground:

1. **Two trunks of similar size** growing up side by side from low on the tree, instead of one clearly dominant trunk.
2. **A tight V-shaped fork** where two big branches meet, rather than a wider U-shaped angle.
3. **Branches that touch or cross** anywhere in the crown.
4. **A lopsided crown**, heavier on one side, especially if the tree was planted near a wall, fence, or another tree that shaded one side.
5. **A trunk that leans or wanders** rather than growing straight.

If you planted a bare-root or young balled-and-burlapped tree and staked it for support, structural pruning and staking removal often go hand in hand. Our post on [staking new trees](/blog/tree-staking-new-trees/) covers when to take the stakes off, which is usually sooner than people think.

If you are not sure whether what you are seeing is normal young-tree growth or an actual problem, a quick look from a professional is worth more than guessing at it yourself.

## DIY or Call a Pro?

Structural pruning on a young tree is one of the few tree jobs a careful homeowner can reasonably do themselves. The cuts are small, the tools are hand pruners or loppers, and the branches are at eye level or close to it. A few rules keep DIY pruning safe: never remove more than about a quarter of the live foliage in one year, always cut just outside the branch collar (the slightly swollen ring where a branch meets the trunk or a larger limb), and never strip branches off more than about a third of the tree's total height in one go. A University of Idaho Extension educator, writing for [East Idaho News](https://www.eastidahonews.com/2024/01/training-young-shade-trees/), calls that last mistake "lion tailing": it creates a weak, top-heavy trunk, and lower branches should instead come off gradually over several years, not all at once. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does structural pruning cost?

For a young tree under about 25 feet, general trimming (which covers structural pruning work) typically runs $150-$400 per tree, depending on the number of cuts needed and access to the tree. The exact price depends on your specific tree and yard, so use our cost calculator or call for a free on-site estimate.

### Can I do structural pruning myself?

Yes, on most young trees, as long as you stick to small cuts, avoid removing more than a quarter of the canopy in one year, and cut at the branch collar rather than flush to the trunk. If the tree already has a major defect like two competing trunks of nearly equal size, it is worth having a professional make that call.

### How is structural pruning different from topping a tree?

They are close to opposites. Structural pruning removes small, targeted branches to build a strong permanent shape. Topping cuts the main leader and large limbs back to stubs, which removes the very structure structural pruning is trying to build and forces weak, poorly attached regrowth. Reputable tree crews do not top trees.

### My newly planted tree already has two trunks. Is that a problem?

It can be. Two trunks of similar size growing from close to the same point (called codominant stems) form a weaker union than a single trunk with side branches, and that weak point does not go away on its own. The earlier one of the two is reduced or removed, the smaller and easier the correction. This is a common reason to schedule a structural pruning visit in a tree's first few years.

### How often does a young tree need structural pruning visits?

Roughly every one to two years through the first several years after planting, tapering off as the tree's basic shape sets. Each visit is usually short, since the goal is a handful of small, well-chosen cuts rather than a full trim.

### Will structural pruning stunt my tree's growth?

No. Because the cuts are small and the total amount of foliage removed is kept modest, a properly structurally pruned tree keeps growing at a normal rate. What changes is the shape it grows into, not how fast it grows.
