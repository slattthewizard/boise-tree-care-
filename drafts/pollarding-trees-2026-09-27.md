# Pollarding Trees: What It Is, Why It Is Not Topping, and Whether It Suits a Boise Yard

Pollarding is a pruning system where a tree's upper branches are cut back, every year or two, to the same set of points on the trunk. Over time those points swell into knobby "knuckles," and each cycle of growth is removed right back to them. Done correctly, it is a deliberate, lifelong commitment that keeps a tree at a fixed size. Done on the wrong tree, or started too late, it turns into topping with extra steps.

Most of what shows up when you search "pollarding" comes from British and European arborists, because the practice is far more common there than in Idaho. That does not mean it cannot work here. It means a Boise homeowner needs to understand what they are actually signing up for before asking a crew to start.

## What Pollarding Actually Is

Pollarding starts on a young, vigorous tree. An arborist selects a framework of main branches and cuts each one back to a specific point, usually well above the ground. [Purdue University's Forestry and Natural Resources Extension](https://www.purdue.edu/fnr/extension/pruning-highlight-pollarding-trees-a-traditional-pruning-practice-with-modern-uses/) describes it as cutting the upper branches "back to the same points on a regular schedule, creating a framework of knobby growth points from which new shoots emerge." Every following season, or every two to three years once the tree matures, a crew removes that year's new growth and cuts back to the same knuckles again.

The result is a tree that never gets much bigger than it was the first year it was pollarded. New shoots sprout fast from the knuckles, so the canopy fills back in within a season or two, then gets cut again.

## Pollarding vs. Topping: The Real Difference

Topping and a fresh pollard cut can look similar in a photo, which is where the confusion starts. The difference is in the plan, the timing, and what happens next.

Topping means cutting large branches back to stubs, or to a lateral branch too small to take over as the new leader, with no schedule for what comes after. Our guide on [why topping is bad for Boise trees](/blog/tree-topping-bad-boise/) covers what that does to a tree's structure. The [International Society of Arboriculture's consumer education site](https://www.treesaregood.org/Tree-Owner-Resources/Pruning-Your-Trees) calls topping "perhaps the most harmful tree pruning practice known," and warns it leads to stress, decay, and long-term hazard rather than a smaller, safer tree.

Pollarding is different in three ways:

1. **It starts young.** The tree is trained into the system while its wood is still small in diameter and the bark can build callus tissue quickly at the cut points.
2. **It repeats on a set cycle**, not as a one-time fix for a tree that got too big.
3. **The cuts return to the same knuckles every time**, which build up scar tissue and seal each wound before the next cut, instead of leaving a fresh open stub each year.

A properly maintained pollard is a system the tree was built for. Topping is an emergency haircut with no follow-up plan.

## Why Starting Pollarding on a Mature Tree Backfires

This is the part homeowners searching "pollarding" for a shade tree that has already gotten too big usually miss: you cannot pollard a mature tree that was never trained for it. The first cut on a mature tree, made through large-diameter wood that has no callus history at that point, is structurally the same as a topping cut. It strips a large share of the canopy at once, the tree responds with a flush of weakly attached sprouts, and the big wound is far more likely to decay than seal over.

Pollarding only stays safer than topping because the knuckle has years of accumulated callus doing the work. Skip that buildup and start on a 40-foot maple that has never been cut this way, and you get topping's downsides with none of the long-term benefit. If a tree has already outgrown its spot, [crown thinning, raising, or reduction](/blog/crown-thinning-raising-reduction/) is usually the better move, or removal if the tree genuinely does not fit the site.

## Which Trees Actually Tolerate Pollarding

Not every species handles repeated hard pruning well. The [Royal Horticultural Society's pollarding guide](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/trees/pollarding) lists species suited to the practice, including London plane, common lime (a European linden relative), ash, elm, and mulberry, all trees that resprout reliably from old wood cut back to the same points year after year.

Conifers are a hard no. Pines, spruces, and firs do not reliably produce new growth from bare wood the way broadleaf trees do, so a "pollarded" conifer usually just stays disfigured. Species that grow slowly or heal wounds poorly are also poor candidates, since the tree needs to close each year's cut before decay organisms get a foothold.

| Good candidates (vigorous resprouting) | Poor candidates |
|---|---|
| London plane, linden, ash, elm, mulberry | Pines, spruces, and other conifers |
| Species that reliably resprout from old wood | Slow-growing or decay-prone species |

None of these are common street or yard trees in the Treasure Valley the way they are in a London park, so if you already have a mature specimen of one of these species, ask an arborist to look at it in person before assuming pollarding is the right long-term plan.

## The Maintenance Commitment (and Why It Is Not a One-Time Cut)

Pollarding is not a project you finish. It is a standing appointment on the calendar for as long as you own the tree. Miss a cycle or two and the knuckles put out branches heavy enough to start acting like normal limbs again, which brings back the weight and size problems pollarding was supposed to solve.

Because it is repeat work rather than a single job, think of the cost the same way you would think of any recurring [tree trimming](/tree-trimming-boise/): a per-visit range based on the tree's size and access, not a one-time number. ## Does Pollarding Make Sense for a Boise Yard?

Sometimes. Pollarding earns its keep when you have a fast-growing, suitable species in a tight spot, a canal bank or a narrow side yard, where the alternative is fighting the same oversized tree every year anyway. Started young and kept on schedule, it can genuinely extend how long that tree stays a good fit for the space.

It is a poor fit for a mature shade tree you just want smaller, for anything already showing decay or a crack, and for any tree anywhere near power lines. If a limb is near a line, do not have anyone cut near it; call Idaho Power, and call 911 first if a line is actually down.

## Getting Started the Right Way

If pollarding still sounds right for your tree, a few things matter more than the first cut:

- Confirm the species actually tolerates it. Guessing wrong means redoing the work as a removal later.
- Start while the tree is young and the branches are still small in diameter.
- Set a real cutting schedule (usually annual, sometimes every two to three years once established) and plan to keep it.
- Have the framework established by someone who understands [how to prune a tree](/blog/how-to-prune-a-tree/) correctly, since a badly placed first cut sets the knuckle in the wrong spot for good.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is pollarding the same thing as topping?

No. Topping is an unplanned, one-time reduction of a mature tree's canopy to stubs, with no follow-up. Pollarding is a repeated system started on a young tree, cut back to the same points on a set schedule for the rest of the tree's life. A first cut on an untrained mature tree behaves like topping even if someone calls it pollarding.

### Can I start pollarding on a tree that is already mature?

Not safely in the way pollarding is meant to work. The first hard cut on a mature, untrained tree removes a large share of the canopy at once and creates the same weak regrowth and decay risk as topping. Pollarding depends on years of built-up callus at the cut points, which a mature tree simply does not have yet.

### Which trees can actually be pollarded in Boise?

Species that resprout vigorously from cut wood, such as London plane, linden, ash, elm, and mulberry, tolerate it best. Conifers and slow-growing or decay-prone species are poor candidates. None of these are especially common in Treasure Valley yards, so have an arborist confirm the species and the tree's condition before committing.

### How often does a pollarded tree need to be cut back?

Usually every year while it is young, then every one to three years once the framework and knuckles are established. Skipping cycles lets the regrowth get heavy enough to act like normal branches again, undoing the point of pollarding.

### Does pollarding hurt the tree?

Done on a suitable, correctly trained tree and kept on schedule, pollarding is a recognized, sustainable pruning system. Done on the wrong species, started on a mature tree, or left unmaintained for years, it causes the same stress, weak attachment, and decay problems as topping.

### Is pollarding common in the Treasure Valley?

Not really. It is far more established in Europe, where the species that tolerate it well are more common in the urban tree mix. It can still work here on the right species in the right spot, but most Boise yards are better served by regular trimming or crown thinning instead.
