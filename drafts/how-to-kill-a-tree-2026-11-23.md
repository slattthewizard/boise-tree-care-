# How to Kill a Tree: Methods That Work and the Risks of Leaving It Standing

University extension services describe four ways to kill a tree you own: treat the stump with herbicide right after cutting, put herbicide into spaced cuts in the trunk (hack-and-squirt), spray the lower trunk (basal bark), or girdle it. The herbicide methods are the dependable ones. Girdling on its own is slow and often fails. But in a yard, the bigger question is usually what happens after the tree dies, because a dead tree left standing next to a house, fence or driveway is a hazard that gets worse every year.

So here's our honest take up front: for most residential trees, the better plan is to cut the tree down and treat the stump, not to poison it and leave it standing. The sections below cover each method, when it makes sense, and the problems people run into.

## Before you do anything: is killing it the right move?

Killing a tree in place makes sense in a few situations: a small, unwanted tree in a back corner, an invasive tree in a fence line that you plan to cut later, or a tree where felling it would damage something and you want to take it down in pieces after it dries out. It makes much less sense for a large tree near anything you care about.

## Read the label first

Everything below involves herbicides, and the label is the law. A homeowner can apply products labeled for home use by following the label exactly, but the label decides which trees, which methods and which amounts are allowed. Not every product is approved for cut-stump or trunk applications, and products with similar names can have very different strengths. Ohio State University Extension puts it plainly: [verify that a product is still labeled for your intended use](https://ohioline.osu.edu/sites/ohioline/files/imce/Agriculture_and_Natural_Resources/F-45-Controlling-Woodlands-2025-0604-TV.pdf), such as cut-stump application, before you start.

We're not going to name products or give mixing ratios here. Those depend on the label in your hand, and they change.

## Cut-stump treatment

This is the method most extension pages recommend for homeowners, because it's the simplest and it keeps the herbicide away from other plants. You cut the tree down, then apply herbicide to the fresh stump surface so it moves down into the roots instead of the stump sending up new shoots.

According to [Alabama Cooperative Extension](https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/forestry/cut-stump-herbicide-treatments-for-invasive-plant-control/), the details that matter are:

- Apply within minutes of cutting. Delays reduce effectiveness, because the cut surface starts to seal.
- Put the herbicide on the outer ring of the stump, about 2 inches in from the bark, since that's where the living tissue that moves it downward sits. On stems under roughly 4 inches across, treat the whole top.
- Skip the job if rain is expected within 4 to 6 hours.
- Wear eye protection and chemical-resistant gloves.

The same page names glyphosate and triclopyr amine as the choices for residential sites because they have little soil activity. If you can't treat right away, an oil-based product applied to the whole stump, sides included, is the fallback it describes, within a few weeks.

Stump treatment stops resprouting from the stump. It doesn't remove the stump. If you want the stump gone, [our guide to getting rid of a tree stump](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-tree-stump/) covers what chemical stump killers do and don't do, and [stump grinding](/stump-grinding-boise/) is the fast route.

## Hack-and-squirt (frilling)

Hack-and-squirt kills a tree that's still standing. Ohio State University Extension describes it as a discontinuous frill: you make a ring of downward-angled cuts around the trunk with a hatchet, each about 2 inches long and deep enough to reach the sapwood (the lighter outer wood), with the edges 1 to 3 inches apart. Then you put a small amount of herbicide in each cut, in the amount the label specifies.

It's more effective than cutting alone, and it works on trunks too big for basal bark spray. Ohio State notes that tough species may need a continuous cut around the trunk instead of spaced ones. It also notes that physical methods without herbicide are generally less dependable and slower.

The catch for a yard: the tree is now dying on its feet. We'll get to what that means below.

## Basal bark spray

Basal bark treatment means spraying herbicide on the lower 12 to 18 inches of the trunk. Ohio State says it works on trees less than about 4 to 6 inches in diameter, and gets less effective as bark gets thicker and rougher. These products are generally oil-based and soak through the bark.

The warning that comes with it is about runoff. Roots often reach well past the crown, so a desirable tree nearby may have roots under the one you're spraying. If excess herbicide runs into the soil, Ohio State warns the neighboring tree can absorb it and be killed or seriously damaged. This is a method for small saplings, applied sparingly, well away from anything you want to keep.

## Girdling

Girdling cuts a groove through the bark and into the wood all the way around the trunk, interrupting the flow between roots and crown. Ohio State says the groove has to completely encircle the trunk and go into the wood at least 1/2 inch deep on small trees, and 1 to 1 1/2 inches on larger ones. A partial ring doesn't do the job.

Girdling with no herbicide is the least reliable method on the list. Ohio State calls physical methods generally less dependable and slower, particularly on hard-to-kill species. It takes a while, and you end up with a dying tree standing in the yard the whole time.

## Timing: late summer and fall beat spring

When you treat matters nearly as much as how. Alabama Extension says late summer through fall is often best for cut-stump work, and warns against early spring, when strong sap flow makes stumps bleed water after cutting and can wash the herbicide out. Ohio State says the same about water-carried products in spring on sap-flow species like maples, and notes that applying herbicide later in summer and into fall improves the odds the plant dies, because the tree is moving resources down to the roots to prepare for winter.

## Why a standing dead tree is a problem

Here's the part most "how to kill a tree" pages skip. Dead wood dries out and gets brittle. [Purdue Extension](https://www.purdue.edu/fnr/extension/timely-tree-removal/), writing about dead ash trees, says they become extremely brittle and break easily, that breaks inside the structural wood are often hidden by the bark, and that limbs can fall at any time, including on calm, clear days. It also says delaying removal makes the tree more brittle and the hazard greater, and that if a dead tree can't be felled, people should stay clear of it. That was about ash, but the same logic applies to any tree that has died and is drying out.

Our view as arborists is that this is also why dead trees get harder to take down the longer you wait. A live tree bends, gives warning and holds together while it's cut. A dead one can shed limbs unpredictably and fail under a climber or a saw. It usually means more rigging, more care and more time, not less.

## Root grafts: what about the neighboring trees?

Trees of the same species sometimes fuse their roots underground, called root grafting. The worry is that herbicide you put into one tree travels through those grafts and hurts a healthy neighbor. The evidence is mixed. Washington State University Extension's Linda Chalker-Scott [reviewed the research](https://wpcdn.web.wsu.edu/wp-puyallup/uploads/sites/403/2015/03/herbicide-translocation.pdf) and found no evidence that glyphosate and related herbicides crossed root grafts in healthy trees, noting that root grafts are most common between trees of the same species. But she also says grafts already breached by fungi might serve as a path, and the herbicide product warnings exist for a reason. [LSU AgCenter](https://www.lsuagcenter.com/profiles/mhferguson/articles/page1663012732190) warns that if the treated plant shares a root system with another plant, through suckers or grafted roots, the other plant may be damaged too.

Our practical read: if you have two or three trees of the same species growing close together, treat the risk as real. Killing one in place with a systemic herbicide next to a tree you want to keep is where we'd stop and call someone.

## Trees that fight back

Some trees are hard to kill because they resprout from the stump and send suckers from the roots. Tree of heaven is the textbook case. Alabama Extension says it [regrows vigorously from cut stumps and roots](https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/forestry-wildlife/tree-of-heaven-identification-and-control/), and that treating about a month before cutting helps reduce resprouting. Established stands need repeat treatments. Locally, the usual suspects are Russian olive, Siberian elm, tree of heaven and black locust.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the fastest way to kill a tree?

Cutting it down and treating the stump with a labeled herbicide right away is the fastest dependable method, since the stump can't resprout once the herbicide moves into the roots. Killing a standing tree takes longer, often a full season, and leaves you with a dead tree to deal with.

### Does girdling a tree kill it?

Sometimes, but it's the least reliable method. Ohio State University Extension says physical methods without herbicide are generally slower and less dependable, especially on hard-to-kill species. The groove must go all the way around and deep enough into the wood, or the tree heals and carries on.

### Can I kill a tree without cutting it down?

Yes, with hack-and-squirt, basal bark spray on small trees, or girdling. They leave the tree standing as it dies. Dead wood gets brittle, so it can drop limbs or fail without warning. That's why we'd only do it where nothing and nobody is in the fall zone.

### When is the best time of year to kill a tree?

Extension sources point to late summer through fall, when the tree is sending resources down to its roots. Early spring is the worst time for stump treatments on sap-flow species like maples because the stump bleeds sap and flushes the herbicide out.

### Will killing one tree hurt the trees next to it?

It can. Herbicide that runs into the soil can be taken up by nearby roots, and trees of the same species may share grafted roots. Research on whether herbicide actually crosses grafts is mixed, but the risk isn't zero, so keep treatments well away from trees you want to keep.

### Is it okay to kill a neighbor's tree or a city street tree?

No, and we'd steer you well away from it. A neighbor's tree is theirs, so talk to them first. A street tree sits in the right-of-way the city manages, so check with the city before touching it.
