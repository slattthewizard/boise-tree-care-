# Mistletoe on Trees: What It Is, What It Does, and How to Get Rid of It

Yes, mistletoe is a parasite. It's a plant that grows on a tree's branches and pulls water, and in some cases nutrients, out of the tree it sits on. How much harm it does depends on which kind you have: leafy mistletoe on a broadleaf tree is usually a slow, minor drain, while dwarf mistletoe on a pine or fir can stunt and eventually kill the tree.

Before you decide anything, figure out which type is growing on your tree. The two look different, behave differently, and call for different responses.

## Is Mistletoe a Parasite?

It is. [Oregon State University Extension](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/es/catalog/em-9679-mistletoes-effects-trees-vary-type) describes true (leafy) mistletoe as a hemiparasite: it has green leaves and makes some of its own food, but it takes water and minerals from the host. [University of California IPM](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/mistletoes/) says dwarf mistletoe is closer to a full parasite, getting nearly all of its nutrients from the host. Either way, the plant's root-like tissue grows into the branch and taps the tree's water-conducting wood, which is why you can't just wipe it off like moss.

Most of the mistletoe you'll run into on a tree is native, and a healthy tree can tolerate a little of it. The trouble starts when there's a lot of it, or when it's the dwarf type on a conifer.

## Leafy Mistletoe vs. Dwarf Mistletoe

These are two different plant groups, and they're easy to tell apart once you know what to look for.

**Leafy (true) mistletoe** has thick, oval green leaves and forms rounded clumps that can reach about two feet across, according to UC IPM. It mostly grows on broadleaf trees, with white berries that birds eat and then spread. It's the plant you picture at Christmas.

**Dwarf mistletoe** is small and easy to miss. UC IPM describes stems under 6 to 8 inches with scale-like leaves, yellow-green to tan in color. It grows only on conifers, and each species tends to specialize in one or a few hosts. Colorado State University Extension's [PlantTalk page on dwarf mistletoe](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/weeds-cultural-problems/2106-dwarf-mistletoe/) lists ponderosa, lodgepole, limber and pinyon pines plus Douglas-fir as hosts in Colorado, and says hardwoods aren't affected.

The spread is different too. Birds carry leafy mistletoe seed from tree to tree. Dwarf mistletoe fires its sticky seeds out of the fruit, and Colorado State puts the launch speed at up to 60 mph. Those seeds land on nearby needles and twigs, usually on trees close by and lower in the canopy than the infected branch. UC IPM says seeds can reach trees up to 40 to 50 feet away.

## Which Kinds Show Up in Idaho?

Here's where we have to be careful. Mistletoe species and their hosts vary a lot from region to region, and we aren't going to guess which species grows in the Treasure Valley or on which landscape tree.

What we can say from sources we did read: a [USDA Forest Service publication on conifer-infecting Phoradendron](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/27736) describes the leafy mistletoes as infecting a variety of hosts across a wide range from Oregon to Mexico, with junipers the main conifer affected. The dwarf mistletoes are tied to conifers like the pines and Douglas-fir listed above.

So the practical answer for a Boise-area yard: if you see a green, leafy clump in a broadleaf tree, or a witches' broom with tiny leafless shoots on a pine or fir, have it identified rather than assuming. ## How Mistletoe Harms a Tree

**Leafy mistletoe** is usually the milder of the two. Oregon State says the effects are typically minor, though heavy infections can cause growth loss, branch dieback and drought stress. A big plant on a branch also makes the branch swell and can leave it weak at the attachment point.

**Dwarf mistletoe** is the serious one. According to [Colorado State University Extension](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/mistletoes-in-colorado-conifers/), its roots grow into the bark and wood and draw nutrients and water from the host, causing witches' brooms, gradual decline and dieback from the top down, often leading to death over many years. The Colorado State Forest Service notes that stressed trees can also attract bark beetles. Their page on [dwarf mistletoe](https://csfs.colostate.edu/forest-management/common-forest-insects-diseases/dwarf-mistletoe/) says heavy infections slow growth, reduce seed production and lower wood quality, and that lodgepole pine infestations can cut growth by 80 to 90 percent.

It's a slow process. Colorado State's life-cycle numbers run to roughly 6 to 8 years from infection to seed production, and visible shoots take two to three years to appear after infection. That timeline matters: by the time you can see the plant, the tree has usually been infected for a while.

## Witches' Brooms From Dwarf Mistletoe

Dwarf mistletoe makes the tree sprout dense, abnormal clusters of branches around the infection. Those are witches' brooms. The Colorado State Forest Service says Douglas-fir brooms can be fan-shaped or spherical and heavy enough to swell the branch badly, while ponderosa pine brooms tend to be smaller and more compact.

Not every broom is mistletoe. Mites, fungi, viruses and plain genetics cause brooms too, which is why we wrote a separate piece on [witches' broom on trees](/blog/witches-broom-on-trees/). The tell for dwarf mistletoe is the small shoots, flowers or fruit on the broom itself. UC IPM says to confirm by finding them.

## Mistletoe Removal: What Works

Pulling or cutting off the visible plant is the first thing most people try, and it doesn't solve the problem. Here's why.

For leafy mistletoe, UC IPM says cutting the plant off reduces spread but doesn't control it, because it regrows from the tissue embedded in the branch. Oregon State agrees: removing visible plants may give a few years of relief, but you'll need to repeat it. If the tree is otherwise healthy and the mistletoe is small, that might be a fine trade-off. We wouldn't lose sleep over a little leafy mistletoe in a big mature tree.

The fix that lasts is pruning the whole branch, not just the plant. UC IPM recommends cutting at least 1 foot below the swelling where the mistletoe attaches, or back to the next node toward the trunk for dwarf mistletoe. It's [a real pruning job](/tree-trimming-boise/), and on a tall tree it's not a ladder-and-loppers one. If you want a refresher on cut placement, our guide to [how to prune a tree](/blog/how-to-prune-a-tree/) covers the basics.

Some rules of thumb from the extension sources:

- Light to moderate dwarf mistletoe: prune infected branches back to the branch collar. Colorado State says keep roughly 30 to 40 percent of the live branches, counted from the top down, even if a few infected ones stay behind. Stripping a conifer bare to chase every shoot does more harm than the mistletoe.
- Heavy infection: remove the tree. Colorado State recommends removing severely infected trees, since trees that are heavily infected and out of reach keep dropping seed on their neighbors. UC IPM says the same for advanced cases. See our page on [tree removal in Boise](/tree-removal-boise/) if it gets to that point, and check [tree removal permits in Boise](/blog/tree-removal-permits-boise/) first if the tree is near the street.
- Trunk infections: if the mistletoe is on the main trunk and can't be pruned away, UC IPM suggests cutting it flush and wrapping the spot in black plastic for up to two years. That's a stopgap, and it's for a tree you really want to keep.
- Sprays: some growth regulator products can knock back shoots, but both UC IPM and Colorado State say they give temporary control and need repeating. Read the label, and honestly, most homeowners are better off with pruning.

Check the tree again every couple of years. UC IPM warns that latent infections are easy to miss, so a tree with a mistletoe history deserves a look for several years after the cuts.

## What About Neighboring Trees?

Dwarf mistletoe spreads tree to tree, so one infected pine can seed the ones around it. Oregon State's advice for infected conifer stands is to remove understory trees of the same species within about 50 feet of the infected overstory tree and favor other species. In a residential yard that translates to: don't plant another pine or Douglas-fir right under an infected one. Colorado State suggests replanting with non-host species once an infected tree comes out.

## The Holiday Mistletoe Is a Different Thing

The sprigs sold for kissing under at Christmas are cut, packaged and sold as a decoration. That's a product, not a sign of an infection. Hanging a sprig indoors doesn't put mistletoe in your trees. The risk to your yard comes from birds and seed on a real, growing plant.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is mistletoe a parasite on trees?

Yes. Leafy mistletoe is a hemiparasite that makes some of its own food but takes water and minerals from the tree. Dwarf mistletoe is closer to a full parasite and gets nearly all its nutrients from the host. Both anchor into the branch with root-like tissue, which is why they can't simply be wiped off.

### Will mistletoe kill my tree?

Leafy mistletoe rarely kills a healthy tree, though heavy infections can cause branch dieback and drought stress. Dwarf mistletoe on a pine or fir is more serious: extension sources describe slow decline from the top down, often over many years, and heavily infected trees can die, especially if beetles move in.

### Can I just pull mistletoe off my tree?

You can, but it won't fix anything. The plant regrows from tissue embedded in the branch, so pulling it only buys a few years at best. Cutting the whole branch at least a foot below the swelling is what actually removes it, and a heavily infected conifer may need to come out.

### Is the mistletoe I buy at Christmas the same as what grows on trees?

It's the same kind of plant, but the sprigs sold for the holidays are cut and sold as decoration. A dried or cut sprig hanging in your house won't infect your trees. Mistletoe spreads through seeds on a living plant, carried by birds or, for dwarf mistletoe, shot out of the fruit.

### What are witches' brooms, and does mistletoe cause them?

Witches' brooms are dense clusters of twigs growing from one spot. Dwarf mistletoe causes them on conifers like Douglas-fir and pine, but mites, fungi, and viruses cause brooms on other trees too. Finding small mistletoe shoots, flowers or fruit on the broom confirms the mistletoe cause.

### When is the best time to prune out mistletoe?

Before the plant sets seed, so it can't spread to other branches or trees. For leafy mistletoe, UC IPM suggests cutting each winter if you're only removing the plants. Removing whole infected branches can be done any time it's safe to do the work, and a quick on-site look will tell you what timing suits your tree.
