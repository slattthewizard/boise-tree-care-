# What a Tree Removal Quote Should Include Before You Sign

Two crews look at the same 55-foot silver maple in a Boise back yard. One tree removal quote comes back at $1,350, the other at $2,400. The homeowner signs the cheaper one, then pays another $700 later for stump grinding and hauling that were never written down anywhere.

That gap is rarely about greed. It is about what each crew put in writing and what it left out, and you can spot the difference before anyone pulls a saw off the truck.

A quote is a scope document. It should say which tree, how it comes down, what leaves your property, what stays behind, who carries the insurance, and what happens if the day goes badly. If the whole thing fits on one line next to a phone number, that is not a quote. It is a guess with a dollar sign on it.

## What a Complete Tree Removal Quote Actually Lists

Nine items. If any of them are missing, ask before you sign, because every one of them becomes an argument later.

**1. The tree, identified.** Species if known, rough height, trunk diameter, and where it stands on the property. "Remove one 55 ft silver maple, northeast corner near the fence" cannot be confused with the neighbor's tree. "Remove tree" can.

**2. The method.** Straight felling means the tree is cut at the base and dropped in one piece. Sectional dismantling means a climber or a lift takes it down in pieces, roped down over anything that would be damaged by a drop. Crane removal means a crane lifts each section out. The method drives most of the price, so it belongs on the page.

**3. Debris and wood.** Does the quote include hauling all brush, chips and logs off site, or does the wood stay stacked in your driveway? Firewood rounds left behind are a legitimate choice, but they should be a choice you made. If you are undecided, read up on [what to do with the wood after a removal](/blog/what-to-do-with-wood-after-removal/) first.

**4. The stump.** Grinding is usually a separate line. A good quote states the stump diameter, the grinding depth, and whether chips are raked out or left in the hole. Added to a removal, [stump grinding](/stump-grinding-boise/) typically runs $150 to $500 depending on size and access.

**5. Property protection.** Plywood or mats over the lawn and driveway for heavy equipment, protection for irrigation heads, a plan for the fence line. Ruts across a wet Treasure Valley lawn in March are expensive to repair.

**6. Access and staging.** Where the truck parks, whether the crew needs to come through a gate, and whether anything of yours has to move first. This is the number one cause of day-of price changes.

**7. Insurance.** General liability and workers compensation, with a certificate you can actually see.

**8. Permits.** Who pulls one, if one is needed.

**9. Price, terms and validity.** The total, what tax applies, the deposit and balance terms, and how long the price holds. Thirty days is normal. Prices for the same tree do shift with the season and crew demand.

## Why Two Tree Removal Estimates for the Same Tree Differ by $1,000

Height is only the starting point. [Tree removal in the Treasure Valley](/tree-removal-boise/) runs roughly $300 for a small tree in the open to $5,000 or more for a large or hazardous one, and the spread inside each tier comes down to six things.

**Drop zone.** A tree with 40 feet of clear grass under it can be felled in minutes. The same tree with a shed, a hot tub and a gas meter underneath has to come down piece by piece, and the labor can double.

**Access.** If a chipper can back within 30 feet of the trunk, brush handling is fast. If every branch has to be dragged 150 feet through a side gate, the crew is paying for that walk on every trip.

**Targets.** Roofs, fences, sheds, greenhouses, sprinkler lines and the neighbor's new siding all raise the rigging requirement. Rigging costs time.

**Wood weight.** Green cottonwood and green silver maple are heavy and awkward. A big cottonwood can produce several tons of wood, and disposal is a real line item.

**Condition.** A dead or decayed tree cannot be climbed safely, so it often needs a lift or a crane even when a healthy tree of the same size would not. That is a safety call, not a sales tactic.

**Timing.** Emergency and after-hours work costs more than a job scheduled three weeks out.

## Insurance, Permits and Credentials: The Part People Skip

Ask for the certificate of insurance to come to you from the insurance agent, not as a photo forwarded by the crew. Certificates expire, and a screenshot of an expired one looks identical to a current one. You are checking two coverages: general liability, which pays if your garage gets hit, and workers compensation, which matters because an uninsured climber injured in your tree can become your problem.

Credentials are verifiable. The International Society of Arboriculture publishes a public lookup so you can [verify an ISA certification](https://www.treesaregood.org/findanarborist/verify) by name. Our crews include ISA-certified arborists, and we carry $2M liability insurance, insured on every job.

Permits are the trap most homeowners walk into. Boise operates a street-tree permit regime for trees in the public right-of-way, which usually means the strip between the sidewalk and the curb, even though you mow it. The city's [community forestry program](https://www.cityofboise.org/departments/parks-and-recreation/community-forestry/) is the authority on which trees are covered. Removal is not automatically permit free, so read [tree removal permits in Boise](/blog/tree-removal-permits-boise/) before you sign anything on a front-yard tree.

One thing no quote should ever include: trimming or removing a tree that is touching a distribution power line. Idaho Power handles vegetation on its lines. Call them, not a tree service.

## Red Flags That Should Stop You Signing

**Full payment up front.** A deposit on a scheduled job is normal. The whole amount before any work is not.

**No written quote at all.** If it stays verbal, there is nothing to hold anyone to.

**A door knock right after a windstorm.** The Treasure Valley gets a wave of out-of-area crews after every big storm. Some are fine. Many leave with a deposit. See [storm chaser scams in Boise](/blog/tree-service-storm-chaser-scams-boise/) for what the pattern looks like.

**Topping recommended as tree care.** Cutting the crown back to stubs damages the tree and creates weak regrowth. A crew that offers it is telling you how it thinks.

**A price far below everything else.** Sometimes that is a crew filling a gap in the schedule. Often it is a crew without insurance, which is the single largest cost a legitimate tree service carries.

**Pressure to decide today.** A tree that has stood for 40 years can wait 48 hours, unless it is an active hazard, in which case you want an emergency crew rather than a bargain.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should a tree removal quote be free?

Yes, for a straightforward removal. On-site estimates are free with no obligation. The exception is a full tree health assessment, which is a different service and typically runs $75 to $250, often waived if the work is scheduled. That is diagnostic work, not a sales visit.

### How many quotes should I get?

Two or three is the sweet spot for most homeowners. More than that and you are mostly collecting numbers for jobs that are not scoped the same way. Give each crew the same brief, then compare the method and the inclusions, not just the total.

### Can the price change after I sign?

It should not, if the scope was written properly. Legitimate change orders happen when something is discovered that nobody could see from the ground, such as internal decay that forces a crane, or a buried structure in the drop zone. Ask before signing what triggers a change order and whether you get to approve it in writing first.

### Does my homeowner insurance pay for the removal?

Usually only when the tree has hit a covered structure. Idaho policies commonly cover $500 to $1,000 per tree for removal and up to $5,000 per storm event, with a deductible on top. A healthy tree you simply want gone is not a claim, and neither, in most policies, is a dead tree that fell on open lawn. Check your own policy language before you assume either way.
