# What Happens to the Wood After Tree Removal?

Your 80-year-old cottonwood just came down in the backyard, and now there is a wall of trunk sections and a small mountain of branches sitting on the lawn. Before the crew loads it all onto a truck and hauls it off, it is worth asking: what happens to this wood, and do you have a say in it?

You do. When homeowners ask us what to do with wood after tree removal, most do not realize how many options are actually on the table, and that some of those options change the price of the job. We handle this decision on nearly every removal we do across Boise, Meridian, and Nampa, so here is the honest rundown of what typically happens and what you can ask for instead.

**Deciding what to do with a tree coming down soon? [Ask us about wood and debris options](/#contact) when you schedule your free estimate.** It is easier to plan for than to sort out after the truck is already loaded.

## The Short Answer: You Usually Have Four Options

Most removals end up going one of four ways: you keep the wood for firewood, it gets chipped for mulch, a large trunk gets milled into lumber, or the crew hauls everything away and you never think about it again. Which one makes sense depends on the species, the size of the tree, whether you actually want to deal with firewood, and how the choice affects your removal cost.

## Option 1: Keep the Wood for Firewood

This is the most common request we get, especially for oak, ash, fruit trees, and other dense hardwoods that burn well. If you want to keep the rounds, tell us before the job starts. We will cut the trunk into manageable lengths (typically 16 to 18 inches for a standard wood stove) and leave them stacked on your property instead of hauling them to the dump.

**Here is the part people do not expect: keeping the wood usually lowers your removal quote.** Hauling green wood off-site costs us dump fees and truck time. When you keep it, that cost comes out of your bill. On a mid-size tree removal, that can mean $100 to $300 off the total, depending on volume.

The catch is that fresh-cut wood, called "green" wood, is not ready to burn. It needs to season.

## How Long Wood Needs to Season Before It Burns Right

Burning unseasoned wood wastes most of its heat boiling off water, and it coats your chimney with creosote, which is a genuine fire hazard. Seasoning times vary quite a bit by species.

| Species | Typical Seasoning Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ash | 6-12 months | Burns well even semi-green, but full seasoning is still better |
| Oak | 12-24 months | Dense wood, worth the wait, excellent heat output once dry |
| Maple | 9-12 months | Good all-around firewood |
| Fruit wood (apple, cherry) | 9-15 months | Great for smoking, dense enough to need real drying time |
| Cottonwood | 12+ months | Soft wood, lower heat value, still needs full seasoning |
| Softwood (pine, spruce) | 6-12 months | Burns fast and hot but leaves more creosote, better for kindling than main heat |

Stack wood off the ground, split it if the rounds are large, and give it airflow and sun. A tarp over the top (not wrapped all the way around) keeps rain off while letting moisture escape from the sides.

## Option 2: Have It Chipped for Mulch

Branches and smaller limbs usually go through a chipper regardless, since they are not much use as firewood. If you want the chips instead of having them hauled away, ask for a "chip drop." We will leave a pile of fresh wood chips on your property, which works well if you have garden beds, a large landscaped area, or bare soil you want covered.

Fresh chips are excellent mulch once they have had a few weeks to start breaking down, and they are free since they are already part of the job. The only real downside is volume. A single large tree can generate an enormous pile, often more than one property can use. If you only want part of it, let us know and we will split the difference between what stays and what goes.

## Option 3: Milling the Big Trunk Sections Into Lumber

This one surprises people. If you have a large-diameter trunk from a species worth milling (oak, walnut, some maples, even large ponderosa pine), it is sometimes possible to have those sections cut into rough lumber or live-edge slabs instead of firewood or mulch.

This is not something we do in-house, but there are portable sawmill operators in the Treasure Valley who will come to your property with a bandsaw mill if the logs are worth the trip. Realistically, this only makes financial sense for larger, straight sections, generally 18 inches or more in diameter, from species without a lot of internal decay. A cottonwood or a storm-damaged tree with rot inside is not going to yield much usable lumber no matter how big it is.

If you are interested in this route, tell us before we cut the trunk into short rounds. Once a large log is bucked into 16-inch firewood lengths, the milling option is gone.

Timing matters too. Logs sitting on the ground for more than a few weeks in warm weather start developing staining and decay that lowers their value for lumber, so if milling is the goal, arrange the sawyer visit or at least get the logs up off the ground and out of direct sun as soon as possible after the removal. A lot of homeowners find out about this option too late, after the wood has already been cut short and stacked for firewood, which is part of why we bring it up during the estimate rather than waiting for someone to ask.

## Option 4: Just Have Us Haul It Everything Away

This is the default for most residential removals, and there is nothing wrong with it. If you do not want to deal with a woodpile taking up yard space for a year while it seasons, or you simply do not burn wood, standard [tree removal](/tree-removal-boise/) pricing includes full cleanup and haul-away. We chip what can be chipped and haul the rest to a disposal or recycling facility, and some jurisdictions also run community chip-drop or yard waste programs; check with your city (Boise's is outlined at [cityofboise.org](https://www.cityofboise.org/)) if you want to explore drop-off options on your own instead of a full haul-away.

**A Meridian homeowner scenario:** A family off Ustick Road had a large silver maple removed after storm damage cracked a major limb. They did not want the wood or the chips, just a clean yard. The removal, including full haul-away of a roughly 60-foot tree, ran about $2,100. When we quoted a similar-sized tree for a neighbor who kept the rounds for firewood and took a chip drop for the branches, the total came in closer to $1,750 for comparable work, mostly because we were not hauling four cords of wood to the dump.

## Why the Stump Is a Completely Different Story

One thing people often assume: if you are keeping the trunk wood, can you also keep the stump for firewood later, once it dries out? No. Stump wood is not usable firewood. It has a much higher concentration of dirt, grit, and often decay than the trunk above it, and grinding is really the only practical way to deal with it. We cover stump options, including why leaving a stump in the ground invites its own problems, in our guide on [whether tree stumps attract termites](/blog/tree-stumps-attract-termites/). If you are handling both removal and stump grinding in the same job, ask about bundling them, since [stump grinding](/stump-grinding-boise/) as an add-on is usually cheaper than scheduling it separately. This comes up a lot with cottonwoods specifically, since they are one of the most commonly removed large trees in the valley; our [cottonwood removal guide](/blog/cottonwood-tree-removal-boise/) covers what that process typically involves start to finish, including the wood volume a mature cottonwood tends to produce.

## What About Diseased or Infested Wood?

This is where "keep the wood" sometimes gets overridden for safety reasons. If a tree came down because of a disease like fire blight, or if it shows signs of a pest concern like emerald ash borer, moving that wood off the property, especially as firewood someone might transport elsewhere, can spread the problem. Emerald ash borer in particular travels through moved firewood far more often than it travels on its own, which is part of why some areas restrict firewood movement. We will flag this during the estimate if it applies to your tree; for background on that specific pest, see our guide to [emerald ash borer in Idaho](/blog/emerald-ash-borer-idaho/). If a fire blight infection is the reason for removal, that wood should be disposed of rather than stacked near other susceptible trees; our [fire blight guide](/blog/fire-blight-treatment-boise/) goes into the pruning and sanitation side of that disease.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I ask to keep only part of the wood?** Yes. It is common to keep the larger rounds for firewood and have the branches chipped and hauled. Just tell us the split you want before the work starts.

**Does keeping the wood always save money?** Usually, but not always. On very large trees or difficult access jobs, hauling cost is a smaller piece of the total, so the savings may be modest. Ask for a breakdown when you get your estimate.

**How much firewood does a typical backyard tree produce?** A mature shade tree, 50 to 70 feet tall, can yield one to three cords depending on species and trunk diameter. A cord is a stack 4 feet by 4 feet by 8 feet.

**Is it safe to burn wood from a diseased tree?** Generally yes for heat, since burning kills most pathogens and pests, but it should not be transported off-property or stored near other vulnerable trees if the removal was due to something like emerald ash borer.

**What if I don't have a truck to move a chip drop pile?** We can typically place the chip pile wherever is most convenient on your property during the job, so you are not moving it afterward, only spreading it as you use it.

**Will you mill logs for me on-site?** We do not offer milling directly, but we can help coordinate timing with a local portable sawmill operator if you want to pursue that option on a large, sound trunk.

**Do stump grindings make good mulch?** Not really. Stump grindings are a mix of wood, dirt, and decayed material that breaks down differently than clean wood chips and is better used as fill rather than surface mulch around living plants.
