# How to Tell the Age of a Tree

The only exact way to tell the age of a tree is to count its growth rings, which means looking at a stump or a core sample from the trunk. For a living tree you don't want to harm, the practical answer is an estimate: measure the trunk, multiply by a species growth factor, and accept that the result could be off by a lot. On young pines and spruce you can do better by counting the layers of branches.

Each method has limits, and none of them tells you what most people actually want to know, which is whether the tree is safe and healthy. That part comes later in this article.

## Method 1: Count the rings on a stump

If the tree has already been cut down, this is the best option. Each year a tree adds a layer of wood under the bark. [Oregon State University Extension](https://ir.library.oregonstate.edu/downloads/4q77fr444) describes it this way: a band of light wood forms in spring and is followed by a darker band of denser summer wood, so one light-and-dark pair is one year.

To count them:

1. Make the surface as flat and clean as you can. A fresh saw cut works, and a light sanding or a damp cloth makes the bands easier to see.
2. Start at the center (the pith) and count outward to the bark, tracking each dark band.
3. Count in a couple of directions and compare, since rings can be uneven or hard to read in places.

The count isn't quite the tree's full age. Oregon State says that for stumps less than a foot tall, the ring count may be only about 2 years less than the total age, while a stump cut 3 feet tall will show fewer rings because the tree can take around 5 years to grow that high.

Where it goes wrong: a rotted or hollow center takes rings with it, tight outer rings on a slow-growing tree blur together, and drought or storm years can create faint extra bands that look like a year but aren't. If the stump is still in the ground, our guide to [stump grinding versus stump removal](/blog/stump-grinding-vs-stump-removal-boise/) explains what happens next, and it's worth counting rings before the grinder turns them to chips.

## Method 2: Estimate from the trunk size

This is the method behind most online tree age calculators, and it works on a living tree without touching it. It comes from the International Society of Arboriculture (ISA). A [handout from Allegheny County Parks](https://www.alleghenycounty.us/files/assets/county/v/1/parks/north-park/latodami/how-to-age-a-living-tree.pdf) describes the ISA formula, which it says is based on ring counts of cut trees:

1. Measure the trunk's circumference at 4 1/2 feet above the ground (54 inches).
2. Divide by 3.14 to get the diameter.
3. Multiply the diameter in inches by a growth factor for the species.

The handout's example is a white oak with a 126-inch circumference. That's about a 40-inch diameter, and with a white oak factor of 5, the estimate is roughly 200 years.

The same handout lists factors for dozens of species. A few that show up around Boise:

| Species | Growth factor |
|---|---|
| Cottonwood | 2 |
| Quaking aspen | 2 |
| Siberian elm | 2 |
| Silver maple | 3 |
| Honey locust | 3 |
| Colorado blue spruce | 4.5 |

The pattern makes sense: fast growers like cottonwood and Siberian elm have a low factor because they add inches of trunk quickly, and slow, dense trees have a high one. So a 30-inch silver maple works out to about 90 years, and a 30-inch cottonwood to about 60.

Here's the catch. The handout notes that growth rate varies considerably between species, and that tree health, soil, climate and site conditions also affect it. A tree watered every week in a Boise yard puts on wood far faster than the same species on a dry foothill, and a factor can't know which one you have. For a few species the handout lists a range because the sources it drew on disagreed. Treat the answer as a rough bracket, not a birth year. If the number matters, for a legal or historic purpose, ask an [ISA-certified arborist](/arborist-boise/) rather than trusting a calculator.

## Method 3: Count the whorls on a young pine or spruce

Many conifers, including most pines and spruce, put out one ring of branches each year at the top of the leader. Count the whorls from the top down and you have the age. Oregon State says this works well on species that form one distinct layer of branches each year, such as pines, true firs and Douglas-fir, and doesn't work on cedars, hemlocks and alder.

It has real limits:

- It's easy for about the first 30 years. After that, lower branches fall off and the pattern disappears. Look for limb scars on the trunk if you want to push further.
- A broken top can make you underestimate the age.
- Extra "false" whorls in a single year can make you overestimate it.

Whorl counting is good for a young tree you planted or a seedling you're curious about. It's not much help on a big old pine.

## Method 4: An increment borer

An increment borer is a hollow hand-turned bit that pulls a thin core of wood from the trunk, usually to the center. You count the rings in the core. [Oregon State's measuring guide](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9058-measuring-your-trees) says to bore horizontally at breast height, slightly farther than the tree's radius, so you reach the middle, then count the rings and add 6 to 10 years for the time the tree took to reach that height. It suggests 6 years for a high-growth site and 10 for a low-growth one.

The borer itself is a foresters' tool, and getting a usable core takes practice. If you miss the center, you undercount, and a rotted center means you can't find the pith at all.

Is it safe for the tree? The [U.S. Geological Survey](https://www.usgs.gov/media/images/sampling-trees-using-increment-borer) says coring with an increment borer leaves minimal evidence of the extraction and no long-term damage to tree growth or tree health. We'd still skip it on a valued yard tree out of curiosity. A hole through the bark is a wound, a stressed or already decaying tree has less ability to wall it off, and the answer you'd get is a number you could estimate well enough from the trunk. If you do want a core, have a professional take it.

## Which method to use

- **Tree already cut down:** count the rings on the stump.
- **Young pine or spruce:** count the whorls.
- **Living tree you care about:** measure the trunk and use a growth factor, and be clear it's an estimate.
- **A tree where the exact age matters:** have an arborist look at it, and ask them about coring only if it's warranted.

## Why age matters less than condition

People usually ask "how old is my tree?" because they're wondering if it's near the end of its life, if it's worth saving, or if it needs to come down. Age answers none of those.

A 120-year-old tree with a sound trunk and a full crown can be safer than a 40-year-old one with a split union, a lean or a decayed base. What decides risk is the tree's structure and health: cracks, cavities, dead wood, root damage, how it's leaning, and what's underneath it if it fails. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I tell how old a tree is without cutting it down?

Measure the trunk's circumference at 4 1/2 feet above the ground, divide by 3.14 to get the diameter, and multiply by a species growth factor (for example, 3 for silver maple in the Allegheny County Parks handout). It's an estimate, since growth varies with water, soil and site. For young pines and spruce, count the whorls of branches.

### Can I count tree rings on a living tree?

Not without taking a core sample, since the rings are inside the trunk. An increment borer pulls a pencil-thin core you can read. The USGS says the method leaves no long-term damage to tree growth or health. Still, we'd avoid coring a valued yard tree without a good reason, and leave it to a professional.

### Are tree age calculators accurate?

They're rough. Most use the ISA growth-factor formula, which multiplies the trunk diameter by a species number. Watering, soil and crowding change how fast a tree grows, so two trees of the same species and size can differ in age by decades. Use the result as a ballpark, not a certificate.

### Does a bigger trunk always mean an older tree?

No. A fast grower such as a cottonwood or silver maple can pack on trunk width in a few decades, while a slower tree may take far longer to reach the same size. A well-watered yard tree often grows faster than the same species in a poor site. Size is only a clue.

### How old is a tree if the stump is only partly there?

Count the rings you can see, then add the rings that would have formed above the cut. Oregon State says a stump under a foot tall may show only about 2 years fewer than the tree's age, while a 3-foot stump may show about 5 fewer. A rotted center means the count will come up short.

### Does the age of a tree tell me if it needs to be removed?

No. Condition matters far more than age. Cracks, decay, dead limbs, root problems and a lean are what raise risk, and young trees can have them too. If you're worried about a tree, have an arborist assess it, and see our [tree removal service](/tree-removal-boise/) if it turns out to be a hazard.
