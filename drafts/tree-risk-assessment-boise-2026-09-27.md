# Tree Risk Assessment: What an Arborist Actually Checks, and When You Need One

A tree risk assessment is a structured look at one question: how likely is this tree, or a specific part of it, to fail, and how bad would it be if it did. An arborist walks the tree, checks the trunk, crown, and root zone for defects, and weighs what's underneath it, a driveway, a bedroom window, a fence line, against how urgent the problem actually is.

It's different from asking "is my tree sick." A tree can be perfectly healthy and still carry a structural defect that makes a limb likely to drop in the next windstorm. A risk assessment is about structure and targets, not leaf color or growth rate.

If you're looking at a lean that seems new, a crack that wasn't there last year, or a big limb over the driveway that makes you nervous every time the wind picks up, this is the service that answers whether you're worrying about nothing or something that needs to come off the tree soon.

## The Framework Behind the Call

Arborists who specialize in this work use a method developed by the International Society of Arboriculture called Tree Risk Assessment Qualification, or TRAQ. It isn't a gut check. It breaks the question into three parts that get evaluated separately and then combined:

- **Likelihood of failure.** Is the defect improbable, possible, probable, or imminent, given what's actually wrong with the wood or roots.
- **Likelihood of impact.** If that part does fail, how likely is it to actually hit something. A limb over an open field is a different problem than the same limb over a patio.
- **Consequences.** How serious is the outcome if it does hit, from minor property damage up to a real injury risk.

Those three factors get combined into a rating, usually low, moderate, high, or extreme, for each specific defect and target combination on the tree ([ISA's TRAQ program page](https://www.isa-arbor.com/Credentials/Types-of-Credentials/ISA-Tree-Risk-Assessment-Qualification) covers the credential in more detail). The point of the framework is that two arborists looking at the same tree should land on close to the same answer, instead of two different guesses.

## The Three Levels of Assessment

Not every tree needs the same depth of inspection. The industry recognizes three levels, and most residential calls in Boise only need the first two.

| Level | What it involves | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Level 1: Limited visual | A quick look from one vantage point, such as a drive-by or a walk around the yard | Screening a whole property, or a first look after a storm |
| Level 2: Basic | A full 360-degree, ground-based walk around the tree, checking trunk, root flare, and crown from every side | The standard assessment for a specific tree someone is worried about |
| Level 3: Advanced | Diagnostic tools used on a specific defect the arborist already found | A trunk that sounds hollow, a crack that needs to be measured, a lean that needs a closer look at the root plate |

A Level 2 basic assessment is what most homeowners actually need: someone walking the full circumference of the tree, looking up into the crown, and checking the base and visible roots for the defects that actually cause failures. That's usually enough to answer "is this a problem" and "how soon."

## What Comes Out at the Advanced Level

Level 3 only gets used when a Level 2 walk-around turns up something that needs a closer answer than eyes alone can give. Depending on what's suspected, an arborist may bring out a sounding mallet to tap the trunk and listen for the hollow tone that suggests internal decay, or use a resistance drill that measures how much force it takes to push a thin probe through the wood, which maps solid wood against decayed or hollow sections without doing much damage to the tree.

Other advanced tools include tomography, which builds a cross-section image of the trunk's interior, and root collar excavation, where soil is carefully removed to check the root flare and buttress roots for decay or girdling. These tools cost more time and more equipment, which is why they only come out for a specific question a Level 2 assessment couldn't answer on its own, not as a routine step.

## What You Get on Paper

A verbal opinion is fine for a quick yes-or-no. If you're dealing with an insurance question, a dispute with a neighbor, a real estate transaction, or a tree the city or an HOA has flagged, you want the findings in writing. A written report typically lists the defects found, the risk rating for each one, photos, and a recommendation: monitor, prune, cable, or remove.

## Tree Risk Assessment vs. Tree Health Assessment

These get confused often enough that it's worth separating them plainly. A risk assessment asks whether a tree, or part of it, is likely to fail and hurt someone or something. A health assessment asks why a tree looks stressed, whether it's a pest, a disease, drought, soil compaction, or something else, and whether it can recover.

A tree can fail a risk assessment while looking green and full, if the problem is a structural defect like a crack or a weak branch union rather than declining health. And a tree can be visibly stressed, with yellowing leaves or thin foliage, and still pose no structural risk at all. The [tree health assessment page](/blog/tree-health-assessment-boise/) covers what that side of the inspection looks for and what it costs. Sometimes one visit covers both questions; sometimes only one applies to your situation.

## Signs That Mean You Should Schedule One Now

Most trees never need a formal risk assessment. These are the situations where it's worth getting an arborist to look rather than waiting:

- A lean that's new, or has clearly gotten worse, especially if the soil is lifting on one side of the trunk
- A crack running along the trunk, particularly one that goes deep or wraps around the circumference
- A cavity or soft, hollow-sounding section of trunk
- A large dead limb, or multiple dead limbs, positioned over a driveway, deck, or roof
- Mushrooms or fungal growth at the base of the trunk, which often points to root or lower-trunk decay
- Roots that have been cut, damaged by construction, or are visibly girdling the trunk
- A branch union that splits into a tight V-shape rather than a wide U-shape, which is structurally weaker

If any of that describes a tree on your property, especially one positioned over something you'd rather not repair, it's worth having someone look before the next windstorm makes the decision for you. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does a tree risk assessment take?

A Level 2 basic assessment on one tree usually takes 20 to 45 minutes on site, depending on the tree's size and how many defects need a closer look. If advanced diagnostic tools are needed, plan on more time, since sounding and drilling take longer than a visual walk-around.

### Is a risk assessment the same thing as an arborist report?

No. The assessment is the inspection itself. A report is a written document summarizing what was found, with photos and a risk rating, and it's a separate deliverable you'd request specifically, usually for insurance, a legal dispute, or a permit application.

### Will I still have to pay for the assessment if I decide to have the tree removed?

Often the assessment fee is waived when you move forward with the recommended work. Ask about this up front, since it depends on the specific job.

### Can an arborist tell me for certain a tree won't fall?

No arborist can guarantee that, and be skeptical of anyone who does. A risk assessment gives you a rating, low, moderate, high, or extreme, based on what's visible and measurable. It narrows the odds and tells you what to watch for, but wind, saturated soil, and hidden root decay can change the picture.

### What happens if the tree comes back as high risk?

The arborist should give you options, not just a removal quote. Depending on the defect, that might mean pruning to reduce weight, cabling or bracing to support a weak union, or removal if the defect can't be safely corrected. The [tree cabling and bracing article](/blog/tree-cabling-bracing-boise/) covers when support is a real alternative to taking the tree down.

### Do I need a risk assessment before every tree removal?

No. If a tree is obviously dead, badly storm-damaged, or you've already decided to remove it for a non-safety reason like construction, you can usually skip straight to a removal estimate. Assessments matter most when you're not sure whether a tree is actually dangerous or just looks concerning.
