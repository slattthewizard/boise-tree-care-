# Water Sprouts: Why Your Tree Shoots Straight Up After Pruning

Water sprouts are the thin, fast-growing shoots that stand nearly straight up from a tree's branches or trunk, usually after a hard pruning cut, storm damage, or a stressful summer. They are the tree's emergency response: dormant buds under the bark wake up and push out new growth to replace leaf area it lost. Most water sprouts are weakly attached and worth cutting out, but a few can be trained into useful replacement limbs if you know which ones to keep.

If your tree looks like it grew a bottle brush overnight, this is almost certainly what you are seeing. The fix depends on why the sprouts showed up in the first place and what kind of tree you have.

## What Are Water Sprouts, Exactly?

Water sprouts are a type of epicormic shoot, a term arborists use for any new growth that erupts from a dormant or "latent" bud on older wood rather than from the tip of an existing branch. On most shade trees they show up as clusters of skinny, upright stems, often several feet long in a single season, growing along the top or inside of a branch. Because they grow so fast and so vertically, they look nothing like the rest of the tree's normal branching.

The wood inside a water sprout is weaker than wood grown gradually at a branch tip. The bark where it attaches rarely forms a strong union with the parent branch, so sprouts tend to split off in wind or under their own weight as they get bigger. That is the main reason arborists remove most of them rather than let them stay.

## Water Sprouts vs. Suckers: What's the Difference

Water sprouts and suckers look almost identical, straight, skinny, fast-growing shoots, but they come from different parts of the tree. According to [University of Maryland Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/water-sprouts-or-suckers-trees), water sprouts develop from dormant buds on the trunk or large branches, while suckers originate from the roots or from the trunk below a graft union. Our [guide to tree suckers and root sprouts](/blog/tree-suckers-root-sprouts/) covers why those show up and how to deal with them, since the removal approach is a little different.

The distinction matters most on grafted trees, like most fruit and ornamental varieties, where root suckers can grow from the rootstock below the graft and will never produce the fruit or flowers you expect. Water sprouts above the graft are at least the variety you planted, even if the growth itself is a problem.

## Why Pruning Triggers Water Sprouts

The single most common cause of a sudden flush of water sprouts is a hard pruning cut, especially a large one made mid-branch instead of back to a natural junction. When a tree loses a big share of its leaf-bearing wood at once, it still has roots sized for the tree it used to be. Those roots keep pushing sugars and water up, and with fewer leaves to use them, the tree opens up dormant buds near the cut to grow new leaf area fast. The bigger and more numerous the cuts, the bigger the sprout response tends to be, which is part of why [how often you trim a tree](/blog/how-often-should-trees-be-trimmed/) affects how much sprouting you see afterward.

Topping, cutting main branches down to stubs instead of pruning back to a lateral branch, is the worst offender by far. It removes a huge share of the canopy at once and leaves large wounds that are slow to close, and the tree typically answers with a dense thicket of water sprouts around every cut within a year. We cover why that cycle gets worse every time it is repeated in [our article on why topping is bad for Boise trees](/blog/tree-topping-bad-boise/). If a tree on your property was topped in the past and is now covered in upright growth, that is almost always what you are looking at.

## Other Causes: Stress, Storm Damage, and Disease

Pruning is not the only trigger. Water sprouts can also show up after:

- **Storm or wind damage** that breaks off a significant amount of canopy, which the tree treats the same way it would treat a hard pruning cut.
- **[Drought stress](/blog/drought-stress-trees-boise/)**, which can push a tree into the same emergency growth response as a hard pruning cut.
- **Root loss or soil compaction**, including construction work that cut roots or compacted the soil near the trunk.
- **Disease**, where a struggling tree pushes out sprouts as part of a broader stress response.

[University of New Hampshire Extension](https://extension.unh.edu/blog/2021/02/can-water-sprouts-suckers-be-prevented-trees) lists storm damage, root loss, soil compaction, disease, over-pruning, and drought as common triggers, and notes that water sprouts and suckers are weakly attached to the tree unless they have been allowed to grow for several years. In other words, the sprouts are usually a symptom worth paying attention to, not just a cosmetic nuisance. Removing them without correcting whatever stressed the tree in the first place tends to bring them right back.

## Should You Remove Water Sprouts?

For most shade and ornamental trees, yes. Water sprouts crowd the canopy, block light and air movement to the leaves underneath, and because of their weak attachment they are more likely to snap in a summer thunderstorm or winter ice event than a normally grown branch. Left alone for a few years, a thicket of sprouts also makes future pruning harder and more expensive, since what could have been a few clean cuts becomes dozens.

The usual approach follows the same clean-cut rule as [pruning a tree properly](/blog/how-to-prune-a-tree/): remove sprouts back to where they emerge from the branch or trunk, flush with the bark, not partway, since a stub left behind tends to sprout again. On a tree with a heavy sprout response, that can mean a lot of small cuts. If sprouts have been building up for more than a season or two, a trained eye is worth it: removing too many at once repeats the same stress cycle that caused the sprouts in the first place.

For DIY-sized jobs, a homeowner with pole pruners can usually manage light water sprout cleanup on lower branches within reach. ## When to Remove Them: Timing Matters

Timing changes how the tree responds. Cutting large limbs during the dormant season, late fall through late winter, tends to trigger a stronger flush of new sprouts the following spring, because the tree still has a full root system's worth of stored energy and fewer buds now competing for it. [Utah State University Extension](https://extension.usu.edu/yardandgarden/research/training-and-pruning-apple-trees) recommends removing water sprouts in late spring to early summer instead, while they are still soft enough to rub or tear off by hand, since that produces less regrowth than cutting them out during dormant pruning.

That is why, if a tree is producing water sprouts every year after a bad topping job or storm damage, summer touch-up removal alongside a lighter dormant-season structural prune usually gets the sprout problem under control faster than one big dormant cut.

## The Exception: Fruit Trees and Replacement Limbs

Not every water sprout is a problem to remove. On apple, pear, and other fruit trees, a well-placed water sprout can be trained into a replacement scaffold limb, especially after storm damage or a hard renewal prune took out an older branch. The trick is selecting one, sometimes two, of the strongest, best-positioned sprouts near the cut and removing the rest, then heading that sprout back over the next couple of seasons to encourage side branching instead of one long whip.

This is a judgment call that depends on the tree's shape, age, and how much of the canopy you are trying to rebuild, and it is easy to get wrong by keeping too many sprouts or the wrong ones. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will cutting off water sprouts kill my tree?

No, removing water sprouts does not harm a healthy tree. They are new growth the tree does not need to survive, and pruning them off, ideally flush with the branch or trunk, redirects the tree's energy back to its existing structure instead of the new upright shoots.

### Do water sprouts ever turn into fruit?

Rarely, and not for several years even when they do. A water sprout has to mature into a fruiting-age branch first, which usually takes a few growing seasons, and most gardeners remove them before that happens because they crowd the canopy and shade out branches that are already producing.

### Why does my tree keep growing water sprouts every year?

Repeated sprouting usually means the underlying cause has not gone away, most often a topping cut, ongoing stress like drought or root damage, or sprouts being cut back to a stub instead of removed flush to the branch. Addressing the root cause, not just cutting the current crop of sprouts, is what breaks the cycle.

### Are water sprouts the same as suckers?

No. Water sprouts grow from buds on branches or the trunk above ground, while suckers grow from the roots or from the base of the trunk near the soil line. The removal approach for suckers is a little different, since you are dealing with root growth rather than branch growth.

### When is the best time to remove water sprouts?

Late spring to early summer, while the sprouts are still soft, tends to produce less regrowth than cutting them out during dormant-season pruning. Rubbing or tearing off small, succulent sprouts by hand works well at that stage; larger woody ones need bypass pruners.

### Can I just leave water sprouts alone?

You can for a season while you decide on a plan, but leaving them for several years lets weakly attached wood build up in the canopy and makes eventual cleanup a bigger job. On fruit trees, a few well-chosen sprouts near a pruning wound are worth keeping as replacement limbs; the rest are usually better removed while they are still small.
