# Honey Locust Trees: A Good Boise Shade Tree, With a Few Catches

A honey locust is one of the better shade tree choices for a Boise yard: it tolerates alkaline, clay, or compacted soil, casts light, filtered shade instead of deep gloom, and grows fast enough to show real results within a few years. The thornless varieties sold at Treasure Valley nurseries were bred specifically to drop the thorns, and many of them drop most of the seed pods too.

It is not maintenance-free, though. Honey locust leafs out later in spring than most trees on your block, some cultivars still pod, and a short list of pests and branch problems shows up often enough in this region that it helps to know what you are looking at.

This guide covers what a honey locust actually needs in Boise, the catches nobody mentions at the nursery, and when a problem is normal versus worth a call.

## What Is a Honey Locust Tree?

Honey locust (Gleditsia triacanthos) is a fast-growing deciduous tree native to the central United States. The wild species carries long thorns and heavy seed pods, which is why nearly every honey locust sold for landscaping today is the thornless variety, Gleditsia triacanthos var. inermis, sold under cultivar names like Skyline, Imperial, and Sunburst.

The small, fern-like leaflets let light through instead of blocking it, which is why grass and garden beds underneath a honey locust tend to do better than under a maple or oak. [North Carolina State University's plant database](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/gleditsia-triacanthos-var-inermis/) confirms the tree casts light shade that still allows grass to grow underneath, and lists several of the common fruitless (podless) cultivars sold today. Mature height typically runs 30-70 feet with a broad, spreading canopy, part of why it shows up so often as a street tree and yard shade tree in the Treasure Valley.

## Why Honey Locust Handles Boise's Soil So Well

Treasure Valley soils are often alkaline, and many yards sit on clay or compacted fill left from construction. That combination causes iron chlorosis, a yellowing between the leaf veins, in trees that prefer acidic soil, such as maples and some oaks.

Honey locust is one of the more tolerant species for alkaline, clay, and dry soil. The [UC Statewide Integrated Pest Management Program](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/honey-locust/) describes it as tolerant of acid to alkaline soils and harsh growing conditions generally, which lines up with why it holds up in so many Treasure Valley yards and parking strips. That tolerance is not unlimited: compacted soil, over-irrigation, and turf competition can still stress a honey locust, just less severely than they stress a fussier species.

## Why Your Honey Locust Looks Dead Every Spring

Honey locust leafs out later in spring than most Treasure Valley trees. The same UC IPM source notes that honey locust trees leaf out late, go dormant early, and drop their leaves early in fall compared to many other shade trees. If you planted one recently or just moved into a house with one, it is easy to assume the tree died over winter when the neighbor's maple is already fully leafed out.

Before you worry, check for these signs of life:
- Bend a small twig. If it is flexible and green underneath the bark, the tree is alive.
- Scratch a small patch of bark with your fingernail on a low branch. Green tissue underneath is a good sign.
- Give it another two to three weeks past when other species leafed out before assuming a problem.

If you still see no buds swelling once nearby honey locusts on the same street have leafed out, or the bark underneath is brown and dry, that is a better reason to have someone look at it. ## Honey Locust Pods: Which Cultivars Actually Drop Them

The wild species produces long, twisted brown seed pods that can litter a yard by the bushel. Most named thornless cultivars were selected to be low-pod or nearly podless, but "nearly podless" is not the same as "never." Depending on the specific cultivar and the growing season, older plantings can still drop some pods, especially trees that predate the more consistently podless cultivars.

If you are choosing a tree to plant, ask the nursery which cultivar it is. Skyline, Imperial, and Sunburst are commonly sold as low-pod to fruitless selections. If you already have a mature tree dropping pods, raking is really the only fix. There is no legitimate spray or treatment that stops a genetically pod-bearing tree from podding.

## Common Honey Locust Problems in the Treasure Valley

Honey locust is generally a low-problem tree compared to species like ash or aspen, but a few issues come up regularly here. The [Colorado State University Extension guide to honeylocust insect and mite pests](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/insect-and-mite-pests-of-honeylocust/) covers the two most common ones in detail: honeylocust plant bug and spider mites.

| Problem | What you will notice | How serious it usually is |
|---|---|---|
| Honeylocust plant bug | Yellow or brown spotting and twisted, dwarfed new leaves in spring | Cosmetic on an established tree; rarely a lasting threat |
| Spider mites | Bronzing or stippling on leaves, worse in hot, dry conditions | Worse on drought-stressed trees; usually eases with consistent watering |
| Canker disease | Sunken bark patches with dark spots on branches, usually after drought or storm stress | Typically causes some branch dieback rather than killing the tree, but worth a look |
| Weak branch unions | A branch that grew at a tight, V-shaped angle to the trunk rather than a wide U | Increases the odds that branch splits out in wind or snow load |

Plant bugs and spider mites are the most common calls, and CSU Extension notes honeylocust is generally considered a pest-resistant species where these infestations rarely affect overall tree health. Canker and weak branch unions are the two worth taking seriously, since either can end with a branch or a larger section of the tree coming down. ## Weak Branch Unions: The Structural Issue to Watch

Honey locust has a tendency to grow codominant stems, two trunks of similar size, that attach at a narrow angle with bark trapped inside the joint instead of solid wood. [Colorado State University Extension's guide to structural pruning](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/structural-pruning-of-young-shade-trees/) treats codominant trunks as a major structural defect, since the included bark keeps the two stems from ever knitting together into strong wood. That is one of the more common reasons a honey locust limb fails during a windstorm or heavy wet snow rather than a healthy limb simply snapping.

The fix is not waiting for a failure. Structural pruning while the tree is young, removing or shortening competing leaders so one central trunk dominates, prevents most of these weak unions from ever forming. On a mature tree with an existing weak union, cabling or bracing can sometimes reduce the risk without removing the branch outright; our [tree cabling and bracing page](/blog/tree-cabling-bracing-boise/) covers when that makes sense.

## Pruning Schedule for a Healthy Honey Locust

Honey locust responds well to routine pruning and tends to need less of it than many fast-growing, weaker-wooded shade trees. A simple schedule:

1. **Years 1-5 (structural pruning):** Prune in late winter before the tree breaks dormancy to establish one dominant leader and remove competing codominant stems. CSU Extension recommends this timing specifically because it is easier to evaluate branch structure before leaves come in, and wounds close faster.
2. **Established tree, every 2-4 years:** Light maintenance pruning to remove dead, crossing, or rubbing branches and keep the canopy open. Our guide on [how often trees should be trimmed](/blog/how-often-should-trees-be-trimmed/) covers the general interval for mature shade trees.
3. **After any storm:** Check for hanging or cracked limbs, especially at branch unions, and have anything questionable removed before it fails on its own schedule.

Avoid heavy pruning in late spring or early summer when the tree is pushing new growth. For technique and how it applies to your yard, see [our trimming and pruning service page](/tree-trimming-boise/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does my honey locust have thorns if it is supposed to be thornless?

Some older trees, or trees grown from seed rather than grafted from a named cultivar, can revert toward the thorny wild type, especially on root suckers or water sprouts. If the main trunk and canopy are thorny, it may not be a true thornless cultivar to begin with, or a thorny sucker grew from below a graft union.

### Are honey locust roots a problem for foundations or sidewalks?

Honey locust roots are generally considered less aggressive than species like cottonwood or willow, but any tree planted too close to a foundation, sidewalk, or sewer line can eventually cause lifting or intrusion as it matures. Planting distance from hardscape and utilities matters more than the species alone.

### Is honey locust sap or pod residue toxic to pets?

We are not veterinarians and cannot give medical guidance. If a pet or child has eaten pods or bark and you are concerned, contact a vet, physician, or poison control rather than relying on general information online.

### How fast does a honey locust grow?

Honey locust is generally considered a fast-growing shade tree, often adding meaningful height in its first several years after planting, which is part of why it is popular on new construction lots that want shade sooner rather than later.

### Can I stop a honey locust from producing pods?

Not with a spray or treatment. Whether a specific tree pods heavily depends on its cultivar and genetics. If pod litter is a dealbreaker, the fix is choosing a documented low-pod or podless cultivar when you plant, or raking on an existing tree.

### Does a honey locust need extra water in Boise summers?

An established honey locust tolerates drought better than many species, but young trees, and any honey locust during Boise's hottest, driest stretches, still benefit from deep, infrequent watering rather than being left entirely on their own.
