# Reporting a Neighbor's Dead Tree in Boise: What the Code Actually Does

Reporting a neighbor's dead tree in Boise can force action, but only when that tree threatens something public: a sidewalk, a street, streetlighting, or the health of the city's own trees. If the danger is only to your fence, your roof, or your yard, the ordinance has no jurisdiction, and calling the city usually goes nowhere.

That's a different problem than the two tree disputes we hear about most. A neighbor's healthy tree with branches hanging into your yard is a trimming question, covered in [when you can trim a neighbor's tree branches in Idaho](/blog/neighbor-tree-trimming-rights-idaho/). A tree that's already down and lying across your fence is an insurance and negligence question, covered in [who pays when a tree falls on your fence in Idaho](/blog/tree-fell-on-fence-responsibility-idaho/). This article covers the tree that's dead or clearly dying, still standing, and hasn't fallen yet, the window where reporting it correctly can keep you out of the other two situations entirely.

## What Actually Makes a Dead Tree a "Public Nuisance" in Boise

Boise's tree ordinance, Title 7, Chapter 2 of the city code, declares a tree a public nuisance, whether it sits on public or private property, when it meets one of three tests: it's a hazard to public safety as determined by the city forester, it obstructs pedestrian or vehicle passage or blocks streetlighting, or it harbors pests that threaten the city's own trees, historically written with Dutch elm disease specifically in mind.

Notice what's doing the work in that first test: "as determined by the city forester." A private homeowner can't declare a neighbor's tree a nuisance by pointing at a dead branch. The city's own forestry staff has to look at it and agree it crosses that line, usually because it endangers a sidewalk, a street, or a right-of-way, not just a backyard.

Under section 7-2-8 of the same chapter, once a private tree is found to meet that public-nuisance definition, the owner becomes responsible for pruning or removing it. If they don't, the city forester can order the tree cut down and bill the cost back to the property. That's real leverage, but it only switches on when the public-safety trigger is met.

## Why the City Often Can't Touch a Purely Private Dead Tree

Here's the part that surprises most homeowners: Boise, Nampa, Caldwell, and Meridian all administer their tree codes the same way. If a dead or dying tree only threatens a neighbor's private property, a fence, a shed, a patio, and doesn't reach a sidewalk, street, or utility right-of-way, none of these cities will use their code to force removal. It gets treated as a civil matter between the two property owners, not a code violation.

**A homeowner off Chinden Boulevard in Garden City** ran into exactly this. A dead cottonwood on the neighboring lot leaned toward a shared cedar fence but stood 40 feet from the street. Code compliance confirmed the tree didn't meet the public-nuisance test and closed the file the same week. The homeowner paid $175 for a written arborist hazard report, shared it with the neighbor directly, and the two split a $1,900 removal bill rather than let it become a bigger fight.

That outcome is common. The code path exists, but for most backyard-to-backyard situations, a documented conversation with your neighbor gets you further than a complaint number.

## How to Actually Report a Hazardous Tree in Boise

If the tree in question does reach a sidewalk, street, or the right-of-way, here's the real process, not the version people assume exists.

1. **Call Boise Community Forestry at (208) 608-7700** or use the [online tree inspection request](https://www.cityofboise.org/departments/parks-and-recreation/community-forestry/public-trees/request-a-tree-inspection/). Inspections are free, and an ISA-certified city forester will assess whether the tree meets the 7-2-12 nuisance definition.
2. If it does, the city can require the owner to prune or remove it, and can act itself and assess the cost if the owner ignores the order.
3. For broader property-condition nuisances that threaten health or safety more generally, Boise's separate nuisance chapter lets the city attorney's office issue a notice giving the owner up to 10 days to abate, with city abatement to follow if they don't.
4. To start a general code compliance complaint instead, call (208) 972-8150, option 4, or file through the [city's report-an-issue portal](https://www.cityofboise.org/government/report-an-issue-or-file-a-complaint/).

**A property owner near Nampa's Sugar Street corridor** had a dead ash leaning over a public sidewalk from the lot next door. A call to code compliance triggered a city forester inspection, which confirmed the pedestrian-obstruction test under the tree ordinance. The neighbor received formal notice and had the tree removed for roughly $1,150 within the window rather than risk the city doing it and billing them for more.

Readers outside Boise proper, in [Nampa](/tree-service-nampa/), Caldwell, Meridian, Eagle, or Garden City, need to check with that city's own code compliance office, since each municipality runs its own version of this ordinance rather than deferring to Ada or Canyon County.

## What This Costs, and Who Pays

Getting ahead of a dead tree is almost always cheaper than the alternative. A tree health assessment from an ISA-certified arborist runs $75 to $250, often waived if you schedule the removal work at the same time, and gives you documentation the city, your neighbor, or an insurer will actually take seriously.

Removal costs scale with size: a small tree under 30 feet typically runs $300 to $800, a medium 30 to 60-foot tree runs $800 to $2,500, and a large tree over 60 feet can run $2,500 to $5,000 or more, especially with crane access or tight clearance near a structure. Stump grinding adds $150 to $500 depending on diameter.

If the tree is already dropping limbs onto a roof or car before anyone acts, emergency limb removal off a structure runs $150 to $850, and a full tree already resting on a structure runs $1,500 to $4,000 or more. Idaho homeowner policies typically cover $500 to $1,000 per tree for storm-related removal, up to $5,000 per storm event, which is worth knowing before you assume a claim will cover everything.

Reporting a dead tree is a paperwork problem. Handling one yourself can turn into a safety problem fast. Stop and call a professional the moment any of these apply: the work would require a chainsaw above shoulder height, the tree or its limbs are within reach of a power line, cutting requires climbing, or a failure could reach a house, garage, or parked car.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I make the city force my neighbor to remove a dead tree?

Only if the tree meets Boise's public-nuisance definition under the tree ordinance, meaning it threatens a sidewalk, street, streetlighting, or public right-of-way, and the city forester agrees after an inspection. If the tree only threatens your private property, the city has no authority to force removal, and it becomes a direct conversation with your neighbor instead.

### What number do I call to report a hazardous tree in Boise?

For a tree touching a public sidewalk, street, or right-of-way, call Boise Community Forestry at (208) 608-7700 for a free inspection. For a broader code compliance complaint, call (208) 972-8150, option 4. Nampa, Caldwell, Meridian, Eagle, and Garden City each run their own code compliance office, so check with that specific city if the tree isn't in Boise.

### What if the city says it isn't their problem?

That's the common outcome for a dead tree that only threatens private property. Document the tree with dated photos, notify your neighbor in writing, and consider a paid arborist hazard assessment. That documentation is exactly what protects you later if the tree eventually falls and the liability question in a fallen-tree claim comes down to what your neighbor knew and when.

### Is a dead tree automatically a hazard?

No. Some standing dead trees remain structurally stable for years, especially smaller ones away from any structure. What matters is trunk and root condition, lean, and proximity to something that could be damaged, which is exactly what a professional assessment is built to determine rather than a guess from the ground.

### How fast does the city act once it accepts a report?

Once a city forester confirms a tree meets the nuisance definition, the owner is given a notice period to address it, and the city can act and bill the cost back to the property if the deadline passes. It is not instant, so if the tree already looks unstable near people or a structure, that's a same-day call to a tree service, not a wait-and-see on a city timeline.
