# Black Locust Tree: What to Know Before You Keep, Trim or Remove One

A black locust tree (Robinia pseudoacacia) is a tough, fast-growing deciduous tree with deeply furrowed bark, paired thorns, and hanging clusters of fragrant white flowers in late spring. It handles drought and poor soil without complaint, which is exactly why it ends up in yards, along ditches and on vacant lots. It also sends up root suckers, drops brittle branches, is prone to a damaging borer, and has bark, leaves and seeds that are poisonous. We wouldn't plant one in a residential yard.

If you've got one already, or you've inherited one with a house, here's how to identify it, what problems to expect, and what removal really involves.

## How to identify a black locust tree

The [North Carolina State Extension Plant Toolbox](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/robinia-pseudoacacia/) describes black locust bark as gray or light brown, thick, fibrous and deeply furrowed, with heavy ridges. The leaves are pinnately compound (one long stem lined with many small leaflets, typically 7 to 23), dark blue-green, and turn yellow in fall. Branches usually carry short paired spines beside each leaf scar. Flowers are white, pea-like and fragrant, hanging in clusters from April to June. The seed pods that follow are flat, smooth and purple-brown, and they can hang on through winter.

[Oregon State University's landscape plants database](https://landscapeplants.oregonstate.edu/plants/robinia-pseudoacacia) lists a typical height of 30 to 50 feet, with the occasional tree reaching 80, and notes the thorns are more prominent on vigorous shoots and suckers. In practice, a thorny sprout coming up in the middle of your lawn or flower bed is often your first clue that a black locust is nearby.

## Black locust vs. honey locust

People mix these up constantly because both are "locusts" with feathery compound leaves. The differences are easy to spot once you know them:

- **Thorns:** Wild honey locust carries large, branched thorns, often on the trunk. Black locust has a pair of short spines at each leaf node.
- **Flowers and pods:** Black locust blooms with showy white pea-like flower clusters and makes smooth, flat pods. Honey locust flowers are small and greenish, and its pods are long and twisted.
- **Planting status:** Most honey locusts sold for landscapes are thornless cultivars. Black locust isn't a tree most nurseries promote for home lots.

Our [honey locust guide](/blog/honey-locust-tree/) covers that tree's pods, late leaf-out and pests in more detail. If you aren't sure which one you have, the flower and pod are the quickest tells, and a certified arborist can settle it on a site visit.

## Drought tolerance: the good part

Here's what black locust genuinely has going for it. NC State Extension calls it heat and drought tolerant, and Oregon State quotes plant authority Michael Dirr describing it as an "alley cat" tree that can survive under the toughest conditions. Oregon State lists it as growing in about any soil except permanently wet ones.

That toughness is real, but it has limits. NC State notes that during drought some leaves turn yellow and drop, and drought-stressed trees are more vulnerable to the borer we'll get to below. If you've got one, an occasional deep soak in a hot, dry stretch is still sensible. Our guide on [how to water trees in Boise](/blog/how-to-water-trees-boise/) covers how.

## Root suckers and thickets

This is the main reason we steer people away from black locust. NC State says the tree sends out long underground root suckers that can become a maintenance problem and disrupt nearby garden beds. [University of Illinois Extension](https://web.extension.illinois.edu/hortanswers/PlantDetail.cfm?PlantID=267&PlantTypeID=7) says the tree can form thickets by suckering roots, and that this habit limits its usefulness in home landscapes.

The suckers don't stay politely at the base of the trunk, either. They can pop up well out in the lawn, along a fence line, or on the neighbor's side. The [USDA Forest Service's Fire Effects Information System](https://research.fs.usda.gov/feis/species-reviews/robpse) says black locust commonly sprouts from roots or stumps, and that sprouting often follows stem or root damage, including cutting. So mowing over a sucker or slicing roots with a trencher doesn't solve the problem. It tends to make more of them.

If suckers are already coming up, our article on [tree suckers and root sprouts](/blog/tree-suckers-root-sprouts/) explains how suckers differ from other shoots and how to deal with them. For a black locust, expect to repeat the job for years unless the parent tree is dealt with.

## Brittle limbs and locust borer

Black locust wood is hard, but the branches are not reliable. NC State says branches are brittle and can break off in wind, and Illinois Extension says weak-wooded branches may be damaged by wind. Add the thorns and you get a tree that's unpleasant to clean up after a Treasure Valley windstorm.

The bigger long-term threat is the locust borer. Illinois Extension calls locust borers a serious problem, and NC State says the borer is often fatal to the tree. The Forest Service review describes a case on the Oklahoma high plains where drought combined with locust borer attack killed 55.5 percent of black locust trees between 1930 and 1937. The takeaway: a stressed tree is an easy target. Keep the tree watered in dry spells, avoid wounding the trunk with mowers and string trimmers, and have an [ISA-certified arborist look at it](/arborist-boise/) if you see trunk damage, holes in the bark, or branches dying back.

## Is black locust toxic?

Yes. NC State says the bark, leaves and seeds are poisonous, rates the severity as high, and says ingestion can be fatal to humans, livestock and pets. Oregon State says the twigs and bark are poisonous to domestic livestock.

Practical consequences:

- Don't let kids or pets chew on bark, pods or seeds.
- If you keep horses or other livestock, don't leave a black locust in or at the edge of a pasture, and clear fallen limbs and trimmings out of reach.
- Don't use fresh trimmings as mulch where animals can get to them.

We aren't doctors or vets. If a child or animal has eaten any part of the tree, call poison control or a veterinarian right away rather than waiting to see what happens.

## The wood: very rot resistant, good for posts

NC State notes black locust wood is naturally rot resistant and commonly used for fence posts and rails, mine timbers and landscape ties. That's a decent upside if you're removing a tree and have a use for straight sections. For burning, it's dense wood, but like anything else it needs time to dry. Our [firewood seasoning guide](/blog/seasoning-firewood-boise/) covers drying and storage. Keep the livestock warning in mind before you send trimmings to anyone with horses.

## Is black locust invasive in Idaho?

Not a question with a tidy answer. The Forest Service review lists black locust as invasive and says it has been widely planted and often escapes cultivation. It also reports the tree growing in southwestern Idaho sagebrush rangeland and on steep slopes in the northern Idaho Palouse. That tells you it can establish itself on its own in this part of the country. It doesn't tell you its legal status, and we're not making a legal claim either way. For regulatory questions, check with the Idaho State Department of Agriculture.

What we can say from a yard-care standpoint: if you have one, don't let the suckers and seed pods carry it beyond your fence line.

## Removal and resprouting

Cutting a black locust down is the easy part. Keeping it from coming back is the hard part. Because the species resprouts from stumps and roots after cutting, a plain cut with no follow-up commonly turns one tree into a patch of shoots. A few things help:

1. **Remove the tree and deal with the stump.** [Stump grinding](/stump-grinding-boise/) takes out the stump, but the root system is still in the ground and can send up shoots. Our guide on [getting rid of a tree stump](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-tree-stump/) walks through the options.
2. **Expect and cut regrowth.** Shoots that show up need to be removed promptly, and repeatedly if they keep coming.
3. **If you use a herbicide on a cut stump,** it has to be a product labeled for that use, and you follow the label exactly. The label is the law.
4. **Don't rototill or trench through the root zone.** Root injury is a classic way to get a new crop of suckers.

For a mature black locust, especially near a house, fence or service line, it's usually safer to have professionals do the cutting. ## Keep it or take it out?

Honestly, for most home lots we'd take it out, especially if it's close to a structure, a play area, a pasture, or a garden bed you care about. If the tree is healthy, set well back, and you're willing to manage suckers, regular pruning can keep it in check. [Tree trimming](/tree-trimming-boise/) to remove deadwood and weak, crossing branches reduces limb failure, and NC State notes the tree can even be held at shrub size by cutting it to the ground every 2 to 3 years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a black locust tree the same as a honey locust?

No. They're related but different species. Black locust has paired short spines at the leaf nodes, white fragrant flower clusters and smooth flat pods. Honey locust has large branched thorns on wild trees, small greenish flowers and long twisted pods. Most honey locusts sold for landscaping are thornless cultivars, while black locust is not a typical nursery shade tree.

### Does black locust have thorns?

Usually, yes. NC State Extension says branches usually bear short paired spines up to about 1.25 inches long beside each leaf scar. Oregon State notes they're more prominent on vigorous shoots and suckers. Wear thick gloves and eye protection when pruning or pulling suckers, because those spines can puncture skin and some gloves.

### Are black locust trees poisonous to dogs, horses and people?

NC State Extension says the bark, leaves and seeds are poisonous and that ingestion can be fatal to humans, livestock and pets. Oregon State says twigs and bark are poisonous to livestock. Keep animals and children away from the tree and its trimmings, and call a vet or poison control if anything was eaten.

### How do I stop black locust root suckers?

Mostly by dealing with the parent tree. Cutting or mowing suckers alone usually brings more, since sprouting commonly follows cutting or root damage. Avoid wounding the roots, remove new suckers promptly, and if the tree has to go, plan on follow-up cutting of regrowth for several seasons after removal.

### Will a black locust grow back after I cut it down?

Very likely. The Forest Service reports that black locust commonly sprouts from stumps and roots, and that cutting is one of the triggers. Grinding the stump helps, but shoots can still rise from remaining roots. Expect to cut or treat regrowth repeatedly, and follow any herbicide label exactly if you choose that route.

### Is black locust good firewood?

It's a dense, durable wood, and NC State notes it's naturally rot resistant. Like any firewood it needs to be dried properly before burning. Keep in mind the bark and leaves are poisonous, so don't pass fresh trimmings to anyone with horses or other livestock.
