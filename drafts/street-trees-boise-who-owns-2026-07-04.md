# Street Trees in Boise: Who Owns the Tree by the Sidewalk?

Here's a question we get constantly: the tree growing in the strip of grass between your sidewalk and the street looks like part of your yard, you mow around it, you rake its leaves, so why can't you just prune it, or remove it, whenever you want?

The answer surprises a lot of homeowners. That strip of land, and the city tree growing in it, is very often not yours to do with as you please, even though it sits closer to your front door than to city hall. If you've got a city tree cracking your sidewalk, dropping limbs on your car, or just blocking the view from your porch, this guide covers who actually owns it, what you're allowed to do, and who to call before you touch it.

## What Is a "Street Tree," Exactly?

A street tree grows in the public right-of-way, which usually includes the parking strip (the grass or planted area between the sidewalk and the curb) and sometimes a few feet on either side of the sidewalk itself, depending on how the street was originally platted. In a lot of Boise's older neighborhoods, like the North End and parts of the East End, this right-of-way can extend surprisingly far onto what looks like a normal front yard.

The right-of-way is public land, managed by the city, even though homeowners are typically responsible for maintaining it (mowing, watering, general upkeep). Ownership and maintenance responsibility are two different things, and that's exactly where the confusion starts.

## So Who Actually Owns the Tree?

In most municipalities, including Boise, trees planted within the public right-of-way are considered city property, managed through Boise's Community Forestry program. That means the city has a say in what species can be planted there, when and how the tree can be pruned, and whether it can be removed, even if you're the one who has watered it for fifteen years.

The specifics of what's allowed, permit requirements, approved species lists, and pruning standards for right-of-way trees change from time to time, so we always tell homeowners to check directly with Boise Community Forestry before planting, pruning, or removing anything in that strip. Don't rely on what your neighbor did five years ago or what a landscaper tells you; call the city and confirm what's currently required for your specific location.

## What You Generally Cannot Do Without Checking First

- **Remove a street tree**, even a dying or unattractive one, without city approval. Right-of-way trees are typically protected, and removal without a permit can result in a citation or a requirement to pay for replacement.
- **Top or heavily prune a street tree** on your own. Improper pruning of a city-owned tree can violate city standards even if you're paying for the work yourself.
- **Plant a new tree in the parking strip** without checking the city's approved species list. Certain fast-growing or invasive species (some of the trees we cover in our [worst trees to plant in Boise](/blog/worst-trees-to-plant-boise/) guide) are commonly restricted from right-of-way planting because of root damage to sidewalks and utilities.

## What You're Typically Responsible For

Even though the tree itself may be city property, homeowners are usually expected to:

- Water the tree, especially during establishment or drought periods
- Mulch and maintain the area around the base
- Keep the tree pruned for basic sidewalk and street clearance (specific height clearances are set by the city; check current standards before doing this yourself)
- Report hazards, like dead limbs or storm damage, to the city rather than handling major work independently

This split, city ownership with homeowner upkeep, is common across the country, and it means the practical reality is often a partnership: you do the routine care, the city handles or approves anything structural.

## Who Pays When a Street Tree Damages Your Sidewalk or Driveway?

This is the other question we hear a lot, especially from homeowners dealing with heaved sidewalk panels from mature street tree roots. The answer depends on your specific situation and current city policy, which is worth confirming directly, but generally:

- Sidewalk repair in the public right-of-way is often a shared or city-managed responsibility, though some cities require adjacent property owners to maintain the sidewalk itself even if the tree causing the damage is city property.
- Root damage to a private driveway is more likely to fall to the homeowner, since the driveway isn't part of the right-of-way.
- Root damage to private sewer or water lines is treated similarly to any other tree root sewer situation; ownership of the tree doesn't automatically make the city liable for private plumbing repairs.

Because these policies can shift and vary by exact location, our honest advice is: document the damage with photos and dates, then contact Boise Community Forestry directly to find out current responsibility before paying for repairs out of pocket.

## Street Trees vs. Trees on Your Actual Property

It's worth being clear about the difference, because the rules genuinely diverge:

A tree entirely within your own lot lines, even one close to a property line or fence, falls under the neighbor and property owner rules we cover in our guide to [neighbor tree trimming rights in Idaho](/blog/neighbor-tree-trimming-rights-idaho/). You generally have much more freedom to prune, treat, or remove those trees on your own timeline.

A tree in the right-of-way strip follows the city forestry rules above, regardless of how long it's been "your" tree in practice.

If you're not sure which category a specific tree falls into, that's a legitimately common point of confusion, especially on older, irregularly platted lots. A property survey or a call to the city's forestry office will settle it. We also run into this a lot when a homeowner's fence, driveway, or landscaping was installed without anyone checking exactly where the right-of-way ends.

## How This Differs from General Tree Removal Permits

A separate but related question we get is about permits for trees on private property, which is a different process from anything involving the right-of-way. If you're removing a large tree entirely within your own lot lines, that typically falls under the general permitting rules we cover in our guide to [tree removal permits in Boise](/blog/tree-removal-permits-boise/), which addresses things like protected tree ordinances, size thresholds, and when a permit is required at all for private property work.

Street trees are a narrower and generally stricter category. Even a small pruning job on a right-of-way tree can require sign-off that the same job wouldn't need on a tree in your backyard, simply because the tree itself belongs to the city rather than to you. Keeping these two processes separate in your head avoids a common mistake: assuming that because your private tree removal didn't require a permit, a similar job on a street tree won't either.

## Why Cities Manage Street Trees Closely

It might seem like overreach for a city to control what happens to a tree sitting on what feels like your property, but there's a practical reason behind it. Right-of-way trees affect sight lines for drivers, clearance for emergency vehicles and utility trucks, sidewalk accessibility for pedestrians and wheelchair users, and the health of a citywide tree canopy that spans thousands of individual parcels. A single homeowner deciding to remove or drastically prune a mature street tree can create a public safety issue or a canopy gap that affects the whole block, not just one yard.

This is also why species selection for new plantings tends to be more restricted in the right-of-way than on private property. A tree with aggressive, shallow roots might be a manageable nuisance in a private backyard, but the same species planted in a narrow parking strip next to a sidewalk and a water line becomes a much more expensive, more disruptive problem for the city to deal with at scale.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I own the tree in the parking strip in front of my house?**
Usually not. Trees in the public right-of-way, including most parking strips, are typically considered city property managed through Boise Community Forestry, even though homeowners generally maintain the surrounding area.

**Can I remove a dead or dying street tree myself?**
Not without checking first. Right-of-way trees generally require city approval for removal, even if the tree is clearly dead. Report it to the city and let them confirm the process.

**Who pays for sidewalk damage caused by street tree roots?**
It depends on current city policy and program availability, which is worth confirming directly with Boise Community Forestry or public works. Don't assume either direction without checking, since this varies by situation.

**Can I plant my own tree in the parking strip?**
Often yes, but usually only from an approved species list and sometimes with a permit, since the city wants to avoid species that damage sidewalks and utilities. Check current guidelines before purchasing a tree for this location.

**What if a street tree is dropping limbs on my car or driveway?**
Report the hazard to the city rather than pruning it yourself. If it's an urgent safety issue, our [emergency tree service](/emergency-tree-service-boise/) team can respond and coordinate documentation, but removal or major pruning of a confirmed right-of-way tree still needs city sign-off.

**How do I find out if a specific tree is a street tree or on my private property?**
A property survey is the most reliable method, especially on older or irregularly platted lots. Boise Community Forestry can also often help clarify right-of-way boundaries for a specific address.

**Are the rules different for trees in Meridian, Eagle, Nampa, or Caldwell?**
Yes, each city manages its own right-of-way and forestry program, so the specific permit process and approved species list will differ from Boise's. Check with your specific city's public works or forestry department.
