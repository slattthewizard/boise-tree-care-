# Climbing Spikes on Trees: When They're Fine and When They're Not

Climbing spikes (also called spurs or gaffs) are steel points strapped to a climber's boots that bite into the trunk, and yes, they hurt trees. Every step is a puncture wound in living bark. That's why the standard advice from tree-care groups and university extension services is to use spikes only on a tree that's coming down, and to climb a tree that's staying with ropes, a saddle, or a lift instead.

If someone is about to climb your tree to prune it and you can see spikes on their boots, that's worth a conversation before they leave the ground. Here's why, what the alternatives look like, and what to ask.

## What climbing spikes do to a tree

A spike climber digs a point into the trunk or limb with each step, and then does it again a few inches higher. A tree's living tissue sits just under the bark, so those points go through the layer the tree uses to move sugars and water.

According to the Tree Care Industry Association (TCIA), in an article republished by [The Charlotte News](https://www.charlottenewsvt.org/2017/02/09/climbing-spikes-harm-pruning-trees/), each puncture kills some tissue. Isolated holes usually seal over, but repeated spiking can kill the bark across a whole area of the trunk. The same article points to sap oozing from the wounds after the work as a sign of spike damage.

Open wounds also give decay fungi and other problems a way in. A tree that has just been pruned already has fresh cuts to deal with, and stacking dozens of spike holes on top of those is a poor trade. Thin-barked trees show the damage most, and the ones you want to stay healthy for decades (a mature maple, an ash, a fruit tree) are exactly the ones where it matters.

## What the standards and extension services say

Oregon State University's Extension Service is blunt about it. In its [guide to selecting, planting and caring for a new tree](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/ec-1438-selecting-planting-caring-new-tree), it says to never allow a climber to use spikes or spurs to climb your tree unless the tree is to be removed.

TCIA's guidance says the same thing and adds the usual exceptions: spikes can be appropriate for a removal, when branches are too far apart to throw a line and there's no other way up, when the tree is too close to power lines, or to reach an injured climber. It also tells homeowners to ask whether a company follows ANSI standards, and to insist on a signed contract that allows spikes only if the tree is being cut down.

You'll also see this rule attributed to ANSI A300 and ISA practice in plenty of arborist write-ups. We're not going to quote a clause number, because we haven't verified the exact wording ourselves and you shouldn't take a number from a blog on faith. What matters for you as the tree owner is that the extension and industry guidance all point the same direction, and it's a reasonable thing to put in writing.

## When spikes are fine

Honestly, there's nothing wrong with spikes on a tree that's being removed. If the trunk is coming down anyway, a few hundred puncture holes don't change the outcome. Climbers use them on removals because they're fast and they give a secure foothold.

The other legitimate cases are rescue (getting to an injured climber quickly) and the narrow situations TCIA lists, like having no safer way to get into the tree. A good climber will tell you when one of those applies. If you hear "I always climb with spikes, it's quicker," that's a preference, not a reason.

## How climbers prune without spikes

Pruning climbers get into a tree the way rock climbers and arborists have for a long time: with a rope. The usual sequence looks like this.

1. The climber throws a weighted line over a strong, high branch and pulls a climbing rope up after it.
2. They set an anchor in the canopy and climb the rope using friction hitches, foot loops, or mechanical ascenders.
3. Once in the tree, they move from limb to limb on the rope, clipped to a saddle (the harness arborists work from), using their hands and feet on branches rather than the trunk.
4. They reposition the rope as they go, sometimes setting a second anchor to reach the far side of a wide crown.

Some jobs don't need a climber at all. A bucket truck or aerial lift puts a worker at the branch with a pole saw or hand saw. ## Can a tree recover from spike wounds?

Often, yes, if there were only a few and the tree is healthy. Trees seal wounds by growing new tissue around them, and a few isolated punctures on a vigorous tree usually close up. The risk goes up when the climber spiked the same stretch of trunk many times, when the tree was already stressed from drought or disease, or when the bark is thin.

Signs to watch for after a pruning job:

- Sap weeping from small holes in a line up the trunk or along limbs
- Patches of dark, sunken, or dead-looking bark where the holes cluster
- Dieback in branches above a heavily spiked area

If you see these, don't panic and don't paint anything on the wounds. Water the tree well through the dry months and consider a [tree health assessment](/blog/tree-health-assessment-boise/) from an ISA-certified arborist if the damage looks widespread.

## What to ask a tree service before anyone climbs

You don't need to be an expert to ask these. A reputable company will answer them without squirming.

**Will your climbers use spikes on this tree?** For a pruning job on a tree that's staying, the answer should be no. For a removal, yes is fine.

**How will you get into the canopy?** Listen for rope and saddle, bucket truck, or lift. If they only say "we'll go up and take care of it," ask again.

**Who's doing the pruning, and is anyone on the crew an ISA-certified arborist?** Oregon State's extension guide recommends asking for a certified arborist, and [Utah State University Extension's pruning overview](https://extension.usu.edu/forestry/publications/utah-forest-facts/004-pruning-landscape-trees-an-overview) says to call an insured, well-trained professional arborist, preferably ISA-certified, whenever climbing is involved. ## Red flags

A few things should slow you down before you sign anything.

- A climber heads up the trunk with spikes on a tree that's supposed to live, and the crew can't explain why.

- The estimate is vague about how the work will be done, and nobody can tell you who's climbing.
- Nobody on the crew is willing to put "no spikes" in writing for a pruning job.
- They suggest topping the tree. Oregon State's guide calls topping both ugly and dangerous, and a crew that recommends it probably isn't thinking much about tree health anyway.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do tree spikes hurt trees?

Yes. Each spike punctures the bark and kills a little living tissue under it. A few isolated holes on a healthy tree usually seal over, but repeated spiking can kill bark across an area of the trunk and give decay a way in. That's why spikes belong on removals, not on trees you want to keep.

### What are tree climbing spurs and gaffs?

They're the same thing. Spurs, spikes, and gaffs all refer to the steel points that strap onto a climber's boots or lower legs and dig into a trunk as they climb. "Gaff" often means the pointed spike itself, while "spurs" or "spikes" can refer to the whole piece of gear.

### Is it ever okay to use climbing spikes on a tree?

On a tree being removed, yes. TCIA also lists a few narrow exceptions: branches too far apart to reach with a throw line and no other way up, a tree too close to power lines, or reaching an injured climber. For routine pruning of a tree that's staying, the guidance is to skip them.

### How do arborists climb trees without spikes?

They use a rope set over a high branch, climbed with friction hitches or an ascender, and a saddle that holds them in place once they're up. Once in the tree, they move between limbs on the rope. For some jobs a bucket truck or lift replaces climbing altogether.

### How can I tell if my tree was spiked?

Look for rows of small puncture holes on the trunk or limbs, often weeping sap, and sometimes patches of dead or sunken bark where the holes cluster. If you see this on a tree that was only supposed to be pruned, ask the company why spikes were used.

### Should I put a no-spikes clause in my contract?

It's a reasonable request. TCIA suggests insisting on a signed contract that permits spikes only if the tree is being cut down. A company that does rope-and-saddle work for pruning shouldn't have a problem agreeing to it.
