# Tree Surgeon vs Arborist: What the Titles Mean in the US

Two crews look at the same 45-foot silver maple in your back yard. One quotes $450, the other $850. The cheaper outfit calls itself a tree surgeon. The other sends someone who introduces himself as an arborist. In the United States only one of those words has an exam behind it, and the difference shows up on your invoice and on your roof.

Here is the plain version. Tree surgeon is a job description, not a qualification. No American body certifies it, no state tests for it, and nothing stops someone with a chainsaw and a magnetic door sign from using the phrase this afternoon. Arborist on its own is barely stricter. The phrase that actually means something is ISA-certified arborist, and you can check it from your phone in under a minute.

That does not make every tree surgeon a hack or every certified arborist worth more money. It means the title is the wrong thing to shop on. What follows is what each label means here, what to check instead, and the small number of jobs where the credential really does change the outcome.

## "Tree surgeon" is a job description, not a credential

The term came over from Britain, where tree surgeon is ordinary language for the person who climbs and cuts. Over there it usually sits next to real tickets from bodies like City and Guilds. Those qualifications do not exist in the United States.

Here the phrase survived as marketing. It sounds medical and it sounds skilled, which is exactly why it works on a truck door. There is no American tree surgeon exam, no registry to search, and nothing to renew.

Idaho does not license tree work either. The state licenses electricians and plumbers. It does not issue a tree surgeon or arborist license, so the words on the truck are self-assigned and mean whatever the person painting them wants.

None of that makes the work bad. Some of the best climbers in the Treasure Valley have twenty years on a rope and no letters after their name. It only means the title tells you nothing by itself, so you have to look at what sits behind it.

## What an ISA-certified arborist has to do to keep the title

The International Society of Arboriculture runs the credential most American tree companies mean when they say certified. To sit the exam a candidate needs documented work experience in arboriculture, or a related degree plus less experience, then passes a written test covering tree biology, soil and roots, diagnosis, pruning standards, cabling, climbing safety and risk assessment.

The certificate is not permanent. It renews on a cycle that requires continuing education credits or retaking the exam, which means a lapsed certificate is a real thing you can catch.

The part that helps you most is that it is public. ISA keeps a searchable directory, so you can type in the name you were given and see whether that certification is current. [Verify a name on the ISA directory](https://www.treesaregood.org/findanarborist/verify) before you sign anything or hand over a deposit.

There are levels above it. Board Certified Master Arborist is the top ISA tier and takes years of experience plus a much longer exam. Tree Risk Assessment Qualification, written TRAQ, is a separate qualification focused only on judging how likely a tree is to fail and what it would land on.

For a normal back yard job you do not need a Master Arborist. You need someone certified, properly insured, and willing to write down exactly what they intend to cut.

| ISA-certified arborist | Passed the ISA exam, holds a current ID, renews with continuing education | The certification number, checked on the ISA directory |
| ISA Board Certified Master Arborist | The top ISA tier. Years of experience plus a longer exam | Same directory, same check |
| TRAQ qualified | Trained specifically in judging tree failure risk | Useful when the argument is whether a tree is dangerous |
| Consulting arborist | Writes reports for permits, neighbor disputes and insurance | A sample report and the credential behind the signature |

One more marker worth knowing: accreditation from the Tree Care Industry Association, which audits a company's safety and business practices rather than one person's knowledge. You can look up accredited firms at [tcia.org](https://www.tcia.org/). Certification is about a person. Accreditation is about a company. Neither replaces an insurance certificate.

## What to check instead of the title on the truck

Ask for a certificate of insurance sent directly by the insurance agency, not a photocopy handed over at the curb. It should show general liability and workers' compensation. Without workers' comp, an injury in your yard can become your problem. For reference, our own cover is $2M liability insurance, insured on every job.

Get the scope in writing, in specific language. "Trim the maple" is not a scope. "Reduce the south-facing crown by up to 20 percent at natural branch unions per ANSI A300, remove deadwood over 2 inches, haul all debris" is a scope, and it is also the thing you can hold someone to afterward.

Ask who is actually doing the work. It is common and legitimate for a certified arborist to price and specify a job that a trained crew then carries out. It is not fine if the certified name exists only on the website.

## When you need an arborist and when a competent crew is enough

Plenty of jobs do not need a credential. A dead 20-foot fruit tree with a clear drop zone, a straightforward stump, deadwood out of a healthy shade tree: any competent, insured crew doing the work to standard will serve you fine. Paying a premium for letters on those jobs buys little.

The credential earns its money when the question is a judgment call rather than a cutting task. Is this tree actually hazardous or does it just look bad. Why is it declining and can it be saved. What will the excavation for a new patio do to the roots. What is the tree worth, and who is liable if it fails onto the neighbor's fence.

It also matters when a document has to satisfy somebody else. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a tree surgeon the same thing as an arborist?

In everyday American usage the two words point at the same job, but only one of them can be verified. Tree surgeon has no certifying body, no exam and no registry in the United States, so it is a description a business chooses for itself. Arborist is loose too unless it is paired with a credential like ISA-certified, which is examined, renewed and publicly searchable.

### Do I need a certified arborist to remove a tree in Boise?

Not for the removal itself. Idaho does not require an arborist license to cut a tree, and a competent insured crew working to standard can handle most straightforward removals. You do want a certified arborist when the decision is contested or documented, such as a permit for a street tree, an insurance claim, a neighbor dispute, or a tree you are not sure needs to come down at all.

### How much more does a certified arborist cost?

Less than most homeowners assume, because the credential does not change the physics of the job. A dedicated assessment visit runs $75 to $250 in this market and is often waived if the work is then scheduled. Where cost really moves is the method chosen, since rigging a tree down in pieces over a garage costs more than dropping one into an open field, whoever is holding the saw.

### How do I check whether someone is actually certified?

Ask for the person's name as it appears on the certification and, if they have it, the certification number. Then search that name yourself on the ISA verification directory rather than trusting a logo on a website, since logos are easy to copy and lapsed certificates are common. If the company will not give you a name to check, treat that as the answer.
